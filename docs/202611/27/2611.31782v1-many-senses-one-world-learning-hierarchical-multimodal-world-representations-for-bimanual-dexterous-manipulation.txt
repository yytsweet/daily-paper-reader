# Abstract 

Bimanual dexterous manipulation relies on multimodal in-puts that capture visual context and non-visual physical in-teraction cues for precise and coordinated control. To ad-dress the challenge of effectively integrating these modali-ties for policy learning, we propose HiMoDex, a two-stage framework centered on hierarchical multimodal world-state representations. In Stage 1, we pretrain a hierarchical rep-resentation network on the collected dataset of 1.19 mil-lion synchronized multimodal time steps. The network in-tegrates visual context with tactile, force-torque, and au-dio cues into unified world-state embeddings. In Stage 2, we freeze the pretrained network and use its embed-dings to condition a shared world-action transformer. The transformer jointly learns action generation and action-conditioned future-state prediction. Experiments across five real-world tasks show that HiMoDex achieves an average success rate of 78.7%, representing an absolute improve-ment of 32.0% over the strongest evaluated baseline. Ex-tensive ablations validate the benefits of hierarchical rep-resentation learning and two-stage training. Failure anal-ysis helps explain performance differences across variants. Code, data, and demos will be released at website. 

# 1. Introduction 

Bimanual dexterous manipulation is essential for robots to perform real-world tasks, enabling coordinated actions, tool use, and fine manipulation [41]. Building on advances in vision-language models and world models, vision-language-action (VLA) models [4, 5, 23] and world-action models (WAM) [28, 51] have made progress in robotic ma-nipulation. Early methods primarily focused on parallel-jaw grippers, while recent methods have extended these models to bimanual systems with multi-fingered hands [34]. Tactile sensing captures hand-object contact, while force sensing measures interaction forces and torques. Audio can reveal impacts, friction, and events such as switch activation [33]. # 2. Related Work 

Multimodal Representation Learning for Robotic Ma-nipulation. Multimodal representation learning integrates heterogeneous observations to support robot decision-making and control. Some studies use language supervi-sion to learn representations that support manipulation poli-cies [14, 21, 36, 47]. Specifically, FuSe [20] and Om-niVTLA [8] align visual and tactile observations with lan-guage descriptions to establish semantic correspondences across modalities. Beyond language supervision, paired multimodal observations provide self-supervised learn-ing signals. Visuo-tactile pretraining learns cross-modal correspondences from spatially paired observations [22]. Masked reconstruction [24] and self-distillation [17] learn robust multimodal representations, while predicting contact events or future observations [16, 30]. HiMoDex introduces a hierarchical representation learning approach that inte-grates visual context and non-visual interaction cues into a unified world-state representation. This representation is shared by action learning and future-state prediction. 

Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation. Compared with bimanual systems using parallel-jaw grippers, those equipped with multifingered hands enable more versatile grasping. Their higher-dimensional action spaces make co-ordinated control more challenging [41]. To address this challenge, existing methods learn visuomotor policies from expert demonstrations [7, 19, 34, 44]. However, visual ob-servations provide limited information about physical inter-actions. Recent studies therefore incorporate tactile sensing to complement visual observations [12, 26, 31, 45]. For ex-ample, HATO [27] incorporates fingertip tactile feedback into diffusion policies to improve robustness in bimanual block stacking. VTAO-BiManip [42] uses visual-tactile-action pretraining for bimanual bottle-cap unscrewing. In addition to tactile feedback, MoDE-VLA [43] incorporates force observations into a VLA model for contact-rich bi-manual dexterous manipulation. In this work, HiMoDex fuses visual, tactile, force, and audio feedback for coordi-nated control across diverse dexterous manipulation tasks. 

World-Action Models for Robotic Manipulation. 

World-action models extend world models by coupling future-state prediction with action generation. Based on their prediction targets, existing methods can be broadly categorized into generative approaches and latent-space ap-proaches. Generative approaches couple future observation prediction with action learning [15, 46, 50]. For example, DreamZero [48] jointly generates future videos and actions using a pretrained video diffusion backbone. Latent-space approaches instead predict or align future representations to avoid costly reconstruction of action-irrelevant observa-tion details [10, 13, 35]. JEPA-WAM [28] couples action learning with prediction of a joint representation of current and future observations. Beyond the prediction target, the deployment efficiency of WAMs also depends on whether future prediction is performed at inference. Fast-WAM [51] demonstrates that training-time future prediction improves policy performance under action-only inference [10, 28]. # 3. Methodology 

3.1. Problem Formulation. 

We learn a bimanual dexterous manipulation policy πϕ(A t |

l, Ot, Pt) from expert demonstrations, conditioned on a natural-language task instruction l, multimodal observation history Ot, and proprioceptive history Pt. Each action chunk At = [ at, . . . , a t+H−1] contains H consecutive ac-tions. Ot comprises multi-view vision, fingertip tactile, tool force-torque, and audio signals. All data are synchronized using timestamps. Visual, tactile, force-torque, and propri-oceptive histories contain T = 16 consecutive steps ending at t on the 30 Hz front-view camera timeline, spanning ap-proximately 0.5 s [37]. Following ManiWAV [33], audio is a continuous two-second segment ending at t.

3.2. Architecture Overview. 

As shown in Fig. 2, HiMoDex follows two-stage training. In Stage 1 (S1), we pretrain a hierarchical multimodal rep-resentation network Fθ to encode temporally aligned ob-servation histories into unified world-state embeddings. We maintain an online network Fθ and a target network F¯θ ,whose parameters are updated via an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online parameters. The online net-work is optimized using three pretraining objectives: cross-representation consistency, signal reconstruction and pre-diction, and task-state contrastive learning. In Stage 2 (S2), we freeze the EMA target network F¯θ from S1 and use it to encode observation histories from downstream tasks into world-state embeddings. A shared world-action trans-former uses these embeddings to jointly learn action gener-ation and action-conditioned future world-state prediction. 3.3. S1: Hierarchical Multimodal Representation. 

In S1, Fθ encodes visual and non-visual observation histo-ries into visual context embeddings ZV and interaction em-beddings ZI , respectively. A world-state encoder then in-tegrates these embeddings into unified world-state embed-dings ZW . This hierarchical representation captures both visual context and physical interactions.

3.4. S2: World-Action Policy Learning. 

In S2, we learn a manipulation policy for downstream tasks. We use the frozen EMA network F¯θ pretrained in S1 to ex-tract world-state embeddings. A shared world-action trans-former is jointly trained for action generation and action-conditioned future world-state prediction. 

Shared World-Action Transformer. During training, it receives five groups of inputs. 1) State embeddings Zs.

We concatenate world-state embeddings from the frozen F¯θ

with proprioceptive embeddings to form Zs. Each proprio-ceptive state in Pt contains 12 arm joint positions, 12 end-effector pose components, and 12 hand joint positions. 2) Action queries Qa. The action head uses these H learnable queries to generate At ∈ RH×36 . Each action matches the dimensionality of a proprioceptive state. 3) Ground-truth action embeddings Za. An MLP encodes the T ground-truth actions aligned with the future prediction window. With temporal position embeddings added, Za conditions future world-state prediction. 4) Future world-state queries Qf .

These T × NW learnable queries are used to predict fu-ture world-state embeddings. Both Za and Qf are used only during training. 5) Task instruction l. A frozen UMT5 [9] encoder maps l to instruction embeddings. Only the action queries Qa cross-attend to these embeddings. # 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setups 

Stage 1: Multimodal Pretraining Data. We collected 1,187,768 time steps of synchronized multimodal observa-tions using our robotic platform. These yielded 291,578 aligned observation windows of T = 16 steps. We used 287,718 windows for pretraining and reserved 3,860 for representation evaluation. More details are in the Appendix. 

Stage 2: Downstream Tasks. We collected 770 expert demonstrations across five bimanual dexterous manipula-tion tasks to train the WAM policy. These tasks assess a range of manipulation capabilities. We varied initial object positions and orientations across demonstrations to increase data diversity. Despite some scenario overlap with S1, S2 demonstrations were collected independently with differ-ent environmental conditions. They were excluded from S1 pretraining. Fig. 3 shows the five task settings. 

Task 1: Pack Conveyor Box. The right hand grasps a toy bear while the left hand opens the lid of a box on a conveyor belt. The right hand then places the toy inside. This task evaluates basic bimanual coordination. 

Task 2: Wipe Whiteboard. The robot grasps an eraser and wipes marks from the whiteboard. This task evaluates the ability to sustain controlled contact. 

Task 3: Insert Plug. The left hand stabilizes a power strip. The right hand grasps a power plug and inserts it into the designated slot. This task evaluates the ability to per-form precise, contact-rich manipulation.

Task 5: Tighten Screw. We introduce this task as the most challenging evaluation setting. It probes the limits of the model’s capabilities. First, the front-view camera is 0.6 m from the object, making the M2 screw difficult to observe clearly, as shown in Fig. 3(e). The left arm must therefore stabilize the workpiece while positioning its wrist-mounted camera for a closer view. Second, grasping is more chal-lenging than in Scrub Pan , as accidental contact can top-ple the unsecured, upright electric screwdriver. Third, the right hand must precisely align the screwdriver with the screw head and tighten the 5 mm-long screw. This near-instantaneous tightening phase is difficult to learn. 

Evaluation Metrics and Baselines. We evaluate poli-cies by task success rate, using 30 trials per task for main comparisons and 10 for ablations. Detailed success crite-ria are provided in the Appindex. We compare HiMoDex with Qwen3Pi and JEPA-WAM [28] as representative VLA and WAM baselines, respectively. Qwen3Pi is implemented using StarVLA [11] with a Qwen3-VL [3] backbone and a flow-matching action head. Its design incorporates choices from π0 [5] and π0.5 [4]. For each framework, we evalu-ate vision-only (V) and full-modality (VTFA) variants. The latter uses visual, tactile, force-torque, and audio inputs. 

4.2. Main Results 

As shown in Fig. 4, HiMoDex (VTFA) achieves an aver-age success rate of 78.7% across five tasks, outperforming all baseline configurations. It achieves absolute gains of 32.7% and 32.0% in success rate over the VTFA variants of Qwen3Pi and JEPA-WAM, respectively. The advantage is pronounced on Insert Plug and Scrub Pan. HiMoDex achieves success rates of 76.7% and 83.3%, whereas the strongest baseline results are only 13.3% and 16.7%, re-spectively. These results highlight its effectiveness in ma-nipulation tasks involving precise alignment, sustained con-tact, and tool use. From V to VTFA, HiMoDex’s average success rate rises from 56.0% to 78.7%. This gain of 22.7% exceeds the corresponding gains of 4.0% for Qwen3Pi and 6.7% for JEPA-WAM. HiMoDex improves substantially on Insert Plug, Scrub Pan, and Tighten Screw 1. These differ-ences suggest that the benefits of additional modalities de-pend on how effectively a model uses them. On Pack Conveyor Box, all variants achieve at least 90.0% success. Additional modalities provide modest gains, suggesting that visual observations support strong performance on this task. On the most challenging task, Tighten Screw 1, HiMoDex (VTFA) achieves 43.3% suc-cess, compared with 36.7% for Qwen3Pi (VTFA). Al-though the performance gap is modest, HiMoDex uses only 327.62M trainable parameters in S2, approximately one-eighth of Qwen3Pi’s 2606.04M.

It processes modality fea-tures and learnable world queries through self-attention to obtain world-state embeddings. H-concat (06) concatenates the visual context and interaction embeddings to form the world representation. Flat reduces average success from 74% to 58% and underperforms the full model on all tasks. These results favor the complete hierarchical design, which combines interme-diate representations with a learned world-state encoder. 

Modality Contributions. Groups I and V-S2 exam-ine how different modality combinations affect policy per-formance. As tactile, force, and audio inputs are pro-gressively introduced, the average success rate steadily in-creases, demonstrating the benefits of multimodal observa-tions. However, additional modalities do not benefit every task. VTF outperforms VTFA on Wipe and Tighten 1 (11 vs. 01). The failure breakdown further suggests that tactile input reduces insertion failures on Insert (09 vs. 10), force reduces residual marks on Wipe (10 vs. 11), and audio re-duces switch-activation failures on Scrub (11 vs. 01). 

Additional Design Choices. Replacing the learnable gates in Algorithm 1 with equal-weight averaging (08) re-duces average success from 74% to 60% (01 vs. 08). This supports the benefit of adaptively weighting modality con-tributions. In addition, removing LW (12) during S2 re-duces average success to 56%. This supports the value of future-prediction supervision for policy learning, consistent with the findings of Fast-WAM [51]. Finally, we evaluate an end-to-end variant (13) that jointly trains the representation network and policy in a single stage. This variant retains all representation and policy objectives but achieves only 52% average success. This supports pretraining world-state representations before optimizing the policy. # 5. Conclusion 

We propose HiMoDex, a multimodal world-action policy for bimanual dexterous manipulation, learned through two-stage training. In the first stage, we learn hierarchical world-state representations that integrate visual context with tac-tile, force-torque, and audio interaction cues. In the second stage, we freeze the pretrained encoders and train a shared world-action transformer for action generation and action-conditioned future-state prediction, retaining only action generation at deployment. Experiments across five real-world task settings show that HiMoDex achieves 78.7% av-erage success, exceeding the strongest baseline by 32.0 per-centage points. Ablations validate the benefits of hierarchi-cal multimodal representations and two-stage training. We also contribute a multimodal dataset with 1.19 million syn-chronized time steps and an expert demonstration dataset for diverse bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks.
