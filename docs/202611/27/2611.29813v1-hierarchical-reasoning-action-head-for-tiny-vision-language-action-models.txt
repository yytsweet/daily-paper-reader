# Abstract 

Real-time onboard robot control calls for a lightweight vision-language-action (VLA) model, yet shrinking the backbone leaves less capacity for deliberation at inference time. Chain-of-thought decoding, dual-system architec-tures, and larger backbones can restore deliberation but reintroduce the latency and memory costs that motivated going small. Recurrent action heads offer a lighter alter-native by iterating in latent space before committing to an action, but existing designs refine a single undifferentiated state that must simultaneously encode slowly evolving task context and rapidly changing motor commands. We pro-pose the Hierarchical Reasoning Action Head (HR-AH), which maintains two latent states updated at different fre-quencies: a low-level state that updates fast, grounded in action tokens and proprioception, and a high-level state that updates slowly, grounded in visual understanding and the instruction-informed low-level state. The two states ex-change information through nested reasoning cycles. Be-cause all iteration occurs in the compact head after a single backbone pass, HR-AH adds hierarchical reasoning with-out increasing the backbone size. With a 22.5M-parameter head, 3–4.3 × smaller than those of RD-VLA and VLA-Adapter on the same backbone, HR-AH reaches 97.0% suc-cess rate on LIBERO and a 4.40 average chain length on CALVIN ABC →D, at the lowest latency among compared methods. We show that this hierarchy improves perfor-mance over a parameter-matched single-state head, and that routing distinct modalities to each level yields further gains in both accuracy and inference cost. 

# 1. Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models attach an action de-coder to a pretrained vision-language backbone, bringing general-purpose robotic manipulation directly from raw im-ages and natural-language commands within reach [4, 19, 20]. However, deploying these models onboard at control

frequency demands a compact backbone. A growing line of tiny VLAs delivers this by shrinking the backbone to a fraction of the original scale [2, 36, 42, 43], but a smaller backbone has less capacity available for deliberation at in-ference time. Chain-of-Thought decoding at 3–8.5B param-eters [16, 17, 51, 54] and dual-system designs that distribute modalities across large VLM subsystems [10, 49] can re-store deliberation, but they reintroduce the very costs that a tiny backbone was chosen to avoid. Recurrent action heads offer a lighter path by iterating in latent space before com-mitting to an action [33, 40]. Latent iteration suits chun-ked continuous control well, since the objective is to refine a motor plan rather than to generate tokens sequentially. However, latent recurrence alone does not determine how computation should be organized. Within an action chunk, task-level context remains relatively steady while concrete motor commands must be jointly refined across timesteps. Refining them in a single recurrent state forces it to repre-sent quantities that evolve at different rates, analogous to running a task planner at servo rate [33, 40]. This raises a central question: can explicitly separating the two improve the effectiveness and efficiency of recurrent computation without increasing the backbone’s computational burden? We introduce the Hierarchical Reasoning Action Head (HR-AH) , which decouples the two processes into levels that correspond to naturally distinct inputs. The motor command draws on instruction-conditioned action queries and proprioception, while the task context draws on vision and the evolving instruction-informed motor plan. This ar-rangement, fast proprioception-driven feedback operating beneath slower vision-driven deliberation, reflects a well-established principle in sensorimotor control [25, 39] and in layered robot control architectures [6]. Unlike dual-system designs that distribute this hierarchy across two large VLM subsystems [10, 49], HR-AH routes modalities to two states inside a single lightweight action head, keeping the entire model compact. To our knowledge, HR-AH is the first hierarchical recur-rent action head for a tiny VLA. The two levels exchange information through nested reasoning cycles with top-down refinement. # 2. Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. VLAs adapt pretrained vision-language models to robot control. They differ chiefly in how actions are decoded, e.g., whether by autoregressive discretization [19], compressed action tokens [27], diffu-sion [11], flow matching [4], or parallel decoding with ℓ1

regression [20]. A complementary line shrinks the back-bone for practical deployment [2, 36, 43]. Most relevant is VLA-Adapter [42], which trains a feed-forward policy over a tiny backbone by injecting per-layer VLM features through Bridge Attention. Its 97M-parameter head is purely feed-forward, with no provision for iterative refinement. We operate in the same tiny-VLA setting but show that a com-pact hierarchical recurrent head can surpass feed-forward decoding in both accuracy and efficiency. 

Iterative computation for action prediction. A grow-ing body of work allocates variable computation per action. Early-exit methods adapt how much of the backbone runs at each step [47], and dual-system designs split slow reasoning and fast control into distinct models or modules [7, 26, 35, 49], with Fast-in-Slow [10] distributing modalities across two subsystems that share a 7B VLM at a 1:4 update ratio. UniFS [38] schedules backbone layer groups at decreas-ing update frequencies, LoopVLA [33] repeatedly applies a shared transformer block to the multimodal tokens, and PredVLA [31] is a sub-million-parameter multi-timescale recurrent policy with no VLM backbone. These approaches organize computation at different frequencies across back-bone components or outside the action head. Such a head uses the same recur-rent representation to integrate visual context and refine mo-tor commands at every iteration, rather than assigning these operations distinct update frequencies. No existing design combines multi-level hierarchy with iterative recurrence in-side the action head. We close this gap with two latent states, updated at different frequencies after a single back-bone pass, whose modality-routed inputs let scene context and motor commands be updated in separate but interact-ing streams. We show this design outperforms a parameter-matched single-state recurrent head with access to the same visual context, and competes with much larger architectures on standard benchmarks. 

Recursive reasoning models. Adaptive and weight-tied recurrent computation was explored early by adaptive com-putation time [15] and Universal Transformers [12], and has resurfaced as recurrent-depth language models [14] and looped transformers for 3D vision [8]. HRM [41] cou-ples a slow high-level and a fast low-level recurrent module on symbolic puzzles. Its high-level module observes only the low-level module’s output, so both levels are ultimately grounded in the same representation despite differing in up-date frequency. Subsequent analyses find that this hierarchy adds little on these tasks [1, 13, 30], and TRM [18] outper-forms it with a single tiny network trained by backprop-agating through the recursion. Embodied control hints at a different outcome, as PredVLA reports that removing the temporal hierarchy from its predictive recurrent architecture lowers success substantially [31]. Our hierarchy routes dis-tinct modalities to each level, so the timescale separation arises naturally from the distinct input modalities. We ab-late against a parameter-matched single-state head that sees the full context from the backbone and a variant that pre-serves both states and their update rates but feeds them iden-tical inputs. Hierarchy accounts for the larger gain, while modality-specific routing contributes a further improvement in both accuracy and inference cost. 

Multi-layer VLM features for control. Which backbone depth best conditions an action head remains unsettled. GR00T N1 conditions on a middle layer [26], SmolVLA discards the upper half of the backbone [36], and FLOWER prunes layers and fuses intermediate tokens [29]. VLA-Adapter matches each policy block to one VLM layer [42] and LIRA widens this to a local window of layers [50], while ABot-M0 reports that, after large-scale robot pretrain-3ing, final-layer features alone suffice [46]. Our head reads both token groups from a deep backbone layer and addi-tionally supplies the planner with gated vision features from the embedding layer. This retains both high-level semantic features and fine-grained spatial detail while extracting all representations once per observation. 

# 3. Methodology 

3.1. Overview 

We consider a manipulation policy πθ that maps visual ob-servation o (third-person and wrist camera views), language instruction ℓ, and proprioceptive states p to an action chunk 

ˆA ∈ RNact ×da , where Nact is the chunk length and da is the action dimension. Unlike standard VLAs that map the fi-nal hidden state directly to actions, or recurrent heads that refine a single undifferentiated latent [40], we propose the 

Hierarchical Reasoning Action Head (HR-AH) as illus-trated in Fig. 2, which decouples the latent state into two interacting components updated at different frequencies: • A low-level action state zL ∈ RNact ×D , where D is the hidden dimension, grounded in action tokens and propri-oception, representing the evolving motor command. • A high-level planning state zH ∈ RNplan ×D , where Nplan 

is the number of planning tokens, grounded in vision to-kens and the instruction-conditioned zL, encoding task-relevant visual context to guide motor-command refine-ment. This routing is deliberate. The action tokens provide a com-pact, instruction-conditioned input that, together with pro-prioception, directly grounds the motor command and feeds the low-level state at every step. Vision tokens are the larger input and change slowly, so routing them (together with zL)to the high-level planner amortizes their cost over multi-ple low-level updates. The refinement step then lets vision guide the executor without requiring it to attend to vision tokens at every step. Both states ( zH and zL) are initialized from learnable pa-rameters ZH and ZL (truncated normal initialization), with sequence order maintained by Rotary Positional Embed-dings (RoPE) [37] within the attention layers. States are reinitialized at every chunk boundary. The overall pipeline extracts features from the backbone VLM once per obser-vation (Sec. 3.2), then iteratively refines (zH , z L) through S

segments of KH hierarchical reasoning cycles each before projecting the refined zL to actions (Eq. (9)):

3.2. Feature Extraction 

We extract features from the VLM backbone to serve as in-put to the HR-AH. Given features F from a chosen back-bone stage (e.g., penultimate layer), we extract vision to-kens hviz and action-query tokens ha:

Language-token positions lie between the two groups and are not extracted directly, as is standard in causal VLAs where action queries already attend to the preceding lan-guage tokens [20, 40, 42]. Proprioceptive input p is pro-jected into dimension D via a linear layer. The planner re-ceives hviz as visual context. 3.3. Hierarchical Reasoning Modules 

Unified Bridge Attention Block. The Unified Bridge At-tention ( UBAttn ) block adapts the Bridge Attention mech-anism [42] to operate over arbitrary context sets. Given a query source X ∈ Rn×D and a context set C =

{C1, . . . , C m}, UBAttn concatenates X and all Ci into one sequence and computes a single softmax so that X at-tends jointly to itself and every context. Unlike Bridge At-tention, UBAttn applies RoPE [37] to queries and keys to encode token order within the concatenated sequence. 

UBAttn also removes the learnable gate on vision atten-tion scores, making it source-agnostic, so C may be any set of sources and each source’s contribution arises from query-key similarity alone. This source-agnostic design is what enables the hierarchical head: a single block defini-tion can serve as the executor, planner, and refinement mod-ule, each receiving different contexts. 3.5. Inference with Adaptive Halting 

Inspired by [40], we adopt a training-free halting criterion to dynamically determine the number of segments at inference time. After each segment s, the model computes the relative change in its action prediction:

Since no prior pre-diction exists at the first segment, the comparison begins at s = 2 . Halting is triggered when δs < τ for P con-secutive segments, subject to a minimum of Smin segments and a hard cap of Smax . 4.1. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate our method across standard simulation bench-marks and a real-world setting to assess both standardized performance and physical generalizability. For simulation, we train and evaluate on all four suites of the LIBERO benchmark [23] (Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long) and the CALVIN ABC →D benchmark [24]. For the real-world ex-periments, we collect a small dataset using a 6-DoF Kinova Gen3 Lite robot arm and evaluate the policy on a set of di-verse manipulation tasks. 

Implementation details. All models are trained on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We use a MiniVLA backbone [2] built on Qwen2.5-0.5B [45] with fused DINOv2/SigLIP vi-sion encoders, initialized from the MiniVLA checkpoint and fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 64). The action dimen-sion da = 7, comprising end-effector translation deltas (∆x, ∆y, ∆z), axis-angle rotation deltas ( ∆rx, ∆ry , ∆rz ), and gripper state. We use a batch size of 24 for LIBERO and 16 for CALVIN, a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, KL = 2 

executor iterations per cycle, and KH = 1 reasoning cy-cle per segment. The head hidden dimension is D = 896 

and the chunk length is Nact = 8 , and the backbone carries 

Nq = 64 action-query tokens. We use Nplan = 32 high-level planning tokens and Nviz = 512 visual tokens. The primary visual features are extracted from the penultimate backbone layer. During training, we unroll all Stotal = 8 

reasoning segments and apply deep supervision to the in-termediate action predictions. At inference, adaptive halt-ing is configured with a minimum of Smin = 2 segments, a maximum of Smax = 8 segments, a prediction-stability threshold of τ = 0 .01 , and a patience of P = 1 .

LIBERO. Table 1 compares our method against state-of-the-art end-to-end (E2E) VLAs, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning models, and iterative refinement baselines on the LIBERO benchmarks. # 5. Conclusion 

We introduced the Hierarchical Reasoning Action Head (HR-AH), a lightweight recurrent module that brings struc-tured deliberation to tiny VLA models. By decoupling the latent state into a slow planner and a fast executor, HR-AH bridges the gap between compact model size and the need for iterative reasoning. Among the methods compared, HR-AH achieves the highest accuracy on LIBERO and matches the best chain length on CALVIN with an action head 3–

4.3× smaller than prior recurrent and feed-forward heads on the same backbone, and the gains transfer to a phys-ical robot. Ablations show that the improvement stems from both the hierarchical state separation and the modality-specific routing, which confines costly vision attention to the slow planner. Together, these results suggest that struc-tured deliberation in a compact head is sufficient for strong 8performance on a tiny backbone, opening a path toward ca-pable VLAs deployable on resource-constrained robots.
