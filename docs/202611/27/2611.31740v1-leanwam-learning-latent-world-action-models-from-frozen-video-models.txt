# Abstract 

World action models (WAMs) typically adapt large pre-trained video generators to jointly predict future observa-tions and robot actions, incurring substantial training and deployment costs. However, it remains unclear which future representations a policy actually needs and how compactly they can be represented. Our probing study shows that com-plementary future representations can be compressed into a few tokens while retaining most of their action-decoding performance. Motivated by this finding, we introduce Lean-WAM, a latent WAM that transfers video priors from a frozen video generator to a lightweight policy via two-stage training. In the first stage, a Future Extractor compresses observed transitions into 32 predictive tokens. These to-kens condition the frozen generator and are jointly shaped by action, RGB, semantic, and geometric supervision, while a compact readout of the generator’s hidden states supplies complementary world tokens. The second stage distills both representations into the policy, which learns to predict them from current observations while generating actions. With 1.4B parameters and no large-scale embodied pretrain-ing, LeanWAM outperforms strong vision-language-action (VLA) and WAM baselines on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0. It predicts an action chunk in 25.1 ms on an A100 GPU, 12.3–41.5 × faster than representative WAMs. These results demonstrate that compact predictive supervi-sion can transfer video priors to robust and efficient control.

Built on pretrained video generators, they bring two benefits to control: joint video-action learning provides dense supervision for antic-ipating scene dynamics [65, 74], and video pretraining sup-plies spatiotemporal priors that robot demonstrations alone may not capture [19]. # 2. Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. Vision-language-action (VLA) models transfer the knowledge of pretrained vision-language models to robot control. RT-2 [5] and Open-VLA [17] represent actions as discrete tokens, while π0 [3] and GR00T N1 [38] pair a vision-language backbone with a continuous action generator. Other work scales train-ing across tasks and data sources [42], robot embodi-ments [57], memory augmentation [10], or aligned het-erogeneous manipulation data [67]. LeanWAM retains a language-conditioned continuous-action policy and uses fu-ture observations as additional training supervision. 

World action models. WAMs couple action learning with the prediction of how the visual world evolves. DreamZero [65] adapts a 14B video generation model to jointly predict videos and actions. Flex-π [59] and DreamWAM [68] extend future prediction to geometry, motion, or semantics. Fast-WAM [69] retains video co-training while skipping future generation at infer-ence. These approaches retain a video backbone at deploy-ment. InternVLA-A1.5 [32] and PDRVLA [51] instead use frozen video models only during training. Recent analy-ses further show that, beyond the predicted future, interme-diate video features also provide useful action representa-tions [12, 15, 23, 55]. LeanWAM learns a compact code and an action-supervised readout of these features, then distills both into a separate policy. 

Latent world action models. WoG [48] and Being-H0.7 [31] use future observations to supervise compact la-tents for action generation. LaWAM [7] predicts visual sub-goals in a frozen feature space. VLA-JEPA [49] and JEPA-WAM [25] learn policies with future-state prediction in pre-trained video representation spaces. ReWAM [16] com-presses DINOv3 features through a bottleneck trained only by action gradients, and GE-Act 2.0 [28] aligns its visual latents with multiple perception teachers. LeanWAM com-plements these approaches by shaping its predictive code through a pretrained video generator, jointly using action, RGB, semantic, and geometric supervision. 

# 3. What Does a Policy Need from the Future? 

LeanWAM rests on the premise that a policy needs only a small, action-relevant part of the future. To test this premise, we train a probe to recover actions from repre-sentations of observed transitions. We evaluate action R2,which measures the relative reduction in squared prediction error compared with predicting the mean action. Higher scores indicate that actions are easier to decode from a rep-resentation under this probe. We ask three questions: (1) Which future representations make actions easy to decode? (2) How many tokens are needed for effective action decod-ing? (3) Do intermediate frames provide useful temporal context? 

Setup. We train an attentive probe [1] to aggregate frozen features into N tokens with learnable queries and decode an 

H-step action chunk with a small transformer. We compare Wan VAE [50] latents (W), DINOv3 [46] patch features (D), and Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B [36] hidden states, before (F) and after (T) video–action training on the probe’s train-ing episodes. Each probe is trained separately with mean squared error on LIBERO [27] demonstrations, using the same episode split and training schedule within each seed. By default, we use N = 32 and the current and final frames of the chunk. For simplicity, we extract video features from a single transformer block. We use block 18 at noise level 

τ = 0 .75 , where τ = 1 denotes pure noise. Full implemen-tation details are provided in Appendix B. 

Feature sources. Figure 3(a) shows that DINOv3 features yield higher action R2 than VAE latents, consistent with broader evidence that semantic latents support action recov-ery [35]. The pretrained video features F have the lowest mean among the tested single sources ( R2 = 0 .30 ), whereas features of the same model after training on robot data (T) reach 0.66 . This gap shows that joint video–action training on robot demonstrations makes the model’s features more accessible for action decoding. The sources also provide complementary cues: W+D reaches 0.55, above either W or D alone, and adding T raises R2 to 0.72, above both W+D and T. Adding F instead gives a lower mean of 0.50, indi-cating that more feature sources do not necessarily improve decoding. Where the video features are read also matters. Block 18 3at τ = 0 .75 has the highest mean R2 among the tested set-tings. Moving to block 12 reduces the score to 0.57, while block 24 remains close at 0.64. At block 18, both a lower noise level ( τ = 0 .25 ) and pure noise ( τ = 1 ) reduce de-coding performance. These results indicate that action de-codability depends on both depth and noise level, consistent with prior analyses of diffusion representations [20, 58]. 

Number of queries. Figure 3(b) varies the number of queries N for W+D+T. Each query is a learnable vector with the probe’s hidden width of 512, and the queries jointly aggregate clip features through cross-attention. Although the probe receives more than a thousand feature tokens, a single summary token already yields R2 = 0 .68 , close to the 0.72 with 32 tokens. As N grows, R2 stays within a narrow range and peaks at N = 8 , with no consistent gain beyond a few queries. These results support learning a com-pact representation of observed transitions that retains most of the action-decoding performance. 

Temporal sampling. Figure 3(c) compares two, three, and five frames for W+D+T over the same action horizon, with N = 32 throughout. All settings include the current frame and the final frame. The three-frame setting adds the midpoint, and the five-frame setting samples at quarter in-tervals. The corresponding mean R2 scores are 0.72, 0.74, and 0.75. Denser observations therefore improve decoding without extending the horizon or increasing the number of queries. The endpoints capture the overall visual change but leave the intervening motion ambiguous, and the gains are consistent with intermediate frames supplying such motion cues. 

Implications. These results suggest that a small set of to-kens can retain complementary action cues from dense fu-ture representations, while intermediate frames provide ad-ditional temporal context. They also expose a challenge: joint video–action training improves video features for ac-tion decoding, but updating a large video model is costly. LeanWAM instead learns a compact predictive code shaped by visual and action supervision through a frozen video gen-erator. We evaluate the resulting representations and closed-loop policies in Sec. 5. 

# 4. Method 

At time t, a policy receives camera observations ot, a pro-prioceptive state st, and a language instruction ℓ, and pre-dicts an action chunk at = ( at, . . . , a t+H−1). Motivated by Sec. 3, training also uses a clip Vt containing the cur-rent frame and several future frames uniformly sampled through t + H. LeanWAM is trained in two stages (Fig-ure 2). Stage 1 encodes the observed transition into a pre-dictive code and world tokens (Sec. 4.1). Stage 2 trains a lean policy to predict the code and world tokens from the current observation (Sec. 4.2), and only this policy is de-ployed. Video and action objectives use flow matching [26, 29]. 4.1. Learning Predictive Codes from a Frozen Video Model 

We use Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B [36] as the frozen video model G. Its frozen Wan VAE encoder compresses time by 4×, producing current and future latents xc and xf .

through two frozen encoders. The VAE latents lie in the latent space of G and preserve fine-grained appearance and motion, while DINOv3 [46] provides semantic patch fea-tures that are more robust to visual distribution shifts. The two are complementary for action decoding (Sec. 3). To-kens of both encoders are mapped to a common width and spatial grid, and fixed spatiotemporal sinusoidal position embeddings are added. An 18-layer Q-Former [21] aggre-gates these tokens through self-attention and cross-attention with Nz learnable queries. A linear layer projects each query to dz dimensions. We set Nz = dz = 32 , encoding the transition over the action horizon in 1,024 scalar values.

4.2. Distilling the Future into a Lean Policy 

Stage 1 encodes future observations available only during training. Stage 2 learns to predict these representations from current observations. 

Architecture. The policy consists of a vision-language backbone, a World Expert, and an Action Expert. The back-bone, Qwen3.5-0.8B [44] truncated to the first 12 of its 24 layers, encodes the multi-view observation and the instruc-tion into context L. The two experts are 12-layer trans-formers coupled as a Mixture-of-Transformers [24], with separate weights and masked joint self-attention at every layer. Only the World Expert can attend to the backbone context L, which is supplied through layer-specific normal-ization and key/value projections. The World Expert holds two groups of tokens. The current tokens C describe the present scene: each camera view is encoded by a frozen DI-NOv3 and tagged with a learnable view embedding, and a visual resampler compresses all views into 64 tokens. The future queries F are Nz + Nw learnable tokens that predict the code and the world tokens. The Action Expert processes the proprioception and noisy action tokens A, conditioned on flow time through adaLN. Detailed module architectures for both training stages are provided in Appendix A. 

Attention mask. The backbone keeps its causal mask and does not attend to the experts. Both C and F attend to 

{L, C, F}, allowing the future queries to predict from the observation and instruction while refining the current to-kens with predictive information. The Action Expert at-tends only to {C, A}, with no direct access to L or F. All Stage-1 modules and the distillation heads are discarded. The deployed policy has 1.4B parameters and renders no future video. 

# 5. Experiments 

We evaluate LeanWAM on LIBERO [27], LIBERO-Plus [13], and RoboTwin 2.0 [9], comparing success rates and latency with VLA and WAM baselines (Sec. 5.2), an-alyzing predictive representations (Sec. 5.3), and ablating model components (Sec. 5.4). 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. We use four 10-task LIBERO suites [27]: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. A single model is trained jointly on these suites and evaluated on 500 episodes per suite. LIBERO-Plus [13] adds seven perturbation types: camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language, lighting, background, sensor noise, and object layout. We evalu-ate the LIBERO-trained checkpoint on all 10,030 episodes without further training. RoboTwin 2.0 [9] contains 50 bimanual tasks with clean and randomized settings, and randomization varies clutter, background, lighting, table height, and instructions. We jointly train on clean and ran-domized demonstrations of 10 selected tasks and evaluate 100 episodes per task per setting.

5.2. Main Results 

LIBERO. LeanWAM reaches 98.4% average success, the highest in Table 1, without large-scale embodied pretrain-ing. It also leads on Long at 98.0%, 5.6 points above 

π0.5, and exceeds Fast-WAM and Motus with roughly one-quarter to one-sixth as many parameters. The fol-lowing benchmarks test robustness beyond these high in-distribution scores. 

LIBERO-Plus. LIBERO-Plus tests generalization to per-turbations unseen in training (Table 2). Without large-scale embodied pretraining, LeanWAM achieves the high-est total success rate, outperforming all other WAMs in the table and π0.5. It is particularly robust to camera and noise perturbations, leading the next best methods by 8.9 and 3.3 points. Several WAMs built on large video mod-els are fragile under such perturbations, with Fast-WAM reaching only 16.4% under camera changes. We hypoth-esize that LeanWAM is robust because its predictive code emphasizes manipulation-relevant regions and behaviors (Sec. 5.3) rather than scene appearance. 5.4. Ablation Studies 

Table 5 ablates the components of LeanWAM on all 10,030 LIBERO-Plus episodes. Each variant modifies a component or training pathway and otherwise follows Sec. 5.1, with details in Appendix C.5. 

Stage 1. Removing action supervision or the video model reduces success by 4.5 and 3.6 points. These are coupled ablations: both also remove world tokens and retain only code distillation. They therefore test the corresponding training pathways, rather than isolating either component alone. Removing the semantic and geometric alignment costs 2.4 points. Replacing alignment with output heads that predict future DINOv3 features and depth, following WAMs that add semantic or geometric targets to the pre-dicted future [51, 59], is even worse (78.0%). This supports 8our design: alignment only steers the internal states of the frozen model, whereas the heads must produce dense fea-ture and depth maps from its last layer, which is specialized for RGB. Fine-tuning the video model, as most WAMs do, lowers the success rate to 79.6%, although training makes video features more decodable in distribution (Sec. 3). A trainable model can rely less on the code and may lose its general prior when fitted to limited robot data. Keeping it frozen is thus both cheaper and more robust. 

Stage 2. With the same policy architecture and inference cost, action-only training reaches 75.8%, 8.8 points below LeanWAM. Distilling world tokens alone or the code alone recovers part of this gap (79.3% and 80.4%), and jointly distilling them performs best, consistent with their com-plementarity in Sec. 5.3. Replacing current DINOv3 to-kens with learnable tokens costs 5.2 points, supporting di-rect visual input to the World Expert alongside the vision-language backbone. 

# 6. Conclusion 

LeanWAM transfers video priors to control by learning compact predictive representations through a frozen video model. A lean policy predicts these representations from current observations, achieving strong task performance, robustness, and low inference latency without deploying the video model. 

Future work. Our evaluation is limited to simulation. Fu-ture work includes testing LeanWAM in complex, dynamic real-world environments, scaling the Future Extractor with larger robot datasets and human videos, and integrating its fast control with a high-level agent for long-horizon plan-ning and task decomposition.

# A. Model Architecture 

This section details the modules of both training stages (Sec. 4). Table 6 summarizes the transformer modules that we introduce, and Table 7 gives the attention mask of the policy. Sec. C.2 lists which modules are trained in each stage. Blocks are indexed from 1 in all networks. 

A.1. Stage 1 

Future Extractor. The Future Extractor encodes the current frame and four future frames of the main camera (Table 9) with two frozen encoders. From the Wan2.1 VAE encoder [50], we take the 384-channel spatiotemporal features after its middle block, before the output head that maps them to latents. From DINOv3 ViT-L/16 [46], we take the patch tokens of the penultimate block for each frame and normalize them with a LayerNorm without affine parameters. Both feature maps are pooled to a common spatial grid, and a linear layer maps the VAE features to the 1,024 channels of DINOv3. The tokens of the two encoders are then concatenated into one memory. An 18-layer Q-Former [21] with 32 learnable queries reads this memory. Each layer applies pre-LN self-attention among the queries, cross-attention to the memory, and an FFN, each with a residual connection. Fixed 3D sinusoidal position embeddings over time, height, and width are added to the keys of the cross-attention but not to its values, which therefore carry only visual content. A final LayerNorm and a linear layer from 1,024 to 32 channels produce the code z ∈ R32 ×32 .

Conditioning and world tokens. A linear projection Pg maps the code from 32 to 1,024 channels, and the result replaces the text embeddings in every cross-attention block of the frozen Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B [36]. The video model receives the clean latent of the current frame and the noised latent of the four future frames, which the VAE compresses into one latent frame. From block 18, we take the 2,048-channel hidden states at the future positions and project them linearly to 1,024 channels. The attentive pooler Rη then aggregates them into the world tokens w ∈ R32 ×1024 . It is a 3-layer Q-Former with 32 learnable queries, whose layers apply pre-LN self-attention, cross-attention with separate LayerNorms for the queries and the memory, and an FFN, followed by a final LayerNorm. 

Action DiT. The action DiT is conditioned on the 64 tokens [Pa(z); w], where Pa is a linear projection from 32 to 1,024 channels. Its input sequence contains a state token and the noisy action tokens. Its 16 blocks alternate between cross-attention to the conditioning tokens and self-attention over the input sequence, and all cross-attention blocks read the same conditioning tokens. The flow time modulates attention and FFN through AdaLN-Zero [40]. 

Alignment projectors. Two projectors map hidden states of the video model to 1,024-channel teacher features. The seman-tic projector reads block 9 and targets the penultimate-block patch tokens of DINOv3, normalized as in the Future Extractor but not pooled. The geometric projector reads block 14 and targets the patch tokens of the 14th global-attention block of VGGT-Ω [52], without additional normalization. Each projector consists of a spatial convolution and a two-layer MLP and keeps the spatial grid of the video model. The teacher features of the current frame are kept, and those of the four future frames are linearly interpolated in time to the future latent, so both the current and the future positions are aligned. 

A.2. Stage 2 

Vision-language backbone. The backbone keeps the full vision encoder of Qwen3.5-0.8B [44] and its first 12 language layers, which form three groups of three Gated DeltaNet layers [61] followed by one gated attention layer. It reads the images of all cameras and the instruction, and the last-layer hidden states of all non-padding tokens form the context L with 1,024 channels. The backbone runs its original forward pass, so L is not updated by the experts. 

Visual resampler. The visual resampler produces the current tokens C from a frozen DINOv3 ViT-L/16, which encodes each camera view separately. From blocks 13, 17, and 21, we take the class token, the register tokens, and the patch tokens. The features of each block pass through a separate learnable LayerNorm, and a linear layer maps their concatenation from 3,072 to 1,024 channels. The main camera keeps its full patch grid. For the other cameras, every 2 × 2 group of neighboring patches is folded into the channels and mapped from 4,096 to 1,024 channels by a second linear layer, which reduces the patch tokens by 4× while keeping the class and register tokens. Both linear layers are shared across views. After adding a learnable embedding for each view, we concatenate the tokens of all views into one memory. Under this mask, future information reaches the actions only through C, which reads F at every layer. Since neither C

nor F attends to A, the World Expert does not depend on the action noise or the integration step. Computing its keys and values once per query and reusing them across all Euler steps is therefore equivalent to recomputing them at every step. 

Prediction heads. At the last layer of the World Expert, the first 32 future queries predict the code through a LayerNorm and a linear layer from 1,024 to 32 channels, and the other 32 predict the world tokens through a separate LayerNorm and a linear layer from 1,024 to 1,024 channels. The token-wise LayerNorm without affine parameters is applied only to the distillation targets, and the video model is always conditioned on the unnormalized code. The heads are used only for training, so the deployed policy consists of the other modules in this subsection (Sec. D). 

Initialization. Both experts are randomly initialized, and the Action Expert does not inherit the weights of the Stage-1 action DiT. In the World Expert, the output projections of attention and FFN are scaled down with layer depth. The AdaLN-Zero modulation of the Action Expert is initialized to zero, so each of its blocks starts as the identity function [40].

# C. Experimental Details 

This section details the protocols and training settings summarized in Sec. 5.1, and reports per-task results on RoboTwin 2.0. Table 9 summarizes the setup on each benchmark. 

C.1. Benchmarks and Protocols 

LIBERO. LIBERO [27] has four suites of 10 language-conditioned tasks, performed by a simulated Franka Panda arm. LIBERO-Spatial places the same objects in different layouts, LIBERO-Object varies the objects to be manipulated, LIBERO-Goal varies the goal in a fixed scene, and LIBERO-Long, also known as LIBERO-10, contains long-horizon tasks that chain several subtasks. Each task has 50 human demonstrations. We use the version released by OpenVLA [17], which replays the demonstrations to render 256 × 256 images, removes no-op actions, and discards demonstrations that fail on replay. This leaves 1,693 demonstrations with 273,465 frames, on which we train a single model for all four suites. The policy receives the instruction, the third-person and wrist images resized to 224 × 224 , and an 8-dimensional propri-oceptive state: the end-effector position, its orientation in axis-angle form, and the positions of the two gripper fingers. It predicts chunks of 16 actions, each a 7-dimensional command of end-effector translation and rotation deltas and a gripper action, and executes the first 10 before predicting again. Stage 1 uses only the third-person camera, with clips of the current frame and four future frames over the next 32 steps. This horizon is set independently of the Stage-2 chunk length, so the distilled targets describe a longer future than each predicted chunk. Following the standard protocol, we evaluate each task over 50 episodes from the initial states provided by the benchmark, giving 500 episodes per suite and 2,000 in total. Each episode begins with 10 idle steps that let the objects settle, after which the policy acts for at most 220, 280, 300, and 520 steps on Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, respectively. An episode is successful if the task is completed within this limit.

C.2. Training and Inference Details 

Both stages of LeanWAM are trained on a single node with 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80 GB), using DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 [45] and BF16 mixed precision, with the action, video, alignment, and distillation losses computed in FP32. We use AdamW [30] with β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .99 , and ϵ = 10 −8, a linear warmup followed by cosine decay to a minimum learning rate, and gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0. C.3. Baselines 

In the tables of the main paper, Size counts the parameters of the deployed model. PT marks methods whose policy is pre-trained to predict actions or latent actions on large-scale robot or human manipulation data before training on the benchmark. LeanWAM builds on pretrained vision, language, and video models, none of which is pretrained on embodied action data. On LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, task-specific training uses only LIBERO demonstrations. Methods marked PT may additionally use large-scale embodied pretraining. On RoboTwin 2.0, the baselines are trained on all 50 tasks in their papers, and we average their reported per-task success rates over our 10 tasks. LeanWAM is trained on these 10 tasks only. The two protocols thus differ: the baselines learn from more tasks and demonstrations, while LeanWAM is trained on fewer tasks.

C.5. Ablation Details 

The ablations in Sec. 5.4 follow the LIBERO-Plus protocol in Sec. C.1. Each variant is trained on LIBERO with the same data, schedule, and hyperparameters as LeanWAM and is evaluated on all 10,030 episodes of LIBERO-Plus. A Stage-1 variant changes the Stage-1 model, and Stage 2 is retrained with it as the teacher. A Stage-2 variant reuses the Stage-1 model of LeanWAM. Each variant is trained once. Under an independent Bernoulli approximation, the evaluation standard error is about 0.4 percentage points. 

Stage-1 variants. 

• − action supervision removes the action DiT and its loss, so the Future Extractor learns only from the video and alignment losses through the frozen video model. Without the action DiT, there is no attentive pooler and hence no world tokens, and Stage 2 is trained with the action loss and the code distillation loss. • − video model removes the video model, together with the alignment losses and the world tokens. The Future Extractor learns only from the action loss, through an action DiT conditioned on the projected code alone. Stage 2 again distills only the code. 19 • − semantic and geometric alignment removes Lsem and Lgeo and keeps the action and video losses. Stage 2 distills both targets, as in LeanWAM. • Frozen → trainable video model unfreezes Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B and updates it with the action, video, and alignment losses at a constant learning rate of 10 −5. All other modules and settings are unchanged. • Alignment → prediction heads replaces the alignment losses with two output heads that predict future DINOv3 features and depth, as described next. 

Prediction heads. This variant follows WAMs that add semantic or geometric targets to the predicted future [59, 68]. The video model predicts the velocity of the future RGB latent from the hidden states of its last block, through adaptive layer normalization, a linear layer, and unpatchifying. We add two heads of the same form next to it, each with its own adaptive layer normalization, a linear layer from width 2048 to the target dimension, and unpatchifying. Both heads are randomly initialized and read the same hidden states as the RGB head. The input of the video model is unchanged: the clean current latent and the noisy future latent. The semantic head predicts DINOv3 features [46]. Since the Wan VAE [50] compresses the four future frames into one latent, we take the target from the last of them: the patch features of the frozen DINOv3 ViT-L/16 after its second-to-last block, followed by a layer normalization without affine parameters. These features have 1,024 channels and the same spatial grid as the tokens of the video model, so the head maps 2048 channels to 1,024 without any resampling. The head regresses the features directly, without noise, under a mean squared error. The geometric head predicts depth. We compute pseudo-depth maps of the future frames with VGGT-Ω [52], clip them to a fixed range estimated on the training set, and apply a logarithmic normalization, using the same range for all videos of a camera. The normalized maps are replicated to three channels and encoded by the frozen Wan VAE encoder, which compresses the four future depth maps into one latent zd. Like the RGB head, the depth head is trained with flow matching and predicts the velocity ϵ − zd. Both head losses have a weight of 1.0. The rest of Stage 1 is unchanged. The attentive pooler reads the world tokens from the same block, the action DiT is conditioned on the code and the world tokens, and both serve as targets in Stage 2. 

Stage-2 variants. 

• − code distillation , − world distillation , and − code and world distillation remove the corresponding distillation losses from the Stage-2 objective. The architecture, including the future queries, is unchanged, so these variants have the same inference cost as LeanWAM. • DINOv3 tokens → learnable tokens replaces the 64 current tokens, produced by DINOv3 and the visual resampler, with 64 learnable tokens. DINOv3 and the resampler are removed from both training and inference, so the policy observes the images only through the vision-language backbone. 

# D. Inference Efficiency and Training Cost 

Latency measurement. We time the complete prediction of one action chunk on a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU with batch size 1 and BF16 mixed precision, using the same GPU for all methods. Each method answers 1,000 queries on the ten LIBERO-Long tasks, 100 per task. We synchronize the GPU immediately before and after the neural computation of each query and measure the wall-clock time in between, which includes every network component that the query requires. We exclude input preprocessing, host-to-device transfer, simulation and rendering, communication, output post-processing, and robot execution. Warmup, compilation, and graph capture are excluded as well: each method runs 30 warmup queries before timing and three untimed queries whenever the task changes. We report the mean and standard deviation over all timed queries without discarding slow samples. Each baseline is timed with its official implementation, keeping its native input resolution and prediction horizon. For π0.5, this is the JAX implementation in openpi 1.

For each query, the backbone and the World Expert run once and the Action Expert runs ten Euler steps. The deployed policy contains no video model and no other Stage-1 module. PyTorch implementation. The PyTorch implementation uses four optimizations that do not change what the model com-putes: 1. Parallel visual encoding. The backbone runs on one CUDA stream while DINOv3 and the visual resampler run on another, and the two branches join before the experts. 2. Compact action attention. Since the action tokens attend only to the current tokens C and to themselves (Sec. 4.2), we pass only the keys and values of [C, A] to their attention instead of masking all other tokens. The World Expert is computed in full. 3. Compiled denoising. A static-shape denoising step of the Action Expert is compiled with TorchInductor. Masks, position embeddings, and the state conditioning are built once per query, and the keys and values of C are computed once and reused across the ten steps. 4. CUDA graphs. DINOv3 with the visual resampler, and the World and Action Experts, are captured as two CUDA graphs and replayed for every query, while the backbone runs eagerly. Each query uses its own observation, state, and action noise, and nothing is cached across queries. 

JAX implementation. We obtain a JAX [4] implementation with torchax [43], a PyTorch frontend for JAX, which runs our PyTorch modules as JAX programs that XLA compiles. This implementation therefore reuses the network definitions of the PyTorch one. Each query calls four functions compiled with jax.jit : the vision encoder of Qwen3.5, DINOv3 with the visual resampler, the language model of Qwen3.5, and the World and Action Experts. The last function runs all ten Euler steps, with the same compact action attention and the same reuse of the keys and values of C as the PyTorch implementation. We use neither separate CUDA streams nor explicit CUDA graphs, and we precompute only the image-grid indices and the rotary-embedding constants, which are identical for all queries. Nine of the 12 language layers that we keep from Qwen3.5 use Gated DeltaNet [61], a linear attention mechanism based on the gated delta rule. We implement its chunkwise form natively in JAX, with chunks of 64 tokens, a triangular solve within each chunk, and a lax.scan over chunks that keeps the recurrent state in FP32. We align the autocast rules of torchax with those of PyTorch, so the two implementations use the same mixed precision. Softmax attention uses the fused cuDNN kernel when its inputs are in BF16 and its head dimension is at most 128, and the XLA implementation otherwise. We time the JAX implementation on 1,000 queries, 100 per task, synchronizing the GPU with jax.block_until_ready .
