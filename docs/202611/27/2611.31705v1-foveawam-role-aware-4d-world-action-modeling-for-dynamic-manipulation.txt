## Abstract 

To manipulate a moving object, a policy must predict the scene at the moment its action executes, yet every millisecond spent predicting pushes that moment further away. Scene-level forecasts are computationally expensive, whereas target-centric forecasts can omit nearby objects that affect contact. Our key insight is that what a policy must pre-dict decouples by role: only the target’s pose, which de-termines contact, requires millimeter precision, while the surroundings matter primarily through the clearance they leave and the motion they impart. We present FoveaWAM, a native 4D world–action model that allocates precision according to these roles. Specifically, it models the target’s past and future motion as a metric SE(3) trajectory, the surroundings as sparse 3D flow, and relies on the current frame for everything static. This compact representation lets FoveaWAM act in 15.1 ms on one RTX 5090, about 

10 × faster than video world–action baselines, leaving sub-stantially less time for the scene to evolve before action execution. To evaluate each policy under its own latency, we introduce OnTimeBench, a dynamic manipulation bench-mark in which the scene keeps moving while the policy com-putes. FoveaWAM reaches 82.1% success on OnTimeBench, 20.8 points above the strongest baseline, and also leads on DOMINO and real-robot tasks. Project page: https://foveawam.github.io/ .

## 1. Introduction 

Robot manipulation policies have made substantial progress in static scenes [4, 10]. In these scenes, the world stays still during inference, so the observed scene remains valid when the action executes. In dynamic manipulation, however, the target keeps moving during inference, so the observed pose is already outdated when the action executes. For example, a block sliding down a ramp at about 0.9 m/s travels 18 cm during a 200 ms inference interval, far exceeding the posi-tional tolerance of accurate contact. time. Predicting the target’s motion requires accounting for surrounding objects, but the target continues to move while the prediction is being computed. The longer prediction takes, the further ahead the policy must predict. This creates a tension between accurate prediction and timely action. Existing methods vary in what they predict (Fig. 2). Scene-level predictors model future images or video [29, 45, 53, 61] or 3D motion [58]. These predictions capture surrounding motion, but a dense forecast can spend compu-tation on parts of the scene with little bearing on contact. In a video forecast, the target may occupy only a small re-gion, and its pose remains implicit in the predicted pixels. AHEAD limits its rollout to task-relevant latent patches [41]. PUMA supervises future features of the target [14], and SPOT predicts target pose trajectories [17]. Focusing on the target can omit surrounding objects that block access or alter its motion. For example, a moving obstacle can close the ap-proach path even if the target follows the predicted trajectory. Determining whether that path stays clear and estimating the target’s pose at contact impose different demands on a forecast. What should a policy predict about a moving scene, and how precisely, to make accurate contact in time? 

Our key insight is that prediction requirements differ by role (Fig. 1). The target’s position and orientation at execu-tion time directly determine contact success and therefore re-quire precise prediction. In contrast, the surroundings affect the contact only indirectly, by obstructing access or altering the target’s motion, so their motion requires only coarse pre-diction. Finally, the rest of the scene is static, so it is already available in the current frame and requires no prediction. A policy can therefore make accurate contact in time by con-centrating precise prediction on the target and capturing the surroundings at low cost. This allocation resembles biologi-cal vision, where the fovea resolves fine detail and peripheral vision preserves broad motion awareness [12, 34, 39]. Based on this insight, we propose FoveaWAM , a na-tive 4D world–action model that allocates precision by role (Fig. 2c). FoveaWAM observes and predicts scene motion directly in metric 3D space over time, rather than in pixels. In this space, each part of the scene can be represented sep-arately, so each receives the precision that its role requires. The target is predicted precisely, as a metric SE(3) trajec-tory that gives its future position and orientation explicitly with only a few parameters. In contrast, the surroundings are predicted coarsely, as sparse 3D flow, and the static scene is not predicted. The same allocation applies to the input. A single frame already shows the static scene and the layout of the surroundings, so only the target’s motion requires a history. FoveaWAM therefore reads one RGB-D frame and a short SE(3) pose history of the target, instead of the image or optical-flow histories used by existing dynamic policies [14, 29, 50]. Since both the input and the predictions are compact, FoveaWAM acts in 15.1 ms on one RTX 5090, about 10 × faster than video world–action models, and the scene has little time to move before the action executes. The tension between accurate prediction and timely ac-tion can be measured only when the evaluation accounts for inference time. However, the default evaluators of DOMINO [14] and PhysMani-Bench [58] pause the simulation during inference, so a slow policy is scored as if it were instant. We therefore introduce OnTimeBench , a benchmark of 14 dynamic manipulation tasks in which the world never pauses and every policy is evaluated at its native latency. On the same scenes, pausing the world overestimates slow policies and changes the ranking of methods (Fig. 6c). Experiments show that FoveaWAM makes accurate con-tact in time. On OnTimeBench, it reaches 82.1% success at native latency, 20.8 points above the strongest baseline. It also leads by 19.9 points on the single-arm tasks of DOMINO [14], whose evaluator pauses during inference, so its advantage does not come from speed alone. On six real-robot tasks, it achieves the highest success on every task and 48.3% on average, 17.5 points above the strongest base-line. Finally, ablations show that each part of the role-aware design contributes to success, with the largest drop from removing the target’s pose history. Our key contributions are as follows: • We identify distinct prediction requirements in dy-namic manipulation and propose a role-aware prediction paradigm. It forecasts target motion precisely and sur-rounding motion coarsely, using the current frame for static geometry. This allocation preserves the precision needed for contact while reducing prediction cost. • We present FoveaWAM , a native 4D world–action model 2that instantiates this paradigm and achieves precise, low-latency action generation. It acts in 15.1 ms, about 10 ×

faster than video world–action models, and outperforms the strongest baseline by 20.8 points on OnTimeBench. It also leads on DOMINO’s single-arm tasks with paused physics and across six dynamic real-robot tasks. • We build OnTimeBench , a benchmark of 14 dynamic manipulation tasks in which the world never pauses and every policy is evaluated at its native latency, exposing the cost of latency that paused evaluation hides. 

## 2. Related Work 

Dynamic manipulation. Earlier systems used explicit tracking and interception planning for moving objects [1, 2, 32]. Recent methods learn to handle motion from data [8, 24, 50]. They use optical-flow histories [14, 29] or condition actions on forecasts at inference [30, 41, 58]. FoveaWAM encodes past motion with a metric SE(3) pose history and a single current frame. Asynchronous execu-tion and chunk-transition methods address action timing and continuity [5, 27, 36, 42, 50]. The default DOMINO [14] and PhysMani-Bench [58] rollouts step physics after actions return. OnTimeBench advances physics during inference to evaluate each policy at its native latency. 

Forecasting in world–action models. World–action mod-els (WAMs) couple video and action prediction [23, 45, 53, 65], building on video prediction as a policy objective [43, 49]. Recent models predict features or latent tokens [40, 60, 64], masks [54], flow [56], or object slots [28] alongside or in place of RGB. PUMA, Fast-WAM, and SG-WAM use future supervision during training and can omit the forecast at deployment [14, 57, 62]. During training, FoveaWAM supervises motion in a role-aware metric 4D state that explicitly represents the target’s future pose. 

Object-centric and geometric motion representations. 

Manipulation policies use metric 3D inputs [35, 59]. Re-lated representations include point tracks [3, 48, 51], 3D flow [15, 18, 55, 58], object keypoints or trajectories [17, 31, 38], and rigid-body decompositions [6, 7]. DOCIR separates robot, target, and obstacles in visual inputs [13]. Gaze-based robot perception concentrates sensing through foveated in-puts [11, 22] or active camera control [20]. FoveaWAM allo-cates prediction precision by role, using coarse sparse flow for the scene and a metric SE(3) trajectory for the target. 

## 3. FoveaWAM 

FoveaWAM applies the role-aware allocation to both what it reads and what it predicts (Fig. 3a). It reads the current RGB-D frame for the static scene and the context, and an SE(3) pose history for the target. It predicts coarse sparse 3D flow for the scene and a precise SE(3) trajectory for the target. In between, a world stream (scene tokens S and summary tokens M ) and an action stream (noisy action tokens A)share one masked attention in each of the L layers. 

3.1. Problem Formulation 

We study manipulation in scenes that keep moving while the policy computes. 3.2. Role-Aware Dynamics Encoding 

A single frame shows where things are but not how they move. The context does not need a motion history of its own. The clearance it leaves is visible in the current geometry, and the motion it imparts appears in how the target has moved. FoveaWAM therefore pairs one RGB-D frame with a short metric pose history of the target. 

Scene tokens from the current frame. Contact is decided in metric space, so FoveaWAM anchors each patch token at a metric 3D point. A frozen MAE ViT-B/16 [16] encoder turns the RGB image into 196 patch tokens on a 14 × 14 

grid. The depth map back-projects the center of patch i to a point xi ∈ R3 in the base frame. A linear embedding of xi

is added to the patch token, and xi also sets the 3D rotary po-sition embeddings of its queries and keys. Attention between patches then depends on their relative metric positions, and observations, predictions, and actions share one frame. 

Target tokens from the pose history. The pose history covers the last 350 ms in 8 samples at 20 Hz. The SE(3) encoder turns it into 8 tokens, one for the current target pose and seven for the motions between consecutive samples. Each motion token holds the translation, relative rotation, time interval, and finite-difference velocity and acceleration in physical units, so the quantities that decide where the target will be enter the model directly. 3.3. Role-Aware 4D Future Prediction 

Each command acts at a future time, so the world stream must also represent the scene at the execution times. The target’s pose decides contact, so the SE(3) decoder predicts its full trajectory. The context matters through clearance and imparted motion, which a coarse flow already captures. Both predictions are base-frame changes from the current state at τt + δh, with δh = h·50 ms for h = 1 , . . . , 8, and together with the current state they cover the execution times of the whole chunk. Both decoders are linear and run only during training. A linear readout forces the world-stream states themselves to encode the predicted motion. 

Sparse 3D scene flow. The flow covers every surface that the patches see, including the robot, the target, and the con-text. 3.4. World–Action Learning and Inference 

Action stream. The action stream holds H = 8 tokens. Each token embeds one noisy command, which consists of an end-effector translation increment, a rotation increment, and a gripper command. World tokens use layer normal-ization, and action tokens use adaptive layer normalization conditioned on the flow time u. The attention mask lets S

attend to S, M to S and M , and A to all tokens (Fig. 3b). In every layer, the action stream reads the patch states shaped by the scene flow and the summary states shaped by the target trajectory. The world stream never reads the actions, so its states do not depend on the noise or flow time.

Inference. At deployment, FoveaWAM skips the flow and SE(3) decoders, as in the action-only inference of prior video WAMs [23, 57] (Fig. 3c). Since the world states do not depend on the noise, the world stream runs once per query and caches its keys and values. The action stream then integrates 5 Euler steps on this cache. One query takes 15.1 ms at p95 on one RTX 5090, within one control period. Commands are indexed by time from capture, so the policy needs no latency input (Sec. 4.2). 

## 4. OnTimeBench 

The default evaluators of DOMINO [14] and PhysMani-Bench [58] advance the simulation only after the policy returns its actions, so inference takes no time in the scene. A policy that computes for 200 ms thus still finds the ball where it observed it, although a real ball would have rolled 18 cm (Fig. 4a). OnTimeBench contains 14 tasks in five families (Fig. 4b, c). Grasp tasks pick up a moving target, and Place tasks put a held object into a moving receptacle. Pass tasks guide a wand along a rotating wire without contact, Capture tasks catch a rolling or sliding object, and the Impact task grasps a part after a striker hits it. Target speeds range from 0.03 to 1.14 m/s. The tasks also differ in what decides success. In the seven rotation-sensitive tasks, the target rotates and its orientation matters at contact. In the nine context-active tasks, an obstacle limits the clearance or a driver such as a conveyor, ramp, or striker moves the target. 

4.1. Construction 

OnTimeBench is built in MuJoCo and robosuite [44, 66] with a Franka Panda arm controlled at 20 Hz. Each scene samples the target’s initial state and motion parameters within task-specific ranges and is kept only if it is physically feasible. A task-specific expert with privileged simulator state provides 800 demonstrations per task. A demonstration is kept only if the expert succeeds, a replay of its actions reproduces the episode, and its path and jerk pass quality checks. We select configurations on 1,400 development scenes and report all OnTimeBench results on 1,400 held-out test scenes, 100 per task, shared by all methods. 

4.2. Evaluation Protocol 

In Native , physics advances during each query by the method’s latency, drawn per query from its distribution mea-sured at batch size 1 on one RTX 5090. All methods share one executor, which hands over each chunk at the next con-trol boundary (Fig. 5). +150 ms adds 150 ms to the native latency of every method, and Paused pauses the world during inference. We report the task-balanced success rate (SR) and the p95 time-to-action, the time from capturing an observa-tion until its first command is available. Task definitions and the full protocol are in the supplementary material.

## 5. Experiments 

We evaluate FoveaWAM on OnTimeBench, on DOMINO [14], and on a real robot. On OnTimeBench, the paused, native, and delayed settings separate how well a policy rep-resents the target’s motion from how quickly it acts. 

5.1. Implementation Details 

Baselines. We compare with the reactive policies ACT [63] and Diffusion Policy (DP) [10], the vision–language– action model (VLA) π0.5 [4], the dynamic VLAs Dynam-icVLA [50] and PUMA [14], the WAM OpenWAM-α [45], and the dynamic WAM DynamicWAM [29]. Each base-line keeps its official architecture and training recipe and is trained on the same demonstrations. To isolate the effect of target-motion input, π0.5 and DynamicWAM also run with our SE(3) history tokens (+SE(3)). OpenWAM-α also runs without its official caching and compilation (off). 

Model and training. The FoveaWAM transformer has 12 layers of width 1024 with 16 heads and 271.8M train-able parameters on top of the frozen MAE ViT-B/16. On OnTimeBench, it is trained from scratch on the agent view for 10 epochs (25,870 updates) and runs in compiled BF16.

Motion input under latency. Precise motion input helps only when the action arrives in time (Fig. 6b). The SE(3) history raises π0.5 by 9.4 points with the world paused but by only 4.3 points at native latency. With 50 and 100 ms added, it lowers the SR of π0.5 by 5.7 and 7.5 points, and the 95% intervals do not overlap. The history describes the target at capture time, so its value decays as the action is delayed. FoveaWAM is designed to keep this value. It encodes the history in 16 tokens and acts within one control period, so the history is still current when the action executes. The history therefore adds 19.6 points to FoveaWAM at native latency, against 4.3 points for π0.5 (Table 3). 

Paused evaluation. Pausing the world hides the cost of latency and reorders 9 of the 11 methods (Fig. 6c). The two OpenWAM-α deployments share one policy and reach 58.7% and 58.4% with the world paused. At native latency, with p95 of 151.7 and 466.3 ms, they reach 46.1% and 15.8%. We presented FoveaWAM, a native 4D world–action model that allocates prediction precision by role, with precise pre-diction for the target, coarse prediction for its surroundings, and none for the static scene. This allocation alleviates the tension between accurate prediction and timely action. FoveaWAM acts in 15.1 ms and outperforms the strongest baselines on OnTimeBench, DOMINO, and real robots. We also introduced OnTimeBench, which evaluates every pol-icy at its native latency and shows that paused evaluation overestimates slow policies. FoveaWAM currently assumes a rigid target whose pose can be tracked online, and we leave articulated and deformable targets to future work.

A.1. Contact Error Analysis 

Sec. 1 argues that prediction requirements differ by role. This section derives the allocation from the error at contact. We bound the error between the end effector and the target at the time a command executes, and assign each term to the target, the context, or the static scene (Sec. 3.1). Three conclusions follow. Target errors enter the contact directly and grow with the time between capture and execution, so the target requires a precise forecast and a short latency. Context errors change the outcome only when they exceed the clearance, so a coarse forecast suffices. The static scene contributes no error that grows over time, so the current frame suffices. A command can execute only after inference ends, so the lead is at least the inference latency and grows with it. Hats denote the estimates behind a policy’s commands.

Context. The context affects contact in two ways. It can block the path of the robot, and it can drive the target. Sup-pose the executed path keeps the robot and the carried object at least m away from the predicted positions of the context. A collision then requires a context point to deviate from its prediction by at least m, so smaller errors do not change the outcome. When the clearance m is much larger than the contact tolerance ε, a coarse forecast suffices for the context, whereas the target must still be predicted within ε. A finer context forecast becomes necessary only when the clearance approaches the error of the coarse one. A context that drives the target, such as a conveyor or a ramp, enters through edyn 

instead. Its effect appears in the motion of the target, so the future trajectory of the target already carries it. 

Static scene. The static scene does not move. The current frame gives its geometry up to sensing error, and none of its terms grows with the lead. It needs no forecast. 

Latency. Every target term except eloc and θloc grows with the lead. The velocity terms grow linearly, and edyn grows faster when the target accelerates or is struck. A shorter la-tency reduces all of these terms at once. Conversely, a policy with n times the latency of another has about n times the lead, so it needs an n times more accurate velocity estimate to keep the same velocity term. FoveaWAM therefore keeps both its inputs and its predictions compact. The remaining term ∥v∥eℓ comes from an error in the execution time. It vanishes when each command executes at its intended time, as for every method here (Appendix B.3).

Scene tokens. The frozen MAE ViT-B/16 [16] turns the RGB image into 196 patch tokens. Each patch token receives a learned view embedding and two linear embeddings. One embeds the mean valid depth of the patch. The other embeds the base-frame position xi of its center pixel, back-projected with the depth map. A patch without valid depth at its center receives no position embedding. Only the patch tokens carry 3D rotary embeddings, computed from xi. The same points 

xi are the source points of the sparse 3D flow. 

State tokens. The 8 target-state tokens describe the tar-get at the offsets −350 , −300 , . . . , 0 ms. Each is a 25-dimensional vector (p, log R, ∆p, log ∆ R, ∆t, v, ω, a, α).Slots 0–6 describe the seven intervals between consecutive offsets, and slot 7 holds the current pose. Velocities and ac-celerations are finite differences over the elapsed times. Each dimension is standardized, and invalid dimensions are set to zero. Three flags mark whether the pose, the interval, and the acceleration are valid. A slot with no valid feature is masked out of attention. The robot tokens hold the end-effector posi-tion, rotation vector, and gripper opening at the same offsets. The 16 summary tokens are learned embeddings. 

Backbone and heads. In each layer, world and action to-kens attend under the mask of Fig. 3(b). The two streams share the query, key, value, and output projections of the attention. Each stream has its own feed-forward network with SiLU activation and a hidden width of 4096. World to-kens use layer normalization, and action tokens use adaptive layer normalization conditioned on the flow time u. Each prediction head is a linear layer on the final world-stream states, except the target reconstruction, which is a two-layer MLP. The pose head reads the mean summary state ( LP ,Eq. 3). The flow head reads each patch state and gives the displacement and visibility logit of its point at every horizon (LF , Eq. 2). All heads are skipped at deployment. 

Inference time. For each of the 900 profiled queries in Table S3, we subtract the three stage times from the query’s wall time to obtain other query time.

A.3. Training 

Action labels. Each action token embeds one seven-dimensional command, which consists of a base-frame trans-lation increment, a rotation vector that left-multiplies the current orientation, and a gripper command. Both incre-ments are relative to the measured end-effector pose when the command is applied. Command j of query t is labeled with the demonstration command issued at τt + j∆a. In the post-impact grasp, labels follow the issue time (Eq. (S4)). Commands are standardized with training statistics. 

Prediction labels. Flow labels come from the simulator. The surface point at each patch center follows the ground-truth rigid motion of its MuJoCo geometry. A future point is visible if it projects onto the same geometry within a depth tolerance of max(1 mm , 0.001 z), where z is its depth. Invisible points still supervise displacement, with visibility label zero. Displacements are divided by the scale s =0.715 m, and the visibility term has weight 1. Pose labels are the target’s ground-truth poses. Horizons beyond the last frame of a demonstration are excluded from both losses. 13 Auxiliary losses. Laux sums four terms. Three of them are squared errors on future metric depth at valid pixels, on the future RGB of surfaces that move by more than 1 mm, and on future layer-normalized DINOv3 features [37]. The fourth reconstructs the RGB of the current target region from the mean summary state with an absolute error. 

Optimization. Each batch holds 512 windows. A window contains up to eight consecutive queries from one demonstra-tion, and each query has its own causal history. The world stream runs once per query, as at inference. Its keys and values are reused for eight independent draws of action noise and flow time, and the action loss averages over these draws. All experiments, including the baselines, the ablations, and DROID pretraining, ran on 64 NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 and 32 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. One training run of FoveaWAM takes about 24 h on 16 of these RTX PRO 6000 GPUs. 

## B. OnTimeBench and Evaluation Protocol 

B.1. Tasks 

Table S4 lists the target and context of each task (Sec. 3.1). The tasks differ in which terms of the decomposition in Appendix A.1 matter. The target moves in every task, so the velocity term always applies. In the seven rotation-sensitive tasks, the target also rotates, and its orientation matters at contact. In the nine context-active tasks, the context limits the clearance or drives the target. In the oscillating, capture, and impact tasks, the motion of the target changes within the episode through oscillation, gravity, or a collision, so its current velocity is not enough. Fig. 4c shows all tasks. 

B.2. Construction 

Simulation. The tasks run in MuJoCo 3.11.0 and robosuite 1.5.2 [44, 66] with a Franka Panda arm and a parallel grip-per. The operational-space controller of robosuite tracks the commanded end-effector pose. The control period is 50 ms, and the physics step is 2 ms. A fixed third-person camera with a 40 ◦ vertical field of view renders 224 × 224 RGB-D images. Actions are the seven-dimensional commands of Appendix A.3. Each translation component is clipped to 0.10 m, and each rotation component to 0.5 rad. 

Scenes. Each scene samples the target’s initial state and motion parameters uniformly within task-specific ranges. Table S4 gives the speed ranges. Other sampled parameters include harmonic amplitudes of up to 35 mm at 0.25–0.7 Hz, yaw rates of up to 1.2 rad/s, and ramp slopes of 5◦, 10 ◦, or 

15 ◦. In the occluded grasp, the target is hidden for 0.5–3.0 s and then reappears. Only physically feasible scenes are kept, and every method replays the same stored scenes.

Protocols. In Native , each query draws Lt from the la-tencies of its method measured on one RTX 5090. Physics advances by this amount, however long the rollout actually takes on the RTX PRO 6000. The latency sweep adds 50, 100, 150, or 200 ms to every Lt. Its 150 ms point is the +150 ms setting of Table 1. In Paused , physics stops during each query. Latency measurement. We time each method alone on one RTX 5090 at batch size 1. Each baseline runs in the precision of its release, and FoveaWAM runs in compiled BF16 with TF32 disabled. Each method runs three rounds of 30 warm-up calls and 300 measured calls on recorded obser-vations with their causal history. A timed query starts from the newest observation and ends when its first command is available. We call this interval the time-to-action. It in-cludes all computation that needs the newest frame, namely preprocessing, image encoding, the online flow and motion descriptors of PUMA and DynamicWAM, sampling, and ac-tion decoding. PUMA is timed with its three official cameras and the other baselines with two, which changes their p95 time-to-action by at most 3.8 ms relative to the agent view.

Common conditions. Each baseline keeps its official ar-chitecture, chunk length, initialization, and training recipe (Table S5). SE(3)-augmented baselines. π0.5+SE(3) and Dynam-icWAM+SE(3) receive the eight target-state tokens of Ap-pendix A.2 from the same pose source and with the same validity flags. In DynamicWAM, they join its kinematic descriptors. Training follows the recipe of the base model.
