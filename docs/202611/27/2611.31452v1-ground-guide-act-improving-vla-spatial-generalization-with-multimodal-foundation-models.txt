Ground, Guide, Act: Improving VLA Spatial Generalization with
Multimodal Foundation Models
Guangting Zheng*, Shaoxuan Wang*, Yu Yuan, Zhipeng Tang, Jiajun Deng, Yanyong Zhang
University of Science and Technology of China

Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models effectively learn ac-
tion policies from robot demonstrations, but struggle to gen-
eralize to changes in object positions and task goals. Gen-
eral multimodal foundation models (MFMs) offer comple-
mentary scene understanding and spatial reasoning, but re-
main slow and unreliable for direct fine-grained control.
To understand how MFMs can improve VLA spatial gen-
eralization, we conduct a systematic capability analysis
across multiple VLA architectures using atomic-stage inter-
ventions, revealing that many failures stem from poor target
grounding and global spatial routing, while local manipu-
lation skills remain largely usable. Based on these findings,
we propose MFM-VLA, a selective collaboration frame-
work in which an MFM provides target grounding and spa-
tial guidance, controllers execute free-space motion, and
the VLA retains control over fine-grained manipulation. We
fine-tune the VLA with action-chunk-level dense geometric
captions and perturbed-state recovery data to improve local
guidance following and recovery from unfamiliar handoff
states. A development-calibrated capability router selects
between direct VLA execution and MFM-assisted execution
to avoid unnecessary intervention. Across LIBERO-PRO,
ALOHA-Sim-PRO, RoboCasa365, and real-world chem-
istry tasks, MFM-VLA improves success rates by 14.9, 74.0,
3.6, and 30.9 percentage points, respectively, over the cor-
responding base VLA. The project page is available at
https://tom-zgt.github.io/MFM-VLA.
1. Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models learn manipulation
policies from demonstrations [2, 3, 10, 12, 27], but struggle
with changes in object positions and task goals due in part
to limited demonstration coverage [6, 11]. General mul-
timodal foundation models (MFMs) offer complementary
capabilities in scene understanding, spatial reasoning, and
*These authors contributed equally.
task planning [1, 7, 9]. Yet direct action generation with
these models is slow and unreliable for fine-grained manip-
ulation, while local trajectory corrections alone may not re-
solve errors in target selection and spatial routing [21, 23].
This raises a central question: how can MFMs improve VLA
spatial generalization?
To answer this question, we conduct a capability analy-
sis across multiple VLA architectures, combining counter-
factual instruction tests with a five-level Phase-Decoupled
Intervention Staircase (PDIS). Counterfactual tests provide
evidence of visual scene shortcuts: policies often select the
original training target despite updated instructions. By de-
composing manipulation into atomic stages, PDIS further
identifies target grounding and global spatial routing as ma-
jor bottlenecks, while showing that local manipulation skills
remain largely usable under appropriate spatial guidance.
For example, guiding the end effector to the correct tar-
get increases the target-lifting rate of π0.5 from 42.3% to
93.1%. These findings suggest that MFMs should provide
target grounding and spatial guidance, while VLAs retain
fine-grained manipulation control.
Based on these findings, we propose MFM-VLA, a se-
lective collaboration framework in which an MFM iden-
tifies task-relevant manipulation targets and provides spa-
tial guidance, controllers execute free-space motion, and
the VLA handles fine-grained manipulation. To help the
VLA use external guidance, we fine-tune it with action-
chunk-level dense geometric captions and perturbed-state
recovery data, improving local guidance following and re-
covery from unfamiliar handoff states. We further intro-
duce a development-calibrated capability router that selects
between direct VLA execution and MFM-assisted execu-
tion, avoiding unnecessary intervention on tasks the VLA
already handles well.
We evaluate MFM-VLA on the LIBERO-PRO bench-
mark [39], ALOHA-Sim-PRO [36], RoboCasa365 [18],
and real-world multi-stage chemistry tasks [25], achiev-
ing success-rate gains of 14.9, 74.0, 3.6, and 30.9 percent-
age points over the corresponding base VLAs, respectively.
We also explore an extended system in which the MFM

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We systematically analyze multiple pretrained VLAs
through counterfactual instruction tests and phase-

cuts and identifying the roles of target grounding, spatial
routing, and local manipulation in VLA failures.
• We propose MFM-VLA, a selective collaboration frame-

and MFM-based spatial guidance.
• We evaluate MFM-VLA on three simulation benchmarks
and real-world chemistry tasks, demonstrating its gener-
alization and effectiveness across scenes and tasks.
2. Related Work
Vision-Language-Action Models.
VLA models have
made significant progress in robotic manipulation. RT-2 [3]
transfers vision-language knowledge to robot control, while
OpenVLA [12] provides an open-source model trained on
large-scale robot demonstrations. π0 [2] uses flow matching
for continuous action generation, and π0.5 [10] extends this
approach toward open-world generalization.
OpenVLA-
OFT [13] and OpenWAM [28] explore efficient policy fine-
tuning and unified world-action modeling, respectively. De-
spite these advances, generalization remains challenging
when scenes, object positions, or task goals differ from
training data [39]. We analyze the roles of target grounding,
spatial routing, and local control in these failures to identify
where external MFMs can effectively assist VLA policies.
Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation. Language
and multimodal foundation models have been used for
robotic task planning, executable program generation, and
spatial grounding [1, 9, 15, 38]. Other approaches integrate
vision-language models (VLMs) into robot policies for spa-
tial guidance and action correction [22, 32], while coding
agents generate and refine manipulation skills through ex-
ecution feedback [4, 8, 17, 30, 35]. PLD further improves
pretrained VLAs through reinforcement learning and recov-
ery data generation [29]. Effective collaboration also re-
quires identifying VLA capability gaps, adapting policies to
external guidance, and avoiding unnecessary intervention.
Concurrent work explores learned intervention for selec-
tive GPT assistance [24], visual-trace guidance for VLA
policies [37], and hybrid control with pretrained VLAs [26,
33]. Other studies investigate direct VLM action genera-
tion [34] and hierarchical VLM–policy collaboration [31].
Unlike uniform guidance, MFM-VLA combines capability
analysis, geometric policy adaptation, and calibrated rout-
ing for selective MFM assistance.
3. Capability Analysis of Pretrained VLAs
To understand the limitations of pretrained VLAs and iden-
tify where MFM assistance is needed, we investigate their
generalization under perturbations, instruction following,
and stage-wise manipulation capabilities.
3.1. Performance Gap Under Perturbations
High success on familiar scenes does not necessarily indi-
cate that a VLA can adapt to new spatial configurations or
task goals. To examine this, we evaluate the official π0.5,
trained on LIBERO, on LIBERO-PRO over 8,000 episodes
with four types of perturbations: Semantic (instruction para-
phrases), Object (appearance changes), Position (object lo-
cation swaps), and Task (changes to the instructed target).
While Semantic and Object perturbations preserve the re-
quired spatial trajectories, Position and Task perturbations
require changes in target selection or spatial movement.
As shown in Fig. 1(a), π0.5 maintains high success un-
der Semantic (92.0%) and Object (80.2%) perturbations,
but drops sharply under Position (22.2%) and Task (25.1%)
perturbations, reaching only 2.4% on LIBERO-10 Position.
This gap suggests that pretrained VLAs handle surface-
level variations better than changes requiring new target se-
lection and spatial trajectories. We next investigate whether
these failures arise from an over-reliance on familiar visual
scenes rather than insufficient local manipulation skills.
3.2. Visual Scene Shortcuts
The performance gap under Task perturbations raises a
question: do VLAs fail to manipulate the newly instructed
targets, or do they simply ignore updated instructions? To
investigate this, we conduct counterfactual instruction tests
by keeping the scene fixed while changing the instruc-
tion. Specifically, we compare an updated instruction ℓnew,
which specifies a new target onew instead of the original
training target otrain, with an empty instruction ℓ∅= "".
We evaluate π0.5, π0, OpenVLA-OFT, and OpenWAM on
LIBERO-Object Task perturbations, with 50 episodes per
condition. We measure the lifting rates of both targets and
the Same-Lift Rate, which measures whether paired rollouts
under the two instructions lift the same object first.
As shown in Fig. 1(b), OpenVLA-OFT and OpenWAM
lift the original training target in 90.0% and 92.0% of
empty-instruction episodes, respectively. Updated instruc-
tions often fail to redirect this behavior: OpenVLA-OFT
never lifts the newly instructed target, lifts the original tar-
get in 94.0% of episodes, and achieves a 96.0% Same-Lift
Rate. Similar patterns are observed for π0.5 on LIBERO-
10 (90.0% agreement) and π0 on bimanual ALOHA-Sim
(86.0% agreement). These results suggest a visual scene
shortcut: pretrained VLAs may use familiar visual configu-
rations to retrieve manipulation behaviors, often overriding
updated language goals. This indicates that improving local

3.3. Atomic Stage Analysis
The preceding analysis reveals target-selection failures but
does not determine whether VLA policies retain local ma-
nipulation skills once the correct target is identified. To in-
vestigate this, we decompose pick-and-place tasks into four
atomic stages: Reach (R), moving the open gripper above
the target; Grasp, acquiring and lifting the object; Carry
(C), transporting it above the destination; and Lower/Place
(L), lowering and releasing the object.
We introduce a
five-level Phase-Decoupled Intervention Staircase (PDIS)
to isolate the effects of assistance at different stages: Base
uses the VLA throughout; +R assists Reach; +C assists
Carry after a successful grasp; +RC assists both Reach and
Carry; and +RCL additionally assists Lower/Place. We use
ground-truth spatial guidance with fixed VLA weights to
isolate stage-specific assistance benefits without introduc-
ing MFM grounding errors. All four VLA architectures are
evaluated over 390 perturbed episodes per setting.
As shown in Fig. 2, Reach assistance increases π0.5’s
target-lifting rate from 42.3% to 93.1% under Position per-
turbations and from 40.0% to 91.5% under Task perturba-
tions. Similar improvements across other architectures sug-
gest that local grasping skills remain largely usable once
the gripper reaches the correct target. However, successful
grasping does not guarantee task completion. Under Posi-
tion perturbations, +R achieves only 42.3% task success
despite a 93.1% target-lifting rate. Adding Carry assistance
(+RC) increases task success to 68.5% on Position and
77.7% on Task, while +RCL further improves these re-
sults to 71.5% and 86.9%, respectively. These results reveal
complementary capabilities: MFMs provide target ground-
ing and free-space spatial guidance, while VLAs retain use-
ful local manipulation skills. This motivates a collaborative
approach that combines MFM spatial reasoning with VLA-
based fine-grained control.

4. Method
Our analysis identifies target grounding and global spa-
tial routing as major sources of VLA failures, while local
manipulation skills remain largely usable. Based on this,
we propose MFM-VLA (Fig. 3), a selective collaboration
framework with three components: (1) dense geometric pol-
icy adaptation to enable local guidance following and re-
covery from perturbed states; (2) development-calibrated
capability routing to determine when to invoke MFMs;
and (3) MFM-based 3D grounding and spatial guidance to
guide free-space motion while retaining VLA control over
fine-grained manipulation. We primarily use GPT-6 Astra
with π0.5, and the official π0 policy for ALOHA-Sim-PRO.

distribution states in imitation learning [20]. To enable the
VLA to follow external guidance, we adapt π0.5 through
perturbed-state recovery and action-chunk-level dense geo-
metric captions.

4.3. Task-Aware Grounding and Spatial Guidance
In ASSISTED mode, the MFM identifies task-relevant ob-
jects and manipulation-specific target points, providing spa-
tial guidance while the adapted VLA retains control over
local manipulation.
Two-view 3D Target Grounding. To estimate 3D target
positions without depth images, we use two calibrated RGB
views: a fixed camera and either a second fixed camera or a
wrist-mounted camera. Given instruction ℓ, the MFM iden-
tifies task-relevant objects and selects manipulation-specific
target points based on object structure and task require-
ments.
For example, opening a bottle requires locating
its cap, whereas placing an object into a basket requires
grounding an appropriate point inside the basket rather than
its object center.
It then refines these points in zoomed
crops and establishes semantic correspondences across the
two camera views. Camera calibration converts the matched
points into viewing rays, whose closest-approach midpoint
provides the 3D estimates: ˆpgrasp and ˆpplace. Geometric
consistency checks and fallback rules are detailed in the Ap-
pendix.
Stage-wise Spatial Guidance and VLA Execution. For
single-arm and mobile manipulation, execution follows four
stages: Reach, Grasp & Lift, Carry, and Place & Release.
Using the grounded targets, a Cartesian controller moves
the open gripper to a pre-grasp handoff pose above the tar-
get. The adapted VLA πθ then performs local alignment,
descent, grasping, and lifting. After grasp verification, the
controller transports the object to a pre-place handoff pose
above the destination, where the VLA resumes control for
lowering and release. The grounded targets also provide
the relative geometric conditions for dense captions. MFM
guidance is queried sparsely, while the controller and VLA
execute the corresponding motions in closed loop.
Grasp Verification and Local Recovery. A separate mon-
itor checks grasp acquisition and object retention.
If a
failed grasp, wrong-object lift, or drop is detected, the con-
troller opens the gripper, retreats, and returns to a pre-grasp
handoff state for another VLA grasp attempt. Recovery is
bounded by the maximum retry count and episode step bud-
get. Platform-specific details are provided in the Appendix.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and Evaluation.
We evaluate MFM-VLA
on three simulation benchmarks. LIBERO-PRO [39] com-
prises 160 tasks across 16 suites with four perturbation
types: Semantic, Object, Position, and Task. We use 14,075
trajectories for VLA fine-tuning, including official LIBERO
demonstrations, perturbed-state recovery data.
Initializa-
tion seeds 0–4 are used for router calibration, and the re-
sulting routing decisions are frozen before evaluation. We
report results on the remaining seeds 5–49 (N = 7,200) and
the full benchmark (N = 8,000). Component and router
ablations use seeds 5–9 (N = 800), a subset of the test set.
RoboCasa365 [18] evaluates mobile manipulation across
50 tasks: 18 Atomic-Seen, 16 Composite-Seen, and 16
Composite-Unseen. We fine-tune on 1,437 trajectories from
seen tasks, excluding all Composite-Unseen tasks. For each
task, we compare Direct and Assist execution on five cali-
bration episodes and select Assisted only when it achieves
higher success. The resulting task-level router is frozen and
applied to all 50 episodes per task, yielding 2,500 evaluation
episodes, including the calibration episodes. ALOHA-Sim-
PRO extends the bimanual Transfer Cube task [36] with Se-
mantic, Object, Position, and Task perturbations, alongside
an original setting. We evaluate 50 episodes per condition
using a consistent 400-step horizon and additionally test tar-
get displacements of 0–16 cm beyond the training region.
End-to-end task success is the primary metric; object lifting
and subgoal completion are reported as other metrics.
Real-world Evaluation. We evaluate MFM-VLA on the
Franka–XHand platform with a 7-DoF Franka arm and a
12-DoF XHand dexterous hand across 11 glassware manip-
ulation tasks, including grasping, placement, funnel inser-
tion, and multi-stage compositions. Scene generalization
introduces unseen distractors, while position generalization
moves targets to locations absent from the demonstrations.

5.2. Main Results
LIBERO-PRO. As shown in Tab. 1, MFM-VLA improves
overall success from 54.9% to 69.8% on the full benchmark.
The largest improvements occur under Position (22.2% to
47.5%) and Task perturbations (25.2% to 52.6%), while per-
formance under Semantic and Object perturbations is also
improved. These results demonstrate that MFM assistance
particularly benefits tasks requiring changes in target selec-
tion and spatial movement. Some operations, such as sus-
tained plate pushing, remain beyond the capabilities of π0.5.
We therefore further explore an extended system, MFM-
VLA + Code Policy, in which the MFM selects between
the adapted VLA and task-specific code-based controllers.
This extension improves overall success to 84.4%, demon-
strating the potential of the MFM-VLA framework.

6. Conclusion
We investigate how MFMs can improve the spatial gen-
eralization of pretrained VLAs and make two key obser-
vations. First, systematic analysis reveals that pretrained
VLAs often rely on visual scene shortcuts, leading to incor-
rect target selection and spatial routing, while local manip-
ulation skills remain largely usable. Second, indiscriminate
8


MFM assistance can interfere with tasks that VLAs already
handle well. Based on these findings, we propose MFM-
VLA, a selective collaboration framework that combines
dense geometric policy adaptation, development-calibrated
routing, and MFM-based spatial guidance.
Experiments
across three simulation benchmarks and real-world chem-
istry tasks demonstrate improved spatial generalization and
reduced shortcut-driven failures while preserving the ma-
nipulation capabilities of pretrained VLAs. We hope this
work provides a practical direction for combining MFM
spatial reasoning with learned VLA manipulation skills.

Input: Task instruction ℓ, routing unit τ, frozen router R, adapted VLA
πθ, calibrated RGB cameras.
1 if R(τ) = DIRECT then execute πθ with ℓ; return.
2 Acquire fixed-view image Ia.
3 Ground the grasp target and destination in Ia with coarse-to-fine
MFM queries.
4 Plan and execute a free-space wrist survey move; acquire second
view Ib.
5 for each target x ∈{grasp, place} do
6
Match the task-relevant contact point across Ia and Ib.
7
Triangulate, check visibility and ray gap, and reobserve if nec-
essary.
8
Set ˆpx to the accepted 3D point, or ∅if invalid.
9 end for
10 if ˆpgrasp̸ = ∅then estimate support height and execute Carte-

Algorithm S1 summarizes the inference procedure of
MFM-VLA. A frozen capability router first determines
whether an episode requires external assistance.
Direct
episodes are executed entirely by the adapted VLA. For
Assisted episodes, the MFM localizes task-relevant targets
from two sequential RGB observations, after which geo-
metric checks determine whether Cartesian handoffs are re-
liable. The controller handles free-space movement where
valid targets are available, while the VLA retains con-
trol over contact-rich manipulation. Visual verification and
bounded recovery are used to handle incorrect grasps and
dropped objects.
Capability routing.
The router compares Direct and As-
sisted success rates measured on a development set and se-
lects Assisted execution only when the estimated gain ex-
ceeds δ = 0.05 (5 percentage points) and the task supports
the handoff interface (C(τ) = 1). We use suite-level rout-
ing with K = 5 calibration episodes per task by default and
freeze the resulting routing decisions before evaluation. A
Direct decision executes πθ with the original instruction ℓ
without invoking the MFM.
Target grounding and view acquisition.
For Assisted
execution, the MFM first identifies the object to grasp
and its destination in the fixed RGB view. A full-frame
query proposes candidates, and a subsequent zoomed-crop
query resolves the intended object and its base-contact
pixel. These first-view observations define calibrated rays
for planning a free-space survey move that places the wrist
camera where the candidate targets are likely to be visible.
Only after this move do we acquire the wrist RGB image.
Conditioned on the selected objects in the first view, the
MFM then identifies the corresponding manipulation points
in the two views, rather than independently selecting a new
object in the wrist view.

7.2. Benchmark Setup and Inference
LIBERO-PRO.
LIBERO-PRO [39] contains 160 tasks
across 16 suites, covering four perturbation types: Semantic
(instruction paraphrases), Object (appearance changes), Po-
sition (object displacement and swaps), and Task (changes
in instructed targets).
We fine-tune π0.5 on 14,075 tra-
jectories, including 6,350 official demonstrations, 5,597
perturbed-state recovery rollouts, and 2,128 spatial aug-
mentations, for 10,000 steps. We evaluate 50 initializations
per task (N = 8,000), using init 0--4 for router cali-
bration and init 5--49 for held-out evaluation.
We use GPT-6 Astra [19] as the external MFM and a
frozen suite-level router with δ = 0.05, calibrated on five
initializations per task. The router selects Direct execution
for 11 suites and Assisted execution for the remaining five.
The adapted VLA receives 224 × 224 RGB observations
and replans every five action steps. Episode horizons fol-
low the benchmark settings: 220 steps for Spatial, 280 for
Object, 300 for Goal, and 520 for LIBERO-10 tasks. In As-
sisted mode, the MFM grounds task-relevant targets from
RGB observations, while the Cartesian controller and VLA
execute the corresponding spatial handoffs and local manip-
ulation.
RoboCasa365.
We evaluate a 50-task subset of Robo-
Casa365 [18], comprising 18 Atomic-Seen, 16 Composite-
Seen, and 16 Composite-Unseen tasks, with 50 episodes
per task (N = 2,500).
The first five episodes per task
(N = 250) are used for task-level router calibration, while
the remaining 45 episodes (N = 2,250) form the held-out
evaluation set. The reported full-benchmark results include
all 2,500 episodes, including the 250 router-calibration
episodes. We additionally distinguish the 2,250 episodes
held out from router selection for evaluation. We fine-tune
π0.5 from the official 75k-step checkpoint for 7,500 cumu-
lative steps using 1,437 trajectories from seen tasks (563
human demonstrations and 874 recovery rollouts), exclud-
ing all Composite-Unseen tasks.
GPT-6 Astra decomposes the task into up to three or-
dered manipulation stages. The task-level routing decisions
are determined from five calibration episodes per task and
frozen before evaluation. For Assisted execution, the MFM
identifies stage-specific targets and selects either top-down
or front-horizontal approaches. Grounding uses calibrated
left and right fixed RGB cameras, supplemented by an ac-
tively positioned wrist camera when needed. The adapted
VLA receives three RGB views and proprioceptive states,
with 224 × 224 image inputs and replanning every five
action steps. Dynamic dense geometric captions provide
stage-specific target-relative guidance during execution.
