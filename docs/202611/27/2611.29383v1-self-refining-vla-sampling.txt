Abstract: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models typically use flow-based gen-erative modeling to learn a rich, often multimodal, distribution of robot actions. Generating samples from flow-based methods involves iteratively updating ran-dom noise, and distinct noise samples from the same state can result in distinct trajectories and task outcomes. Existing test-time scaling methods improve ac-tion chunk generation via selection, aggregation, or correction, but commonly require the generation of large ensembles of candidates or the training of an exter-nal corrector. We adapt Predict-and-Perturb (P&P), a self-refinement procedure for flow-based models originally used for mode-seeking in video generation, to the robotics domain. We find that applying P&P to VLA models requires mod-ifications to the refinement process used in video generation due to differences in the underlying structure of the data. Across 400 episodes of LIBERO evalu-ated using SmolVLA, P&P improves upon the stock baseline by 5.25% . Further, episode-level P&P uncertainty correlates strongly with task failure (AUC 0.851 ). 

Keywords: Test-time optimization, generalist policies, flow matching, steering 

# 1 Introduction 

Learning-based robot manipulation has progressed rapidly through Vision-Language-Action mod-els (VLAs), which combine pretrained vision-language backbones with action generators [2, 3, 4]. These models report high scores on popular benchmarks such as LIBERO [5]. Most VLAs generate actions through a flow-matching process, which iteratively transforms a random noise sample into an action chunk by integrating along a learned velocity field [6, 3, 4]. However, recent evidence suggests that sampled VLA trajectories are highly stochastic: at identical simulator states, distinct noise samples can lead to markedly different trajectories and eventual task success rates [7, 8]. The fact that generated actions are highly variable suggests that VLAs can be improved at test time via selection, aggregation, or guidance towards an “optimal” action chunk that maximizes later success. Common test-time scaling methods use additional candidate generations or externally-learned critics: TACO [7] samples 32 actions and selects one using a pseudo-count estimator, while Q-Planning samples 64 actions and aggregates them using weights from a learned Q-function [9]. These methods effectively improve VLA success rates, but add computational overhead. One potential cause of this is off-mode behavior: flow-based policies learn a distribution over actions based on finitely many expert demonstrations, which means that the learned distribution of the policy may contain incorrectly interpolated regions that do not correspond to desirable behaviors. The flow-based generation process may produce samples from these off-mode parts of the learned distribution. As such, this work investigates whether a minimal test-time correction can improve VLA success rate without the use of an external critic or the generation of many candidates, by employing test-time techniques to steer the policy towards modes in its learned distribution. P&P works by iteratively “predicting” a clean sample (using the velocity field at an earlier timestep) and “perturbing” back to the designated timestep. Jang et al. [1] characterize this as a procedure that steers generations towards the learned data manifold, particularly towards modes, i.e., areas of high probability density. While adapting P&P, we make adjustments in cases where VLA policies do not permit tools originally used by Jang et al. [1]. Most significantly, instead of using selective P&P refinement on locally uncertain pixel regions, we repurpose P&P refinement as a means for on-manifold aggregation of two action chunks. We find that when applied to SmolVLA, P&P refinement improves SmolVLA’s success rate on LIBERO by 5.25% . 2.1 Flow-based VLA policies and SmolVLA 

VLA policies have become a standard recipe for robot manipulation, mapping camera and lan-guage input to robot actions by scaling imitation learning over large datasets. SmolVLA [10] is a compact 450M-parameter VLA that combines a SmolVLM-2 vision-language backbone with a flow-matching transformer action expert. The model predicts an action chunk of H = 50 steps at a time, along C = 7 dimensions per step. Chunks are executed open-loop: the first E < H 

steps are enacted before the environment is re-observed and another action chunk is generated. As such, a correction (such as P&P refinement) applied during sampling is committed for the whole chunk before any feedback is observed. By default, the recommended configuration for SmolVLA on LIBERO uses E = 1 .

2.2 Inference-time correction and guidance-based steering 

Prior work has explored approaches to control the entire process of sampling from the rich, often multimodal, distribution expressed by VLAs. The inherent stochasticity in the sampling process can be exploited to utilize external models to perform discrete search, such as to rerank actions generated by the model [11]. Another prominent approach is to augment the vector field used during the iterative integration process with auxiliary vector fields from sources such as the gradient of a learned quality function [12, 13], a learned residual actor [14], or an external vision-language model [15]. Our approach utilizes the VLA’s inherent learned structure of the action distribution to perform exploration at inference time. This obviates the need for training, calibrating, or verifying an external model, and scales with the intrinsic quality of the VLA.

# 4 Experiments and Results 

4.1 Experiment Setup 

We evaluate the publicly available LIBERO-finetuned SmolVLA checkpoint on the four standard LIBERO suites (LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Goal, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-10). Each suite contains 10 tasks with 50 numbered starting-state configurations. From each suite, we allocate 100 episodes (states 0-9 of each task, 400 episodes total) towards a held-out evaluation set, and 50 episodes (states 10-14) towards a validation set. We evaluate the checkpoint’s unrefined policy using two configurations: E = 1 executed actions per chunk generation (the recommended default configuration) and E = 10 . Unless stated otherwise, we use H = 50 , C = 7 , and ten Euler integration steps. In total, six configurations are compared on this 400-episode evaluation set: the stock and refined policies are each evaluated with E ∈ { 1, 10 }. The refined steps are s = 0 .9, 0.8, 0.7 (corresponding to Euler steps i = 1 , 2, 3) with K0.9 = 3 , K 0.8 = K0.7 = 1 refinement iterations. Two additional stock/refinement aggregation configurations (described in Section 3.2) are included: a refined ag-gregation arm and a naive average baseline, each using E = 10 . We report overall success rates for each configuration, along with per-suite success rate and episode-level transition comparisons for select configurations. Finally, we evaluate whether high episode-level U10 (defined in Sec. 3.1) during P&P refinement correlates highly with task failure.

# 5 Conclusion 

We adapt Predict-and-Perturb, originally developed for self-refining video generation, to flow-matching VLA policies. Along with P&P refinement, we propose refined aggregation, in which the average of a stock and refined chunk is further refined at a late flow time s = 0 .3 in order to steer towards higher-density regions of the action distribution. Across a 400-episode LIBERO evaluation set, our method improves upon the stock policy by 5.25% . We additionally find that episode-level uncertainty is strongly associated with failure, achieving a pooled failure AUC of 0.851 . Together, these results suggest that a flow policy’s own refinement dynamics can provide useful test-time in-tervention. 

Future Work. Our investigation is limited to the SmolVLA LIBERO checkpoint and 400-episode LIBERO subset. Further experiments could extend this investigation to larger models (such as π0.5,which approaches saturation on LIBERO), evaluate using repeated-seed results, and assess whether refinement assists in real-world observations and difficult, out-of-distribution benchmarks such as LIBERO-PRO. 7Further work should closely analyze whether chunk-level uncertainty can be purposed as a useful online signal for selective refinement. Another promising direction lies in utilizing P&P for the purpose of training a lightweight Q-corrector. Combining learned guidance towards correct behavior with P&P’s model-internal self-consistency signal towards mode-seeking behavior, and exploration of refined trajectories, may en-able more selective and reliable deployment-time correction. 8Acknowledgments
