## WeaveVLA: 3D Relational Grounding for Vision-Language-Action Models 

Zien Wang 1,* Yuhao Pan 1,* Yunfeng FAN 2 Nan Cheng 3 Xin Xia 4 Wenchao Xu 1, †

Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models leverage pretrained vision-language representations for instruction-conditioned robot control, yet their understanding of task-relevant 3D relations and how these relations evolve during manipulation remains largely implicit. To address this challenge, we introduce WeaveVLA , a framework that enhances VLA action generation by ex-plicitly modeling task-relevant relative 3D relations and maintaining relational context across observations. WeaveVLA employs a task-conditioned sparse graph neural network to encode relative 3D relations among objects, the gripper, and nearby references. Pair-specific recurrent edge states integrate geometric evidence across observations to maintain evolving spatial context. A learned-query readout summarizes the updated graph representations into compact relational tokens, which condition the action expert alongside the original vision-language context. An auxiliary geometry reconstruction objective further encourages spatial structure preservation. We instantiate WeaveVLA on StarVLA-GR00T and π0.5, achieving over 98% average success on LIBERO with both backbones. On LIBERO-Plus, WeaveVLA improves success from 74.9% to 82.0% and from 85.7% to 87.8%, respectively, with additional im-provements on CALVIN D →D. On three real-world tasks, the StarVLA-GR00T-based variant improves average success from 55.6% to 70.0% under standard conditions and from 33.3% to 53.3% under OOD perturbations, surpassing the π0.5 baseline in both settings.

1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models combine pretrained vision-language representations with robot demonstrations to learn instruction-conditioned manipulation policies. Representative approaches such as OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT have advanced general-purpose manipulation, while recent systems have expanded these capabilities across robotic embodiments (Kim et al., 2024, 2025; NVIDIA, 2026). Flow-based policies, including π0, π0.5, and π0.7, further advance continuous action generation, generalization, and steerable control (Black et al., 2025b,a; Physical Intelligence et al., 2026). Despite these advances, understanding and maintaining the spatial relationships that govern manipulation remains an important challenge. Robot actions depend on different relative 3D relations at different stages of manipulation. For example, placing a cup into a bowl requires reasoning about gripper–cup relations during grasping and cup–bowl relations during placement, while other object pairs may be less relevant. These relations change with robot and object motion, while geometric estimation errors, viewpoint changes, and occasional occlusion can make their current configurations difficult to determine reliably (Peng et al., 2026). SpatialVLA and 3DS-VLA improve spatial awareness through geometric representations and sequential spatial constraints (Qu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). GraphCoT-VLA and TriRelVLA further explore structured 3D relations for manipulation (Huang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). This raises a central question: How can VLA models explicitly model task-relevant 3D relations and continuously update their representations as manipulation unfolds to support action generation?

To address this challenge, we introduce WeaveVLA , a framework that explicitly models task-relevant relative 3D relations and their evolution during manipulation to enhance VLA action generation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Its object-centric representation combines visual features with estimated 3D geometry, using the robot’s tool center point (TCP) to anchor gripper–object relations. A task-conditioned graph connects instruction-relevant objects, the gripper, and nearby spatial references, while edge-aware graph attention encodes their relative 3D geometry. To maintain evolving spatial context, WeaveVLA assigns a recurrent state to each selected entity pair and updates it with valid geometric evidence across observations. The resulting relation features are conditioned on the vision-language context and summarized into compact relational tokens. An auxiliary geometry reconstruction objective further encourages the learned relational features to preserve spatial structure. Together with the original vision-language representations, these tokens condition a continuous action expert for robot manipulation. We instantiate WeaveVLA on two VLA backbones, StarVLA-GR00T and π0.5, and evaluate its effectiveness on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022). On the four standard LIBERO suites, WeaveVLA achieves average success rates of 98.3% and 98.2%, respectively, improving both backbones. On LIBERO-Plus, it improves StarVLA-GR00T from 74.9% to 82.0% and π0.5 from 85.7% to 87.8%, demonstrating consistent gains across the two architectures. On CALVIN D →D, WeaveVLA increases the average completed sequence length from 3.645 to 3.910 for StarVLA-GR00T, surpassing the original π0.5 baseline, and from 3.885 to 3.997 for π0.5. Across three real-world tasks, the StarVLA-GR00T-based WeaveVLA achieves average success rates of 70.0% under standard conditions and 53.3% under perturbations, outperforming both baselines. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 1. We introduce WeaveVLA, a 3D relational grounding framework that explicitly models task-relevant spatial relations among objects, the gripper, and nearby references to enhance VLA action generation. 2. We develop pair-specific recurrent relational updating to maintain evolving 3D relations across observations, integrating valid geometric evidence while preserving historical relational context. 3. We demonstrate consistent improvements across two VLA backbones on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and CALVIN D →D, and validate WeaveVLA on three real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming both baselines under standard and OOD conditions.

2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models 

Building on pretrained vision-language representations and robot demonstrations, vision-language-action (VLA) research has advanced toward more generalizable robot manipulation. Early approaches such as RT-2 and OpenVLA demonstrated the transfer of visual-semantic knowledge to robotic control (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). OpenVLA-OFT improved action generation efficiency through continuous action representations, parallel decoding, and action chunking (Kim et al., 2025). Subsequent VLA architectures further advanced continuous action generation and manipulation across diverse tasks (Black et al., 2025b; Bjorck et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2026). More recent models have expanded generalization and adaptability, with π0.5 demonstrating generalization to unseen environments (Black et al., 2025a) and π0.7 advancing steerable execution and compositional generalization (Physical Intelligence et al., 2026). LingBot-VLA 2.0 and GR00T N1.7 further advanced cross-embodiment manipulation (Wu et al., 2026; NVIDIA, 2026), while Gemini Robotics 2 extended generalist control to whole-body and dexterous manipulation. WeaveVLA complements these advances by augmenting VLA action generation with explicit, task-relevant 3D spatial relations. 

2.2 Spatial Grounding for Robotic Manipulation 

Spatial grounding connects visual observations with the geometric structure of the environment to support robotic manipulation. Recent studies have incorporated explicit 3D representations into VLA policies. SpatialVLA introduces 3D positional encoding to enhance spatial awareness (Qu et al., 2025), while 3DS-VLA combines 2D-3D feature alignment with sequential spatial constraints for manipulation (Li et al., 2025). Graph-based representations have also been explored for spatial relation modeling in navigation and autonomous driving, exemplified by BSG and GraphAD (Liu et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2024). Graph-of-Mark further enhances spatial reasoning for multimodal models via graph-based visual prompting (Frisoni et al., 2026). For robotic manipulation, scene graphs have been used for both high-level planning and action con-ditioning. Compose by Focus employs task-relevant graphs to condition diffusion-based manipulation skills (Qi et al., 2026), while GraSP-VLA uses scene graphs for symbolic planning and policy orchestra-tion (Neau et al., 2025). SIR further learns task-relevant graph sparsification for action generation (Mattes et al., 2026). More directly, GraphCoT-VLA combines an updatable 3D pose-object graph with struc-tured reasoning (Huang et al., 2026), while TriRelVLA models object-hand-task relations to condition action prediction (Zhou et al., 2026). Other studies have explored spatiotemporal representations for manipulation: ConsisVLA-4D addresses spatiotemporal consistency (Li et al., 2026), semantic-geometric 

3task representations jointly encode object relations and motion histories (Herbert et al., 2026), and Qian et al. maintain dynamic scene graphs as structured memory under partial observability (Qian et al., 2026). While these studies demonstrate the value of spatial structure and temporal context, WeaveVLA focuses on explicitly encoding task-relevant relative 3D relations for action generation. A task-conditioned graph captures gripper–object and object–reference geometry, while observation-aware recurrent updates maintain pair-specific relational states across observations. 

3 Method 

WeaveVLA grounds robot actions in task-relevant relative 3D relations, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Given RGB observations from the available cameras, proprioception st, and instruction ℓ, the framework associates tracked entities with visual features and estimated 3D geometry. A task-conditioned graph organizes relative spatial relations among relevant objects, the gripper, and nearby references, while pair-specific recurrent states maintain these relations across observations. A learned-query readout then extracts compact relational tokens from the updated graph features, which complement the original vision-language context Ht to condition a continuous action expert. 

3.1 Object-Centric Observation Representation 

To ground 3D spatial relations, WeaveVLA first establishes object-centric visual representations with consistent identities across observations. At episode initialization, an adapted YOLOE detector (Wang et al., 2025) supplies labeled boxes to frozen SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2026); Cutie (Cheng et al., 2024) then propagates instance masks and identities. Robot regions are excluded from object instance masks before feature pooling. Object appearance features are pooled from visual tokens aligned with instance masks in the same camera view, while the original VLM retains its backbone-specific camera inputs. Since visual tokens may only partially overlap an object, we aggregate instance features according to their foreground coverage. 3.2 Spatial Graph Construction and Encoding 

Spatial grounding aligns geometry of tracked objects and the gripper TCP within a shared image-aligned coordinate frame. Masked metric depth from DA3Metric-Large (Lin et al., 2025) is backprojected using camera intrinsics, while the calibrated TCP is transformed into the same frame. Stable references help mitigate depth-scale drift under a fixed camera, with the correction applied only to estimated object geometry. Node features combine foreground appearance, averaged VLM input embeddings of the category label, and a point MLP with max pooling over centered point clouds. Appendix B details the geometry-retention rules and graph-attention operations. The instruction and local geometry jointly determine graph connectivity. A parser and frozen sentence encoder match instruction phrases to object categories and bind the corresponding candidate instances once per episode. Task candidates connect to one another and to the gripper. Each candidate with current geometric support selects its K = 2 nearest currently supported objects, counting existing connections and including unmentioned objects. Selected connections are bidirectional, and eligible object edges can persist during occlusion. Scene nodes with usable geometry can contribute to the readout even if they have no selected edges.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate how explicit 3D relational grounding enhances StarVLA-GR00T and π0.5 on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a), LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025), and CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022), comparing each WeaveVLA instantiation with its corresponding backbone. We further evaluate the StarVLA-GR00T-based variant on three real-world manipulation tasks and analyze key design choices and inference latency.

LIBERO. LIBERO comprises four suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, with Long corresponding to LIBERO-10. We evaluate 500 rollouts per suite and report the success rate of each suite and their average. 

LIBERO-Plus. LIBERO-Plus contains 10,030 perturbed tasks spanning seven perturbation categories. For each model, we reuse its LIBERO-trained checkpoint with unchanged inference settings, without additional training or fine-tuning on LIBERO-Plus. Each perturbed task is evaluated once, and overall success is pooled across all 10,030 trials. Both benchmarks use control-step limits of 220, 280, 300, and 520 for the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites, respectively, with ten settling steps. 

CALVIN. We evaluate WeaveVLA on CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022) under the D →D setting, where models are trained and evaluated in environment D using separate training and evaluation sequences. The evaluation consists of 1,000 sequences of five consecutive language-conditioned subtasks, with at most 360 control steps per subtask. We report the success rates for completing at least one through five consecutive subtasks and the average completed sequence length (Avg. Len.). Each instruction is treated as a separate graph-state episode, with task bindings and recurrent edge states reset at each subtask transition. 

Real-world setup. We evaluate three real-world manipulation tasks as shown in Fig. 3. 1) Bowl Stacking. 

The robot places the first bowl on a plate, then uses its position to align and stack the second bowl. 2) Place Pens in Cups. The robot sequentially picks up two pens and places each in its designated cup. 3) Clean Table. The robot hangs the mug on the rack, then places the first paper ball, the block, and the second paper ball in the basket. For each task and method, we conduct 30 trials under standard conditions and 30 out-of-distribution (OOD) trials, evenly split across table clutter, lighting perturbations, and background variations. We report binary task success and a stage-wise progress score based on completed stages in the predefined task sequence. Appendix G provides detailed task configurations, OOD settings, and progress scoring criteria.

4.2.2 LIBERO-Plus 

Table 2 compares WeaveVLA instantiations with their corresponding backbones on LIBERO-Plus. WeaveVLA improves pooled success from 74.9% to 82.0% with StarVLA-GR00T and from 85.7% to 87.8% with π0.5, corresponding to gains of 7.1 and 2.1 percentage points, respectively. Notably, the StarVLA-based variant improves performance across all seven perturbation categories, with substantial gains of 15.4 points under Camera, 9.4 under Noise, 8.9 under Language, and 6.9 under Robot perturbations. The π0.5-based variant achieves gains of 8.2 and 4.1 points under Robot and Camera perturbations, respectively, while reaching 97.9% and 97.4% success under Light and Background perturbations. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of explicit 3D relational grounding across diverse perturbations and VLA backbones. 

4.2.3 CALVIN 

Table 3 compares WeaveVLA with existing methods on CALVIN D →D. With StarVLA-GR00T as the backbone, WeaveVLA improves the average completed sequence length from 3.645 to 3.910, surpassing the original π0.5 baseline. With π0.5 as the backbone, WeaveVLA improves the average length from 3.885 to 3.997, achieving the highest result among the compared methods. The two WeaveVLA instantiations achieve five-subtask success rates of 64.7% and 69.2%, improving their corresponding backbones by 6.6 and 4.8 percentage points, respectively. These results demonstrate consistent improvements in sequential manipulation across both VLA backbones.

5 Conclusion 

We have introduced WeaveVLA, a framework that enhances VLA models by explicitly encoding task-relevant relative 3D relations and modeling their evolution throughout manipulation. By combining task-conditioned sparse graph construction with pair-specific recurrent relational updating, WeaveVLA integrates current spatial geometry and historical relational context for continuous action generation. Extensive evaluations on simulation benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements across different VLA backbones, highlighting the effectiveness of explicit relational grounding for robotic manipulation. On three real-world tasks, WeaveVLA improves success over both baselines under standard and perturbed conditions.

B Implementation Details 

WeaveVLA maintains graph states using tracked instance identities and ordered entity pairs. Newly observed node features replace cached values, while eligible historical features are retained when observa-tions are missing. A shared GRU maintains separate recurrent states for each ordered pair, initialized to zero for unseen pairs. These states persist across observations but are detached between updates. They are reset when the geometric reference changes, and expire 64 control steps after the last valid update. 

15 Geometric validity is determined by observation consistency and the age of retained information. Static-geometry consistency is assessed using mask IoU, mask-centroid displacement, relative mask-area change, and mean absolute RGB difference within an eroded common mask region, with respective thresholds of 0.85, 1.5 pixels at a 224-pixel reference scale, 15%, and 8 intensity units. Depth-scale correction requires two confirmations and at least two reliable reference objects. The correction factor is clipped to [0 .8, 1.25] and can be retained for up to 16 control steps. Occluded object geometry may remain available for 64 control steps under fixed calibration, subject to clearance from the gripper. The TCP remains independent of depth-scale correction. Retained geometry can contribute to spatial encoding, but only valid geometric support permits recurrent updates, as defined in Eq. (6). Instance appearance features are obtained through foreground-coverage pooling, as described in Eq. (1). Training and inference use identical pooling operations on backbone-specific visual token grids. For StarVLA, Qwen3-VL resizes 224 × 224 images to 256 × 256 , yielding an 8 × 8 post-merger token grid, while π0.5 uses a 16 × 16 SigLIP patch grid. Masks are aligned with their visual tokens, and foreground coverage determines the pooling weights. When instance masks or visible finger regions are unavailable, the corresponding current visual features are set to zero, while eligible historical node features are retained. Instance masks are used to construct graph nodes and pool foreground features. We obtain these masks by using YOLOE bounding boxes to prompt a frozen SAM 3 model. To improve instance coverage within each benchmark, we fine-tune separate YOLOE detectors for LIBERO and CALVIN D-to-D using simulator-derived supervision. We evaluate both the detector outputs and the resulting SAM 3 masks to assess how detector adaptation affects the perception input to the graph module. Simulator states provide paired static-camera RGB observations and visible-instance masks for detector supervision. For LIBERO, the dataset is constructed from 2,000 demonstrations covering 28 canonical 

18 object categories. Before splitting, we group demonstrations by hashes of their complete state and action sequences to keep identical recorded trajectories within the same partition. We remove robot pixels from the detector inputs and convert visible-instance masks into polygon annotations. A frame is discarded if any retained object’s polygon has an IoU below 0.90 with its source mask. This filtering removes 182 frames and yields 12,599 training, 1,578 validation, and 1,641 test frames. The test split contains held-out demonstrations with task categories and simulator assets shared with the training split. For CALVIN, we fine-tune the detector directly on scene-D demonstrations using 11 categories covering scene objects and robot parts. We split demonstration episodes before sampling frames and render the recorded robot and scene states to obtain RGB observations and instance annotations. The dataset contains 256 training frames from 24 episodes and 64 validation frames from seven disjoint episodes. All 320 RGB images are unique, and exact RGB matches to the 40 scene-D comparison frames used below are excluded. Polygon annotations retain contours with at least three vertices and an area of at least two pixels. Both adaptations start from the original YOLOE-11L segmentation checkpoint and use AdamW with an input resolution of 640. For LIBERO, we use an initial learning rate of 2 × 10 −4, a batch size of 16, two warm-up epochs, and up to 30 training epochs, with an early-stopping patience of eight epochs. For CALVIN, we use an initial learning rate of 1.5 × 10 −4, a batch size of four, one warm-up epoch, and eight training epochs. We select the checkpoint with the best validation performance for each benchmark. We evaluate the box and mask predictions produced by YOLOE itself in Table 7. Within each benchmark, the original and fine-tuned detectors use the same evaluation images and canonical vocabulary. LIBERO results are computed on the 1,641 held-out test frames retained after polygon-quality filtering, using a confidence threshold of 0.001 and a non-maximum-suppression IoU threshold of 0.5. CALVIN results are computed on the same 64 scene-D validation frames used for checkpoint selection, with a configured non-maximum-suppression IoU threshold of 0.7. Fine-tuning improves all reported box and mask AP metrics under these evaluation protocols.

F Policy Ablation Protocols 

Table 4 compares the original StarVLA-GR00T backbone, full WeaveVLA, and its three ablated variants on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. The three ablated WeaveVLA variants use the same backbone, training data, optimization budget, and checkpoint-selection rule as the full model. Evaluation uses matched initial states and 500 rollouts per LIBERO suite, while LIBERO-Plus reports pooled success rates across perturbations without additional fine-tuning. Across the full model and its three ablations, perception, tracked identities, TCP anchoring, coverage-weighted appearance pooling, and the original VLM context are kept consistent. Removing geometry reconstruction sets λg = 0 , retaining the flow-matching objective and all geometric inputs. The no-edge-memory variant removes the GRU and its pair-specific hidden states. Spatial edge features are passed to the subsequent VLM-conditioning and readout stages without recurrent fusion, while geometry caching and graph connectivity remain unchanged. The full-connectivity variant replaces task-conditioned connections with directed edges between all distinct valid scene nodes, including task-irrelevant objects and the gripper. For N valid nodes, this produces N (N − 1) directed edges without self-loops. No additional ground-truth objects are introduced. Relative geometric descriptors, recurrent edge updates, observation-validity rules, and the remaining model components are preserved.
