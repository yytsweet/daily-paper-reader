Independently pretrained language models and vision–language–action (VLA) policies provide complemen-
tary building blocks for embodied agents, but connecting them does not automatically produce coordinated
behavior. The VLA may generate actions that diverge from the LLM’s intended subtask and, upon regaining
control after a correction, undo the promising state restored by the LLM. We introduce ESI (Embodied
Self-Improvement), an automated flywheel that uses environmental interaction to align reasoning and action.
ESI treats the modular agent as the unit of post-training, repeatedly refining the prompts that guide the LLM,
the shared skill interface that expresses its intent, and the VLA parameters that realize that intent, then
redeploying these components together. Our key insight is that collaboration itself can supply supervision
for improving this interface. The LLM’s subtask decisions provide semantic supervision for what the policy
should execute, while its effective corrections and subsequent student continuations provide action-level
supervision for recovery and handoff. The student also learns from its own effective execution, allowing
locally useful experience to contribute even when the overall episode fails. On ten selected RoboDojo tasks,
ESI achieves a mean Score of 62.03 and a mean success rate of 49.41% using an LLM–VLA agent without
generating task-specific control code. On both metrics, it outperforms the published reference results of all
hybrid embodied agents, including the Agent+WAM system GO-3 Nano Preview (47.27 Score and 36.60%
success), as well as all compared code-based methods.

1 Introduction
Robotics is entering a stage in which strong general-purpose reasoning models can participate directly in the
control loop. They can interpret unfamiliar instructions, identify relevant objects, reason about intermediate
goals, and revise a plan after observing a failure. At the same time, VLA policies provide a powerful substrate
for generating continuous robot actions from images, language, and proprioception [1, 2]. Agentic systems
that combine these capabilities are a compelling route toward more general robots: a reasoning model decides
what should happen next, and an action model provides the motor behavior needed to make it happen [3–6].
The central challenge is making this division of labor effective. Both components may be capable in
isolation, yet their composition can fail at the interface. A task-finetuned VLA may recognize a familiar
scene and reproduce a familiar motion while underusing the teacher’s more specific instruction. Related work
identifies visual shortcuts that allow scene information to dominate language constraints [7]. A teacher may
correctly diagnose a bad approach, adjust the gripper, and restore a promising configuration, only for the
student to undo that correction when control returns. These failures expose two closely related gaps: an intent

Consider an illustrative packing task. The teacher selects the red object and asks the left arm to grasp
it. The student instead approaches another object, or closes its gripper before reaching a suitable contact
configuration. The teacher corrects the approach and gives control back. If the student has never learned to
act from this corrected state under the intended subtask, it may repeat the original motion or abandon the
target. The immediate problem can be solved with more teacher control, but the same coordination failure
may recur in the next episode. A system that only intervenes repeatedly spends reasoning effort without
necessarily improving its underlying action capability.
Our premise is that these interactions are valuable learning data. They identify where the deployed
policy diverges from a selected goal, show how that divergence can be corrected, and expose the states from
which execution must resume. We therefore treat the reasoning–action interface as a target of collaborative
post-training. The teacher remains an online planner, reviewer, and source of corrections. Their joint
experience updates the teacher’s guidance, the shared skill vocabulary, and the student policy so that future
cooperation becomes more effective. This creates a path from episodic assistance to persistent capability.

This motivates a shift from model-level to system-level post-training. In a modular embodied agent,
decision guidance, the language interface, and motor behavior jointly determine what the system can
accomplish. ESI makes their cooperation a learning target: interaction supplies evidence for updating each
component, and their joint deployment determines the experience collected next. Through this process, the
LLM’s scene understanding and corrective judgments become supervision for persistent action capability.
Web-to-robot knowledge transfer [8] and language-guided policy distillation [9] illustrate how foundation
models can support embodied learning. ESI provides a pathway for converting advances in general-purpose
foundation models into executable embodied capabilities. This pathway has two stages. During deployment,
the LLM contributes scene understanding, planning, and corrective decisions to the current agent. Through
the flywheel, effective interaction becomes supervision that updates the action policy and guidance for
subsequent rounds. A foundation model thus participates both in performing tasks and in producing the
experience from which the embodied system learns.
We instantiate this idea in ESI (Embodied Self-Improvement), a configure-once flywheel built on the
open-source GPT-as-Policy system [6] (Figure 1). During collection, the teacher selects a structured subtask,
the VLA proposes an action chunk, and the teacher accepts useful motion or applies a correction. The
resulting observations, instructions, reasoning, executed actions, and feedback support round-level updates of
decision guidance, the shared skill catalog, and policy parameters. These components are deployed together
in the next round, while the LLM’s weights remain fixed.
The link from interaction to policy improvement is the selection of locally effective behavior. ESI
retains successful subtasks from unsuccessful episodes and connects teacher corrections with valid student
continuations on the executed timeline. Training under the corresponding local intent teaches the policy
both what operation to perform and how to continue from states reached during collaboration. Episode-level
review complements this action supervision by refining the teacher’s guidance and consolidating recurring
custom operations into reusable skills.
On the selected RoboDojo tasks, ESI scores higher than both individual component references on all ten
tasks and exceeds both in success rate on nine. For packing objects into a box, ESI completes ten of twenty
cases, against reported success rates of 0% for GPT-6-Astra and 2.67% for 𝜋0.5. Section 6 reports the full
comparison.
This report develops three main contributions:
• System-level post-training for modular embodied agents. We make the cooperation between
independently pretrained reasoning and action models a target of adaptation, updating decision
guidance, the shared skill interface, and motor execution through environmental interaction.
• Interaction-grounded embodied distillation. We connect semantic intent and corrective behavior to
VLA learning through a shared control and supervision interface. The pipeline retains successful student
behavior, effective corrections, and their continuations, including useful subtasks from unsuccessful
episodes.
• A framework for automated embodied self-improvement. We automate experience-driven updates
of decision prompts, a shared skill catalog, and VLA parameters, and return the updated components to
the next deployment round.
2 Related Work
2.1 Reasoning models and language-conditioned robot policies
SayCan grounds language-model planning in robot skills and their affordances [3], while Code as Policies
uses generated programs to connect language instructions with perception and control APIs [10]. Hi Robot
uses a hierarchical VLM–VLA architecture to interpret complex requests and situated feedback before

executing the selected subtask [4]. Gemini Robotics 1.5 develops an agentic architecture linking embodied
reasoning with action generation [5]. More recently, GPT-as-Policy studies direct and hybrid uses of a
general-purpose reasoning model across embodied tasks [6]. These systems motivate a compositional view of
robot intelligence. Our flywheel uses the experience produced by this composition to improve subsequent
collaboration.
RT-2 transfers knowledge from web-scale vision–language pretraining into robot control through joint
training on web and robot data [8]. The 𝜋0 family combines pretrained visual-language representations with
flow-based action generation [1]. 𝜋0.5 extends this direction through heterogeneous training that includes
semantic subtask information [2]. LangForce studies visual shortcuts that weaken instruction following and
explicitly models the contribution of language to action selection [7]. Such capabilities make a common
language interface attractive, while task-specific post-training offers a practical way to connect a deployed
teacher’s instruction distribution to the student’s motor behavior. Our focus is the interaction data generated
by that particular teacher–student pairing.
2.2 Learning from language guidance, deployment, and corrections
Scaling Up and Distilling Down uses language-model-guided planning and robot motion planners to generate
language-labeled trajectories, then distills them into a language-conditioned visuomotor policy [9]. This
connects language-guided data generation to persistent motor capability. ESI draws its training experience
from online teacher–student collaboration, including effective interventions and student continuations.
Dataset aggregation makes the learner’s visited states central to imitation learning [11]. Sirius learns
from autonomous execution and human interventions using weighted behavioral cloning [12]. Yell At Your
Robot uses human language corrections to improve a hierarchical robot’s high-level policy [13]. RECAP
combines demonstrations, autonomous experience, and expert interventions to improve VLA policies through
advantage-conditioned learning [14]. Our system follows the broader principle that deployment should
produce new learning opportunities. It uses a multimodal teacher to supply task decisions and corrections,
and supervised flow-matching updates to absorb eligible mixed-control behavior. The shared subtask interface
connects those updates to the teacher’s intended operation.
2.3 Experience-driven adaptation of modular agents
Reflexion uses verbal feedback and episodic memory to improve subsequent decisions while keeping the
language model’s weights fixed [15]. Voyager accumulates reusable executable skills in Minecraft and refines
programs using environmental feedback [16]. These works illustrate how interaction can improve an agent
through its guidance and retained skills. In ESI, round-level review updates decision prompts and the shared
instruction catalog alongside VLA post-training, connecting adaptation of the agent’s context to adaptation of
its motor policy.
DSPy represents language-model programs as composable modules and optimizes their pipelines against
a task metric [17]. GEPA uses reflection on execution traces to propose and evaluate prompt revisions [18].
These approaches make the configuration of a modular system an explicit optimization target. ESI brings
that perspective into an embodied learning loop, where prompts, the shared skill interface, and motor-policy
parameters adapt through environmental interaction.
FastHarness uses accumulated experience to schedule reviews of a frozen VLA, retaining recovery
knowledge while reducing unnecessary supervision [19]. CaPtain studies post-training of code-as-policy
agents through supervised learning and multi-turn reinforcement learning [20]. Together, these directions
illustrate several places where an embodied agent can improve: its supervision strategy, its reasoning policy,
and its motor policy. Our framework centers on learning the cooperation between an online reasoning teacher
and a trainable VLA. Review scheduling and reasoning-model adaptation can be integrated as complementary
branches.

3 ESI: System-Level Post-Training for Embodied Agents
3.1 Three coupled adaptation targets
ESI treats the deployed agent as a composition A𝑟= (T; 𝑃𝑟, 𝑆𝑟, 𝜃𝑟) at collection round 𝑟. Here T is a fixed
pretrained language model, 𝑃𝑟contains its system and task guidance, 𝑆𝑟defines the shared skill catalog and
argument schema, and 𝜃𝑟contains the VLA parameters. Environmental interaction yields an experience
buffer B𝑟. The flywheel uses this shared evidence to update the prompt, skill catalog, and policy for the next
collection round.
These objects address different sources of coordination failure. Prompt evolution changes how the teacher
plans, reviews action, and responds to difficulty. Skill evolution turns recurring operations into reusable
descriptions that make the teacher’s intent explicit. Policy learning changes how the robot realizes that intent
from the states encountered during collaboration. The skill catalog defines the shared language used by a
single VLA policy; each entry describes an operation rather than a separately trained controller.
The coupling is carried by subsequent interaction: changes in guidance and skill descriptions alter the
operations collected, while policy updates change which operations still require teacher intervention.
3.2 Learning the interface between reasoning and action
Let 𝑔denote the episode-level instruction, 𝑜𝑡the available observation including images and robot state, and
ℎ𝑡the interaction history. A multimodal teacher T uses this context to select a local intent and a structured
subtask 𝑧𝑡. The student policy 𝜋𝜃maps the observation and a rendered language condition 𝑐𝑡to an action
chunk. The teacher then decides which prefix to execute or how to correct it.
The two models communicate through language, action proposals, and environmental feedback. Having
compatible input and output formats makes this interaction possible, but reliable cooperation also requires
behavioral alignment. We focus on two properties:
Intent following. The student should translate the teacher’s selected object, acting arm, destination, relation,
and orientation into appropriate motion. A meaningful subtask instruction should change behavior when the
current goal changes.
Continuation after intervention. The student should preserve useful changes made by the teacher and
continue toward the active goal. A correction that improves contact geometry or object arrangement should
become a viable starting point for subsequent student execution.
These properties are coupled. The same visual state may support several plausible actions, so a handoff
requires both a recoverable physical configuration and an unambiguous goal. Conversely, a correct instruction
has limited value when the student cannot act from the state produced by the preceding correction. The
learning unit is therefore the combination of observation, intended operation, and effective executed behavior.
3.3 System-level post-training and embodied knowledge transfer
We interpret the transfer from teacher-guided interaction to policy learning as interaction-grounded embodied
distillation. It connects the teacher’s embodied knowledge to persistent policy capability at two complementary
levels.
Semantic-level transfer. The LLM applies its general knowledge to interpret a scene, identify relevant
objects and relations, and select the next operation. A structured subtask turns this reasoning into an explicit
local goal. For example, within a packing task, “Left arm: Grasp red block” identifies what the current action
segment should accomplish. Training on effective behavior under this condition teaches the VLA to realize
the selected intent. Local supervision connects each action segment to its immediate purpose, while the
teacher continues to organize those operations into the broader task.

ESI uses this connection to carry experience from online cooperation into subsequent policy execution.
3.4 From online feedback to round-level updates
ESI operates at two complementary levels. Within an episode, the teacher and VLA use fresh observations to
plan, review, correct, and continue execution. Across collection rounds, the system consolidates experience
into updated prompts, skills, and policy parameters. Episode summaries also provide task memory for
subsequent attempts. Figure 2 expands the current-round agent, its interaction record, and the three update
paths that form the next-round agent.

4 Online Collaboration and Experience Collection
4.1 A shared subtask language
The teacher expresses the current local goal through a shared subtask interface. Each subtask identifies
the operation, acting arm, manipulated object, and relevant target or orientation. The system renders this
representation as a concise instruction appended to the original task and supplies it to the VLA. We refer to
this task-plus-local-instruction input as subtask conditioning.
Illustrative student input
Put the red block in the tray.
Current subtask:
Left arm:
Grasp red block.
The same expression of local intent is used both to request actions online and to train the policy on the
corresponding execution experience. The catalog provides reusable operations such as grasping, placing,
and rotating, while a custom option expresses behavior beyond its current vocabulary. Recurring custom
operations provide evidence for subsequent skill expansion. Appendix A gives the interface fields and
representative instructions.
4.2 Plan, propose, review, and execute
Each decision cycle begins with the current observation and task context. The teacher selects a subtask,
and the VLA proposes an action chunk conditioned on the observation and the resulting instruction. The
teacher reviews which actions to execute using images, robot state, recent feedback, and the proposal’s
forward-kinematics trajectory.
The teacher can accept a useful segment, correct its motion or gripper behavior, or directly specify a
recovery motion. Execution produces fresh observations and feedback for the next planning and review cycle.
The VLA supplies continuous actions, while the teacher adapts the control decision to the intended operation
and observed task progress.
Teacher corrections and student continuations together form the executed trajectory. ESI records these
handoffs so that subsequent learning can use both the states reached through teacher recovery and the student’s
behavior after control returns.
4.3 A shared record of experience
The shared experience record links observations, local instructions, proposed actions, teacher reasoning,
control sources, executed actions, feedback, and outcomes. Episode summaries organize the difficulties
encountered and the strategies attempted, and can serve as task memory for subsequent attempts.
This record supports three complementary updates. Summaries and detailed interaction evidence inform
decision guidance; recurring custom operations inform skill consolidation; and executed trajectories with
local outcome annotations provide policy supervision. The next section describes how ESI uses these sources
to update the agent jointly across rounds.
5 Learning from Interaction: Prompt, Skill, and Policy Updates
After each collection round, ESI updates decision guidance, the shared skill catalog, and the action policy from
the same experience record. Prompt updates shape planning and review, skill updates shape the expression of
local intent, and policy updates learn to realize that intent from effective execution. The updated components
enter the next interaction round together and generate new evidence for learning.

5.1 Experience-driven prompt evolution
Reflective prompt optimization uses execution traces to turn observed difficulties into revised instructions [18].
ESI reviews episode summaries, outcomes, and teacher-control histories to identify recurring manipulation
and recovery difficulties. It then examines relevant reasoning records, previous guidance, and selected images
to connect those difficulties to the surrounding decisions and execution conditions.
The resulting revisions update reusable manipulation guidance, recovery rules, and task-specific strategies.
An independent reviewer checks whether the proposals are supported by the recorded evidence and preserve
productive existing behavior. Candidates requiring further evidence return for refinement; accepted guidance
is automatically published for the next collection round. This process updates the teacher’s decision context
while keeping the LLM’s weights fixed.
Prompt evolution turns difficulties encountered in individual interactions into reusable guidance for later
attempts. Appendix B.1 details the review criteria and a recorded proposal, including the evidence used to
determine whether it should be published.
5.2 From custom operations to reusable skills
The shared skill catalog evolves from operations that arise during collection. When the teacher frequently
uses custom descriptions, the system reviews the associated instructions, reasoning, images, and execution
context to identify recurring behavior that can be expressed through a stable, reusable skill description.
Suitable operations are consolidated into the catalog for subsequent rounds. This gives the teacher a
consistent way to request an operation that previously required ad hoc wording and gives the VLA a reusable
language condition for that intent. The custom option remains available for behavior beyond the revised
catalog, exposing further opportunities for expansion.
Prompt evolution changes how the teacher decides and reviews; skill evolution changes how the selected
operation is conveyed to the policy. Together, they shape the instruction distribution and interaction experience
available for policy learning in subsequent rounds.
5.3 Constructing policy supervision from interaction
Aligning local intent with executed behavior. The pipeline aligns executed actions with their preceding
observations and corresponding local intent. An LLM annotator uses the textual interaction record and post-
action feedback to assess local outcomes and instruction–action consistency. Annotation preserves instructions
supplied online and assigns local instructions when collection precedes online subtask conditioning. The
resulting examples pair observations and intended operations with the motion actually executed. Reasoning
and outcome evidence guide annotation and selection; the VLA receives images, robot state, and the task
instruction with its applicable subtask condition.
Selecting locally effective behavior. Selection follows local effectiveness and consistency with the intended
operation. ESI retains productive student actions, effective teacher corrections, and preparatory actions whose
progress is supported by subsequent feedback. Failed, uncertain, or inconsistent segments are excluded
from direct action supervision. Selection is independent of the episode’s final outcome: a successful grasp,
transport, or correction can remain useful even if a later operation prevents task completion.
Connecting corrections and continuations. Retained execution is organized into temporally continuous
action windows. A switch between student and teacher control does not itself break a window, allowing a
correction and the student’s subsequent continuation to contribute jointly to supervision. These examples
teach both how useful corrected states are reached and how execution continues from them. We refer to
this action-level mechanism as intervention-to-policy distillation. Supervision follows the contiguous valid
portion of each window and stops when subsequent actions are no longer eligible.

5.4 Policy updates with experience replay
Each training round incorporates new interaction while retaining experience from earlier rounds. Let 𝜇𝑟
denote the sampling distribution over eligible training windows collected in round 𝑟, and let 𝑞𝑟denote the
distribution used for policy training after that round. The replay rule is recursive:

𝑞𝑟= 𝛼𝜇𝑟+ (1 −𝛼)𝑞𝑟−1,

We use 𝛼= 0.6: the current round receives 60% of the sampling mass and the recursively organized history
receives 40%. Earlier rounds retain their relative proportions within that history. The rule applies to any
number of collection rounds, allowing new experience to enter the training distribution without discarding
previously acquired behavior. Within each round, sampling balances tasks and cases and gives greater weight
to teacher-controlled window starts. Appendix B.6 details the resulting round weights and the within-round
sampling procedure.
We instantiate the student with a 𝜋0.5 policy [2] using the OpenPI/JAX implementation1 and retain its
native flow-matching objective [1, 21]. Each round initializes from the preceding policy and updates the
full VLA on the replay distribution. Action loss is applied only to the retained valid execution within each
window; invalid and padded positions are excluded. Appendix B.7–B.8 gives the full objective and training
settings.

This collect–update–redeploy cycle repeats across rounds, with task memory providing episode-level
context. It connects online interaction, experience consolidation, and joint deployment into a continuing
process of system-level improvement.

6 Experiments
6.1 Experimental setup
System instantiation. The current system pairs GPT-6 Astra with a RoboDojo-adapted 𝜋0.5 policy in
simulated dual-arm ARX X5 manipulation. The student consumes a high camera view, two wrist-camera
views, robot state, and language. Its action representation contains six joint targets and one gripper command
per arm. The teacher operates through the GPT-as-Policy execution tools and reviews student proposals
within the same episode context [6].
RoboDojo provides a diverse manipulation environment [22]. The project’s collection suite selects ten tasks
involving packing, semantic sorting, number arrangement, sequential manipulation, and language-conditioned
object organization. Its three-round configuration specifies 200 episodes per round, with cumulative pools of
200, 400, and 600 episodes. The three-round policy-learning configuration underlying the reported evaluation
uses a fixed prompt and skill catalog across its VLA updates. The first round bootstraps subtask labels
offline; subsequent rounds use online subtask conditioning and preserve those conditions during annotation.
Evaluation uses the task and case selection described below.
Task and case selection. We adapt the ten-task suite of GPT-as-Policy [6], replacing several easier tasks with
more demanding manipulation tasks. The main evaluation schedules ten tasks with twenty cases each, totaling
200 cases. Cases follow benchmark order: tasks with standard and random splits contribute equally from
each split (ten each here), while single-list tasks use the first twenty cases. The component study follows the
same balanced sampling rule with ten cases per task, totaling 100 cases. Aggregation details are consolidated
in Appendix C.
Metrics and comparison. Score measures task progress on a 0–100 scale, and success rate (SR) measures
full task completion, following RoboDojo [22]. For each system, we report the arithmetic mean of their ten
task-level Scores and SRs. The reference values come from the RoboDojo leaderboard2 for the same task
names, using each entry’s reported case set. The first three model columns in Table 1 compare the deployed
ESI system with its standalone components.
Matched system baseline. We additionally evaluate Baseline (task-only), a GPT-as-Policy agent with the
original RoboDojo-adapted VLA, task-only policy conditioning, and baseline decision guidance. ESI uses the
round-three checkpoint, structured subtask conditioning, and task guidance. Both use GPT-6-Astra with xhigh
reasoning, the same environment code and matched layouts and scene seeds, the same motion executor, and
identical native task step budgets. Each evaluation uses a fixed agent configuration and disables cross-episode
task memory. The comparison measures the deployed systems as a whole; execution configuration is
documented in Appendix C. These layouts are drawn from the flywheel’s collection suite.
Both agents use the same ten-task, 200-case evaluation schedule. Performance uses task means to match
the leaderboard table; control statistics use episode means of recorded teacher actions. A teacher intervention
decision is an action-review decision that results in at least one executed teacher correction (edit or EEF
action); it is not a count of LLM calls. Teacher-executed steps count the resulting environment actions. Tables
abbreviate the decision metric as “Interventions. The shared-success comparison uses the cases completed by
both agents.
6.2 Overall task performance
Overall performance. Averaged equally across the ten selected tasks, ESI reaches 62.03 Score and 49.41%

control allocation toward the action policy.
The same successful cases. The shared-success columns of Table 2 compares the 41 cases completed by

Intervention frequency. Teacher intervention decisions with nonzero executed edit or EEF actions decrease

6.7 Intent following under reordered operations
We test whether the agent can reorganize learned operations according to language-specified intent, rather
than replay a fixed execution sequence associated with a familiar scene. The initial scene and final goal
remain unchanged, while the requested order of the placements varies.
We evaluate three requested operation orders in one fixed initial scene of arrange_largest_number,
with three runs per agent. The pad-filling orders are: 1→2→3→4, 2→3→1→4, and 4→3→2→1, with
pads numbered from left to right. The target arrangement remains 8720. Video inspection verifies adherence
to the requested order; native scoring separately measures task completion. ESI completes the task under all
three requested orders, while Baseline completes it under two (Table 4).
Figure 6 makes the dependence of execution on intent visible. Its rows share the same scene and target
arrangement, while each column advances one requested placement. ESI first places digit 8, 7, or 0 when
the requested order begins with pad 1, 2, or 4, respectively, and continues with the remaining placements
in the specified order. These sequences support compositional intent following: familiar local operations
can be selected and reordered through language, extending their use beyond one scene-associated execution
sequence.
Across these runs, mean teacher intervention decisions fall from 36.00 to 8.00 and teacher-executed steps
from 294.33 to 71.33, reductions of 77.78% and 75.76%, respectively. Teacher step share decreases from
33.16% to 11.74%. Together with the observed order adherence, reduced direct teacher control supports
the view that the adapted VLA carries more of the execution needed to realize changing local intentions.
This illustrates the behavioral role of the shared LLM–VLA interface: the teacher organizes the task through
local instructions, and the policy supplies the corresponding motion. The order study complements the
held-out-task example by changing the intended sequence rather than the task scene. Appendix C includes
per-order outcomes and token measurements under the shared subtask-conditioning control flow.

7.1 Research directions
Foundation-model upgrades. This perspective opens a second axis of improvement alongside additional
robot experience: upgrading the reasoning foundation model. A more capable teacher can supply new
operation decompositions, richer scene interpretations, and useful recovery strategies for the system to
explore and absorb. The shared interface and automated learning loop provide the means to translate
these contributions into policy capability. The research opportunity is to make progress in general-purpose
intelligence a continuing source of progress in robot execution.
The framework supports examining both immediate and persistent benefits of a stronger teacher. Immediate
benefits appear when the teacher is changed during deployment. Persistent benefits can be studied by collecting
experience with different teachers, training the resulting students under matched budgets, and evaluating those
16


ESI / TECHNICAL REPORT
students with one common teacher. This separates improvements in online guidance from the capabilities
retained through learning and offers a practical way to investigate foundation-model-driven embodied learning.
Interaction-driven learning. The teacher encounters the student’s current weaknesses during deployment.
As the policy learns an operation, subsequent interaction can expose later-stage failures or more demanding
combinations of skills. This creates an opportunity to study curricula driven by the evolving boundary of
the agent’s capability. Experience-based collection priorities could direct additional effort toward recurring
difficulties and useful recoveries.
Future experiments can hold the teacher’s instruction stream fixed to isolate policy-level intent following,
or start policies from identical corrected states to examine continuation after intervention.
A correction can contribute both to the current episode and to future policy capability. This motivates
tracking task performance together with intervention burden and interaction cost across rounds. Policy
adaptation and review scheduling offer complementary research directions: one changes the actions the
student proposes, while the other determines when additional reasoning is most useful.
An extensible framework. The prompt, skill interface, and policy provide distinct points for extending ESI.
Different reasoning models can review experience, alternative skill representations can express intent, and
other action models or training objectives can absorb effective behavior. Their common connection is the
interaction record, which links decisions to actions and outcomes. The framework supports studying how
these choices affect the resulting embodied agent.

B Update Procedures and Policy Training Details
This appendix specifies the review procedure and data-processing rules used by the updates in Section 5,
followed by the reported policy-training configurations.
B.1 Prompt review and a recorded proposal
An independent reviewer checks candidate guidance against the cited episode summaries, reasoning, feedback,
and images, including successful continuations that a new rule should preserve. The review examines whether
the evidence supports the proposed failure mechanism, whether the trigger can be observed online, and
whether the revision preserves task goals and the execution protocol. It uses historical interaction records
without launching additional rollouts. Revisions that need further evidence return for refinement. The
complete guidance bundle is automatically published after it passes review and replaces the corresponding
prompt content in the next collection round.

B.2 Annotation and local eligibility
Each executed command is aligned with its preceding observation. An LLM annotator uses the textual
interaction record and post-action feedback to label local outcomes. For the first collection round, which
precedes online subtask conditioning, offline annotation groups decisions into operation attempts and assigns
subtask instructions. For later rounds, annotation preserves the original online instructions and executed
decision intervals, adding outcome and instruction–action consistency labels.
Supported successful execution is retained for both student actions and teacher corrections, including
preparatory actions whose useful progress is verified by subsequent feedback. Failed, uncertain, or inconsistent
segments are excluded from direct action supervision. Eligibility is independent of the episode’s final outcome:
a completed grasp and transport can be retained even if a later placement fails, while an unsuccessful attempt
within a successful episode can be excluded. Across the three collection rounds, 47.9–49.7% of candidate
training-window starts come from episodes that ultimately fail to complete the task.
B.3 Window construction and validity masks
Eligible execution is organized into contiguous intervals, from which the pipeline extracts overlapping action
windows of horizon 𝐻= 50. A change between student control and teacher correction does not break an
interval. Full-length windows provide the main training samples. Additional tail windows are retained when
teacher control occurs near the end of a valid interval, provided at least 15 valid action steps remain.
For a selected start 𝑡, let 𝐿𝑡≤𝐻be the length of its contiguous valid prefix. The temporal mask 𝑚𝑡,𝑘is
one for 𝑘< 𝐿𝑡and zero otherwise. Supervision stops at the first ineligible position or episode end and does
not resume if later positions become eligible again. Invalid and padded positions therefore receive no direct
action-loss supervision.
