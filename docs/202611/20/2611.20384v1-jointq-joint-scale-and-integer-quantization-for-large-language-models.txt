JointQ: Joint Scale-and-Integer Quantization
for Large Language Models
Keiji Kimura*
Yuma Ichikawa†
Abstract
GPTQ is the de facto standard solver for layer-wise post-training quantization
(PTQ) of large language models, yet the solver itself has received less attention
than weight preprocessing and cross-layer objectives. We target a bottleneck of
GPTQ: its greedy procedure optimizes continuous group scales separately from
the assignment of discrete integers. Evaluating each integer candidate with its
closed-form optimal scale has been used for per-channel quantization; JointQ
extends it to group-wise quantization, where a row’s scales are coupled through
the Hessian, and applies it to refine the GPTQ solution. Each local-search move
changes one integer and recomputes its group’s optimal scale in closed form,
accounting for the other groups through the full Hessian; all scales are periodically
re-solved jointly. The search starts from a row-wise selection among scale-refitted
candidates including the GPTQ solution, so unregularized JointQ never has a
larger layer-wise calibration error than GPTQ. JointQ preserves the GPTQ output
format and is thus a drop-in replacement. Across four models and four settings,
JointQ improves six-task average zero-shot accuracy over vanilla GPTQ in 14 of
16 comparisons and perplexity in 12 of 16. The mean accuracy gain increases
from 0.0044 in W4G128 to 0.1559 in W3 per-channel, and JointQ also improves
average accuracy over GPTQ with MSE-based scale search and activation ordering
in all eight per-channel comparisons, though rarely in G128. An ablation shows
that JointQ’s diagonal Tikhonov regularization mitigates severe W3 per-channel
perplexity degradation, so the largest gains are not due to the local search alone.
Overall, JointQ’s primary empirical benefit is downstream accuracy preservation in
challenging low-bit and large-group regimes. Code is available in the OneComp
library at https://github.com/FujitsuResearch/OneCompression.

Introduction
The rapid scaling of transformer-based language models has made efficient deployment a
central challenge. Modern models now routinely contain billions to hundreds of billions
of parameters, placing substantial pressure on memory capacity, memory bandwidth,
and inference cost [Vaswani et al., 2017, Touvron et al., 2023, Dubey et al., 2024,

Yang et al., 2025]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is therefore a key technique for
practical inference: it compresses pretrained weights from 16-bit precision to 4, 3,
or even fewer bits without retraining, thereby reducing both model size and serving
cost [Gholami et al., 2022, Nagel et al., 2021]. Among PTQ methods, layer-wise
approaches are particularly attractive for large language models because each linear
layer can be quantized independently using only a small calibration set, while the
resulting model remains compatible with efficient low-bit inference kernels.
In this layer-wise setting, GPTQ [Frantar et al., 2023], built on Optimal Brain
Compression [Frantar et al., 2022], has become the de facto standard solver due to its
strong speed–quality trade-off: it quantizes weight columns greedily using second-order
activation statistics while compensating for the induced error in the remaining columns.
It is widely used both as a standalone quantizer and as the final projection step in more
elaborate PTQ pipelines [Arai and Ichikawa, 2025, Li et al., 2025, Lin and Wan, 2025,
Ichikawa et al., 2025]. Recent progress in LLM quantization has largely advanced
along two complementary directions. The first improves the geometry of weights and
activations before quantization, for example, through rotation-based preprocessing meth-
ods [Ashkboos et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2025, Tseng et al., 2024b,a, Chee et al., 2023, Lin
et al., 2024a, Sun et al., 2025] that mitigate outliers and make weight distributions more
quantization-friendly. The second modifies the quantization objective to better account
for calibration mismatch and error propagation, for example, through asymmetric cali-
bration, output-aware formulations, or submodule-level correction [Arai and Ichikawa,
2025, Li et al., 2025, Lin and Wan, 2025, Ichikawa et al., 2025]. Although these ad-
vances have substantially improved PTQ performance, they mainly refine the context
in which quantization is performed while leaving the core projector—the optimization
step that maps a real-valued layer back to a low-bit group-wise quantized representation
in the standard GPTQ format—largely unchanged.
We argue that this bottleneck becomes especially pronounced in challenging quan-
tization regimes. In group-wise scalar quantization, the final quantized weights are
governed by two tightly coupled components: continuous group scales and discrete
integer assignments. The scales determine the spacing of the quantization grid, while
the integers select the grid points. Standard GPTQ-style quantization optimizes these
two components separately: it first sets the scales using a heuristic rule and then greed-
ily assigns integers under the resulting fixed grid. Although this decoupling is often
sufficient when the grid is dense or groups are small, it becomes increasingly restrictive
in more challenging settings, such as low-bit quantization or large-group schemes like
per-channel quantization, where the choice of scale significantly impacts reconstruction
quality.
Joint treatments of the two components have so far been formulated mainly for a
single scale per layer or per output channel. COMQ [Zhang et al., 2025] alternates be-
tween integer updates under a fixed scale and closed-form scale updates. Beacon [Zhang
and Saab, 2026] eliminates the scale analytically, obtaining a reduced objective that
evaluates every integer candidate with its optimal scale, and searches over the integers
under a cosine criterion derived from it. Neither addresses group-wise quantization, in
which the scales of a row are coupled through the off-diagonal blocks of the Hessian,
and both construct their integers independently of GPTQ. PiSO [Amboage et al., 2026]
computes optimal scales for round-to-nearest assignment and interleaves them with
2


GPTQ, but the integers are then still assigned under fixed scales.
To address these gaps, we propose JointQ, a drop-in replacement for GPTQ that
jointly optimizes scales and integer assignments while preserving the exact deployment
format. JointQ rests on two design choices. First, its local search extends scale-
eliminated integer search to the group-wise setting. Each candidate move modifies a
single integer assignment and recomputes the optimal scale of the affected group in
closed form, while accounting for the other groups through the full Hessian, so that the
cross-group coupling enters the score of every move; after each pass over the groups,
the search also re-solves all group scales of selected rows jointly with the integers fixed.
Second, JointQ refines the GPTQ solution rather than replacing it. A GPTQ solution
and a clipping-based solution are each given jointly refitted group scales, the candidate
with the lower layer-local objective is selected independently for each output row, and
the local search starts from this selection. Because the GPTQ solution is in the candidate
pool, unregularized JointQ never has a larger layer-wise calibration error than GPTQ. In
the per-channel case, the move score coincides with Beacon’s reduced objective, so the
distinction from such methods there lies in the second choice and in the regularization
of Section 3.7. The resulting representation remains fully compatible with existing
inference kernels and GPTQ-style checkpoints.
Empirically, across four models and four quantization configurations, JointQ im-
proves six-task average zero-shot accuracy over vanilla GPTQ in 14 of 16 comparisons
and perplexity in 12 of 16. The benefit grows with the difficulty of the quantization
regime: averaged over the models, the accuracy gain increases from 0.0044 in W4G128
to 0.1559 in W3 per-channel. Against GPTQ with MSE-based scale search and activa-
tion ordering, JointQ wins all eight per-channel accuracy comparisons, with the clearest
gains appearing in W3 per-channel. In G128, by contrast, the value of JointQ when
its GPTQ candidate uses the same enhanced heuristics is model dependent; under the
actorder-free evaluation condition, JointQ remains stronger than both vanilla and MSE-
only GPTQ in most comparisons. A staged ablation separates the unregularized JointQ
pipeline, consisting of the warm start and the local search, from the full stabilization
policy: by construction, the unregularized pipeline never increases layer-wise calibration
output error relative to GPTQ, and it reduces this error in every model, setting, and layer
type shown, but the magnitude of this reduction does not predict downstream improve-
ment. These findings are consistent with the mechanism exposed by our separation
result, but the theory does not guarantee downstream-metric improvement. Perplexity
also remains model dependent. We do not compare with COMQ, Beacon, or PiSO, so
the experiments do not isolate the source of the per-channel gains, which are the largest
(Section 6). We therefore identify downstream accuracy preservation in challenging
low-bit and large-group regimes, rather than uniform dominance on every metric, as the
principal empirical benefit. The gains require no change to the standard GPTQ output
format.

Although OBQ provides closed-form optimal updates, it is too expensive for billion-

tion across output channels. GPTQ [Frantar et al., 2023] makes this framework practical
through three key modifications. First, instead of following the greedy ordering induced

blocks of size B and applies them lazily, which raises the compute-to-memory ratio

complement update above, which accumulates numerical errors at billion-parameter
scale, it precomputes the required rows of the updated inverse Hessians from a single
Cholesky factorization of H−1, improving numerical stability.
Despite its effectiveness, GPTQ does not jointly optimize the continuous group
scales {st} and the discrete integer assignments {qj}. Each group scale is set by Eq. (5)
before any integer of the group is assigned, and once the greedy column-wise pass
fixes the integer assignments, the scales are not revisited; Appendix E describes at
which point of the pass each scale is computed. This decoupling is often harmless
when the quantization grid is sufficiently dense, such as at higher bit-widths or with
small groups. However, it becomes much more restrictive in low-bit or per-channel
settings, where each group contains only a few available grid points and scale selection
significantly affects reconstruction quality. The next section introduces JointQ, which
directly addresses this limitation.
3
JointQ
This section introduces JointQ. We begin by formulating the coupled row-wise opti-
mization problem and showing that the continuous scale variables can be eliminated
analytically, yielding a reduced discrete reference objective. We then present the
6


group-conditional surrogate used by the practical algorithm, derive the corresponding
closed-form best-response scale, and decompose the resulting conditional error into
a direction gap and a scale gap. Next, we provide an illustrative separation example
for a single-group instance, showing that frozen-scale quantization can be fundamen-
tally suboptimal in low-bit regimes. Finally, we describe the local search procedure,
regularization strategy, and implementation details.

Calibration-set Sensitivity
We evaluate calibration-set sensitivity on Llama-3-8B in W3G128 and W4G128 using
8–128 length-2048 calibration sequences and 20 paired seeds per sequence count. Full
results are reported in Appendix G.
In W3G128, JointQ has lower PPL variance than GPTQ⋆at every sequence count;

Related Work
Layer-wise solvers.
Recent advances in post-training quantization (PTQ) for large
language models have been shaped largely by layer-wise reconstruction objectives and
second-order approximations. OBQ [Frantar et al., 2022] quantizes weights sequen-
tially using second-order updates, and GPTQ [Frantar et al., 2023] extends this line
to billion-parameter transformers. CDQuant [Nair and Suggala, 2024] also minimizes
reconstruction error through coordinate descent, but it assumes fixed scales throughout
the optimization.
Joint optimization of scales and integers.
The works closest to JointQ treat the scales
and the integers of a standard scalar format as joint variables of the layer-wise objective.
COMQ [Zhang et al., 2025] applies coordinate descent to both: it updates the integers
one coordinate at a time with the scale held fixed and then updates the scale in closed
form, so each integer update is evaluated under a frozen scale, the setting of Corollary 3.4.
Beacon [Zhang and Saab, 2026] instead substitutes the closed-form optimal scale into
the per-channel objective, which reduces the problem to maximizing the absolute value
of the cosine of the angle between wX and qX, where q is the integer vector. Its
search then drops the absolute value: it assigns the integers by a greedy sequential pass
followed by cyclic coordinate updates that maximize the signed cosine, and computes
the scale in closed form only at the end. In the per-channel case (T = 1), JointQ scores
its integer moves by this reduced objective rather than by the signed criterion: with
r0 = w, the captured energy in Eq. (13) is κ0(u) = ∥w∥2

JointQ differs from these methods in two respects. First, it targets group-wise
quantization, in which the T scales of a row are coupled through the off-diagonal blocks
of eH, whereas COMQ and Beacon are formulated with one scale per layer or per output
channel. A group-wise extension of COMQ would still evaluate each integer update
under frozen scales. A direct group-wise extension of Beacon would either treat the
groups independently, discarding the cross-group coupling, or score every candidate
move by the reduced objective F, which couples all T scales of the row (Theorem 3.1)
and makes each evaluation considerably more expensive. JointQ takes an intermediate
route. Its surrogate Lt re-optimizes only the scale of the modified group but retains the
full eH through the residual rt, so the cross-group coupling enters every move while
each move is still scored in O(1) time. Its block-coordinate passes revisit every group
and are interleaved with joint re-solves of all T scales. Second, JointQ refines the
GPTQ solution rather than replacing it: its warm start contains the error-compensated
GPTQ solution, and Proposition 3.7 guarantees that, without regularization, the output
never has a larger row-wise calibration error than GPTQ. In the per-channel case, the
Clip-Optimize candidate of the warm start is itself a Beacon-type search (Section 3.8).
We do not include COMQ or Beacon as baselines; Section 6 discusses what this leaves
open.
Scale selection and continuous parameters.
PiSO [Amboage et al., 2026] approaches
the same coupling from the scale side. Under round-to-nearest assignment, the integers
are piecewise constant in the scale, and PiSO computes the optimal channel-wise scale
exactly by sweeping these pieces. It extends the sweep to group-wise quantization by
treating the groups independently or sequentially in a single pass, and it interleaves
the sweep with GPTQ, which then assigns the integers under the fixed scales. JointQ
works in the opposite direction: it changes the integers after GPTQ and re-optimizes
the scales for the integers it selects. Two-Stage Grid Optimization [Kim et al., 2026]
refines group scales while keeping integer assignments fixed. JointQ also uses such
fixed-integer refinement: its warm start refits all group scales with the integers fixed, and
its local search periodically re-solves them jointly. Our contribution lies not in this refit
but in the group-wise coupled search and the GPTQ-anchored design described above.
EasyQuant [Wu et al., 2020], AWQ [Lin et al., 2024b], OmniQuant [Shao et al., 2024],
AffineQuant [Ma et al., 2024], and LRQ [Lee et al., 2024] optimize scales, clipping
thresholds, or related continuous transformations. JointQ differs from these methods
in that it does not introduce a new parameterization; instead, it jointly optimizes the
quantities already present in the standard GPTQ format.
Rounding under a fixed grid.
Another closely related direction seeks to improve
rounding while holding the quantization grid fixed. Representative methods include
AdaRound [Nagel et al., 2020], AdaQuant [Hubara et al., 2020], BRECQ [Li et al.,
2021], SignRound [Cheng et al., 2023], GuidedQuant [Kim et al., 2025], YAQA [Tseng
et al., 2025], VQRound [Zhou et al., 2026], and regularized successive-rounding [Cha
et al., 2026]. Rather than improving rounding under a fixed grid, JointQ addresses the
26


underlying discrete–continuous coupling between integer assignments and group scales.
Other quantization families.
Finally, several methods pursue alternative quantization
families altogether, including QuIP [Chee et al., 2023], AQLM [Egiazarian et al., 2024],
GPTVQ [van Baalen et al., 2024], and QTIP [Tseng et al., 2024b]. By contrast, JointQ
targets the standard group-wise uniform scalar quantization format and improves the
solver itself within this widely used deployment setting.
6
Conclusion
We introduced JointQ, a layer-wise PTQ method that improves quantization within the
standard GPTQ deployment format. The central idea is to address a limitation of standard
GPTQ-style quantization: continuous group scales and discrete integer assignments are
treated separately even though they are tightly coupled. JointQ addresses this mismatch
by extending scale-eliminated integer search, previously formulated for a single scale per
output channel, to group-wise quantization: each move updates an integer assignment
and recomputes the optimal scale of the affected group in closed form while accounting
for the other groups through the full Hessian. The search refines the GPTQ solution
rather than replacing it, so that its unregularized layer-wise calibration error never
exceeds that of GPTQ. The resulting weights retain the standard low-bit representation.
Our analysis provides a mechanism for why this joint treatment can matter when
bit-widths are low or groups are large, without claiming a downstream-metric guarantee.
Across four models and four quantization configurations, JointQ improves six-task
average zero-shot accuracy over GPTQ in 14 of 16 comparisons and perplexity in 12 of
16, with the mean accuracy gain increasing from 0.0044 in W4G128 to 0.1559 in W3
per-channel. A staged ablation shows that the unregularized JointQ pipeline reduces
layer-wise calibration output error in every case shown, as guaranteed by its construction,
while improving perplexity in 10 of 16 model–configuration pairs and average accuracy
in 11 of 16. Thus, better layer-local reconstruction alone does not ensure better end-
to-end performance. JointQ also improves average accuracy over GPTQ⋆in all eight
per-channel comparisons, although the additional value of JointQ (GPTQ⋆cand.) in
G128 is model dependent. The full stabilization policy further mitigates severe W3 per-
channel perplexity degradation and reduces calibration-seed sensitivity in the evaluated
low-sample setting. Consequently, the largest gains in the most challenging regimes
cannot be attributed to the local search alone. Perplexity remains model dependent, and
W2G32 remains far below BF16 despite substantial improvement over GPTQ. Overall,
the results support a focused conclusion: strengthening scale–integer optimization
helps preserve downstream accuracy when challenging quantization regimes expose the
limitations of a frozen grid, while retaining the deployment format practitioners already
use.
Limitations.
Our experiments evaluate JointQ as a complete pipeline. Because the
local search starts from a multi-candidate warm start that already refits all group scales
and selects candidates per output row, the present results do not separate the contributions
of the Clip-Optimize candidate, the global scale refit, the row-wise selection, and the
27


local search. Within the local search, the G128 results also do not separate the per-pass
joint scale re-solve from the integer moves. The thresholds (τlo, τhi) that select rows for
this re-solve were set by a brief preliminary tuning and have not been ablated, although
the per-channel results do not depend on them (Section 3.6). We have also not studied
the effect of the stopping parameters ωin and ωout or of the iteration cap. Finally, JointQ
(GPTQ⋆cand.) retains the Clip-Optimize candidate and therefore does not measure
the effect of a pure GPTQ⋆warm start. Quantifying these components, for example
by evaluating the warm start without the local search or with a single candidate, or the
local search without the joint re-solve, is left for future work.
We also do not compare JointQ with COMQ, Beacon, or PiSO [Zhang et al., 2025,
Zhang and Saab, 2026, Amboage et al., 2026]. In the per-channel settings, where
the gains over GPTQ are largest, the move score of JointQ coincides with Beacon’s
reduced objective, and the Clip-Optimize candidate is itself a Beacon-type search
(Section 3.8). The results therefore do not show whether JointQ improves on such
methods, or how much of its per-channel gain over GPTQ is due to the scale-eliminated
search rather than to the GPTQ candidate, the row-wise selection, or the regularization.
Likewise, we have not compared the group-wise local search with a variant that treats
the groups independently, so the benefit of retaining the cross-group coupling has not
been measured separately.
The regularization strengths λ were selected by a manual preliminary search on
Llama-3-8B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B using WikiText-2 perplexity and zero-shot
accuracy on ARC-c, ARC-e, PIQA, and WinoGrande, which are part of the evaluation
data that we report (Appendix D), whereas the baselines use their default settings. The
reported results for these three models may therefore be optimistically biased. Llama-2-
7B and the HellaSwag and BoolQ tasks, which were not used in the search, provide only
a partial check of how the values transfer. A selection protocol based on held-out data,
the effect of the exponent η, which was not tuned, and the choice of the retry threshold
and retry strength, including whether a different retry policy would help in W2G32,
remain to be studied.
JointQ is also considerably more expensive than GPTQ at quantization time. In the
four main settings, its quantization time is 2.63–15.71× that of GPTQ (Appendix H),
with the largest ratios on Qwen3-14B, and the ratio depends on the FP64 throughput of
the GPU (Section 4). This cost is incurred once, offline, and the quantized model has
the same format and hence the same inference cost as a GPTQ model, but it may matter
when many models or configurations must be quantized.

Enhanced GPTQ Heuristics
We denote by GPTQ⋆an enhanced GPTQ configuration that combines two independent
heuristics—activation ordering and MSE-based scale search—on top of the base GPTQ
algorithm described in Section 2.3.
Activation ordering (actorder).
The base GPTQ processes columns in the natural
order l = 0, 1, . . . , p −1. The actorder heuristic instead processes them in decreas-
ing order of the Hessian diagonal Hll, so that the most activation-sensitive columns
are quantized first, before the inverse Hessian accumulates error from less important
columns. Concretely, let π be a permutation such that Hπ(0),π(0) ≥Hπ(1),π(1) ≥
· · · ≥Hπ(p−1),π(p−1), and let P be the corresponding permutation matrix. GPTQ
is then applied to the permuted system W ′ = WP, H′ = P ⊤HP, and the result is
permuted back. Since the layer objective tr((W −c
W)H(W −c
W)⊤) is invariant under
simultaneous column permutation of W, c
W, and H, the final reconstruction error is
well-defined. With group-wise quantization, the groups are formed contiguously in
the permuted order, that is, Gt = {π(td), . . . , π(td+d−1)} in the original coordinates.
The cost of the permutation itself is O(mp), negligible compared to the O(mp2) GPTQ
pass.
