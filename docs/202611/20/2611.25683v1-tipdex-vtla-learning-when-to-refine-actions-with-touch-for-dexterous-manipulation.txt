1. Introduction
Vision–Language–Action (VLA) policies generate manipu-
lation actions from visual observations and language instruc-
tions [1, 2, 9, 17]. In contact-rich dexterous manipulation,
however, the hand often occludes the interaction region,
making the onset and evolution of local contact difficult to
observe. Besides, visual guidance is also sensitive to illu-
mination: lighting changes can alter policy predictions even
when the physical scene remains unchanged [5, 7, 10, 36].
Their impact depends on action-relevant cues: a dim image
may preserve grasp geometry, whereas a localized reflection
may obscure the intended contact region.
Vision–Tactile–Language–Action (VTLA) policies com-
plement visual observations with tactile feedback during
action generation and execution [19, 28, 45]. For example,
fingertip pressure is expected to increase when grasping an
egg. An increase beyond the expected level may indicate
excessive compression. Interpreting such changes requires
a reference for how contact is expected to evolve under the
planned actions. Tactile forecasting provides this reference,
allowing incoming measurements to be compared with ear-
lier predictions during execution [26]. Such verification
assesses whether contact follows the current plan, but leaves
open whether that plan remains reliable under the current
visual conditions.
This motivates jointly considering visual reliability and
tactile forecast agreement (Fig. 1). A plan perturbed by il-
lumination may still produce the tactile responses predicted
for it, while unexpected contact can arise even under reliable
vision. These complementary signals define four refinement
regimes. When touch agrees with the forecast, low visual
reliability motivates visual compensation, whereas high re-
liability favors nominal preservation. When touch deviates
from the forecast, low visual reliability motivates stronger
joint correction, while high reliability can still favor con-
tact correction. Together, these regimes motivate us using
visual reliability for refinement, with tactile forecast dis-
crepancies further increasing correction strength as contact
deviates from expectation. The question is how to learn
visual reliability and jointly refine actions.
In this paper, we present TipDex-VTLA, a predictive and
reactive VTLA framework that uses estimated visual relia-
bility and tactile forecast verification to gate tactile feature
corrections. Specifically, we introduce Visual Action Sensi-
tivity Estimation (VASE), which learns a proxy for visual
reliability from the action discrepancies of a frozen VTLA
policy across paired real normal and synthesized perturbed
observations that differ only in illumination. For tactile-
guided refinement, we develop Tactile Anticipation and
Correction during Execution (TACE), which compares
incoming tactile measurements with temporally aligned fore-
casts consistent with the intervening executed actions. The
estimated visual reliability and verified tactile discrepancies
jointly control the strength of bounded feature corrections
computed from current tactile feedback and execution con-
text. By reusing cached flow states, the fast branch refines
replaceable, unexecuted actions while preserving their origi-
nal execution times.
We evaluate TipDex-VTLA on four real-world dexter-
ous manipulation tasks and the UniVTAC simulation bench-
mark [4]. Across the four real-world tasks, our TipDex-
VTLA achieves average full-task success rates of 87.5%
under standard lighting and 73.8% under challenging un-
seen lighting. These results demonstrate the effectiveness
of reliability-aware tactile refinement under illumination
changes. The main contributions are as follows:
• We develop TipDex-VTLA, a predictive and reactive
VTLA framework that effectively refines actions under
practical yet challenging unseen illumination variations.
• We introduce the concept of action-grounded visual reli-
ability via VASE and jointly regulate tactile refinement
using verified tactile forecast discrepancies via TACE.
• Experiments on UniVTAC benchmark [4] and four real-
world dexterous manipulation tasks demonstrate TipDex-
VTLA’s effectiveness and significantly improved perfor-
mance under unseen illumination.
2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models
Vision–language–action (VLA) models map visual observa-
tions and language instructions to robot actions. RT-1 [3]
scales multi-task learning from diverse robot demonstra-
tions, while RT-2 [48] transfers pretrained vision–language
knowledge to control through joint fine-tuning on robotic and
web-scale vision–language data. Open X-Embodiment [29]
aggregates demonstrations across robots, tasks, and envi-
ronments to support cross-embodiment learning. Octo [9]
develops a transformer-based diffusion policy that supports
adaptation to new observation and action spaces. Open-
VLA [17] adapts a pretrained vision–language model for
robotic control and enables parameter-efficient fine-tuning
for new robot setups.
For continuous action generation, π0 [2] combines a pre-
trained vision–language backbone with a flow-matching ac-
tion expert to generate action chunks. Building on this archi-
tecture, π0.5 [1] improves open-world generalization through
2


heterogeneous co-training with robot demonstrations, web
data, and high-level semantic prediction. These advances
provide a foundation for incorporating tactile observations,
which complement visual scene understanding with local
contact cues.
2.2. Vision-Tactile-Language-Action Models
Vision–tactile–language–action (VTLA) models extend gen-
eralist robot policies with physical feedback to improve
contact-rich manipulation. Prior works [11, 16] explore
tactile representations and multimodal fusion across sens-
ing modalities. AnyTouch [6] learns unified static-dynamic
representations across visuo-tactile sensors. ForceVLA [37]
and ForceVLA2 [21] incorporate force-aware feedback into
robot action generation, while DECO [20] introduces a
lightweight tactile adapter for pretrained policies. FTP-
1 [38] learns transferable tactile representations across sen-
sors and embodiments. Beyond direct fusion, AT-VLA [19]
combines adaptive tactile injection with dual-stream feed-
back control, while TacVLA [42] activates tactile tokens ac-
cording to contact states. T-Rex [28] enables high-frequency
tactile refinement through cached flow states and variable-
rate action generation.
Recent studies [31, 39, 40] extend tactile feedback from
current observations to anticipated physical interactions. N0-
VTLA [27] predicts latent tactile tokens to condition ac-
tion generation, while ForeTac-VLA [30] investigates multi-
step tactile forecasting. UniTacVLA [45] jointly models
tactile understanding and future contact prediction. These
approaches [32, 34] demonstrate that predicted tactile re-
sponses can provide useful information before physical con-
tact is fully observed.
Recent methods [35] combine tactile prediction with
execution-time action refinement. TouchWorld [47] cou-
ples tactile prediction with high-frequency residual feed-
back, while ReTouch [44] updates tactile predictions and
actions using incoming measurements. TacPAC [26] com-
pares observed touch with cached contact predictions to
correct unexecuted actions, while ResTacVLA [43] adap-
tively gates tactile residuals relative to visual predictions.
However, these methods do not explicitly address reliability
under unseen illumination changes.
2.3. Visual Robustness in Robotic Manipulation
Visual distribution shifts [24, 25] remain a major chal-
lenge for deploying robotic policies beyond their train-
ing environments. General robustness studies, including
ImageNet-C [13], AugMix [14], and DeepAugment [15],
investigate model sensitivity to visual corruptions and
augmentation-based generalization. In robotic manipulation,
LIBERO-Plus [5] systematically evaluates VLA policies un-
der changes in illumination, camera viewpoints, and other
environmental factors.
Recent approaches improve VLA robustness through dif-
ferent strategies. RobustVLA [10] introduces robustness-
aware post-training, while StableVLA [7] uses an informa-
tion bottleneck adapter to suppress irrelevant visual noise.
ChromaGuard [33] studies illumination robustness while
preserving task-relevant color information. E-VLA [41]
incorporates event-based sensing under degraded visual con-
ditions. Beyond visual feature robustness, Evidence-Gated
Regularization [36] uses task-relevance signals to regulate
sensor dependence, while BAS-VLA [23] investigates action
calibration under task-preserving appearance changes and
task-semantic variations.
Building on these studies, we combine action-aware vi-
sual reliability assessment with tactile feedback as comple-
mentary physical evidence. This integration supports robust
manipulation under unseen illumination variations.
3. Method
TipDex-VTLA integrates a multimodal base policy with
reliability-aware tactile refinement during execution (Fig. 2).
At observation time t, the base policy πθ receives multi-view
RGB images It, a language instruction ℓ, robot propriocep-
tive state st, and recent tactile history T≤t, where Tt denotes
a tactile frame. The VLM encodes the image–language–state
prefix into context ct, and the Tactile Encoder extracts ob-
served tactile features Ft. Together, these representations
condition the Action Expert to generate a nominal chunk
At of H actions (Sec. 3.1). VASE estimates action-relevant
visual reliability qv from ct (Sec. 3.2). During execution,
TACE verifies incoming tactile measurements against tem-
porally aligned forecasts and combines the resulting discrep-
ancies with qv to gate bounded feature corrections (Sec. 3.3).
The fast branch reuses cached flow states to incorporate these
corrections into replaceable, unexecuted actions, yielding
an updated chunk eAt while preserving the original action
positions and execution times. Training proceeds in two
stages, as illustrated in Fig. 3 and detailed in Sec. 3.4.
3.1. Model Architecture
The backbone of πθ comprises a pretrained VLM, a Tactile
Encoder, and a flow-matching Action Expert [22] (Fig. 2(a)).
The VLM processes the prefix formed by (It, ℓ, st) to pro-
duce ct. The Tactile Encoder aggregates recent tactile obser-
vations into Ft, preserving five fingertip regions and three
palm sensing regions. The observed tactile features Ft are
concatenated with ct to condition the Action Expert. VASE
branches from ct before this concatenation: a lightweight
VASE Head pools the prefix representation and outputs qv,
which is cached for fast refinement.
The Action Expert adopts a slow–fast flow generation
scheme based on T-Rex [28]. We use τ for flow time, with
noise at τ = 0 and actions at τ = 1. The slow update caches
ct and an intermediate flow state Xτs, where 0 < τs < 1. At

Uτ ∈RH×dU . The corrected features pass through the re-

Only replaceable, unexecuted commands are submitted as
replacements in eAt.
3.2. Visual Action Sensitivity Estimation (VASE)
VASE estimates visual action reliability using the sensitiv-
ity of a fixed reference policy to illumination changes. For
each demonstration observation at t, we retain the original
multi-view image Ia and construct its paired variant Ib by
applying illumination augmentation to the same observa-
tion. The paired-image sampling distribution assigns equal
probability (20% each) to five conditions: low illumination,
strong illumination, changed illumination direction, uneven
illumination, and unchanged illumination. The pairing pre-
serves the viewpoint and physical state. We use lower-case
action symbols for normalized chunks. The frozen base pol-
icy obtained in Stage 1 generates baa and bab using identical
language, robot state, tactile history, sampling noise, and
sampling settings. Their difference therefore measures the
reference policy’s response to the image perturbation under
fixed non-image conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 3(b), the
same paired predictions supply the supervision targets for
both VASE and TACE.

3.3. Tactile Anticipation and Correction during Ex-
ecution (TACE)
As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), TACE uses cached tactile predic-
tions for verification. The discrepancy and visual reliability
gate feature residuals computed from Action Expert features,
measured touch, and execution context. The predictor path
in Fig. 2(a) summarizes this delayed verification.

4. Experiments
We evaluate TipDex-VTLA on four real-world dexterous
manipulation tasks and in UniVTAC simulation [4]. We
assess robustness to unseen lighting and analyze the cues
that govern execution-time tactile refinement.

Platforms and Data.
Our platform consists of two Real-
Man RM75 7-DoF arms equipped with Inspire RH56F2-
E2-T1 dexterous hands. Each hand has eight piezoresistive
tactile arrays comprising 364 taxels, sampled at 30 Hz. We
capture multi-view RGB observations using one overhead
RealSense D405 and two wrist-mounted D435i cameras.

Training and Implementation.
In Stage 1, we jointly
train the VTLA backbone, tactile encoder, and tactile fore-
caster for 20,000 optimization steps. In Stage 2, we freeze
these modules and optimize only VASE and TACE for
10,000 steps. Both stages use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs
and the AdamW optimizer, with a global batch size of 128
and a peak learning rate of 5 × 10−5. We use ten uniform
flow integration steps. Fast tactile refinement is applied dur-
ing the final four steps, from τs = 0.6 to τ = 1. We bound
each feature residual component by b = 0.1. All model
parameters remain fixed during deployment.
Evaluation Protocol.
For real-world evaluation, we adapt
vision–tactile baselines to our piezoresistive tactile sensors
by integrating our tactile encoder. For each method and task,
we run 20 trials under standard indoor lighting (N) and 20 un-
der unseen lighting (U). The 20 unseen configurations span
ten combinations of asymmetric lighting and color tempera-
ture, five low-light settings, and five bright-light settings. We
match task layouts and reset conditions and use automatic
camera exposure. Further details of the experimental settings
and evaluation procedures are provided in the supplementary
material. We report full-task success rates (%) across tasks.
For simulation, we integrate the optical tactile encoder re-
leased with UniVTAC into our VTLA backbone and evaluate
under standard simulated lighting. We use 100 test rollouts
per policy per task.
Baselines.
For real-world evaluation, we compare TipDex-
VTLA with vision-only ACT [46] and π0.5 [1], as well as
tactile-enabled T-Rex [28] and N0-VTLA [27]. For a fair
comparison, all methods are trained on the complete dataset
described above. For simulation, we compare with these
four methods on all eight UniVTAC tasks [4]. We addi-
tionally include published results for vision-only LingBot-
VA [18] and tactile-enabled VITaL [8], FTP-1 [38], and ACT
equipped with the UniVTAC tactile encoder [4], retaining
their reported configurations. The two ACT variants provide
references with and without tactile conditioning within the
same policy framework.

4.3. Simulation Results
We evaluate TipDex-VTLA on all eight UniVTAC tasks with
both VASE and TACE enabled. Optical tactile images are
encoded by a shared ResNet-18 [12], initialized from the
released UniVTAC tactile encoder [4]. The tactile forecaster
predicts future tactile images for TACE verification.
As shown in Tab. 1, the full adapted model achieves a
mean success rate of 84.9%. TACE compares forecasts with
time-aligned measurements and combines discrepancies with
VASE reliability to gate Action Expert feature corrections
for replaceable, unexecuted actions.
4.4. Ablation Studies
Component Ablation.
Tab. 3 compares components un-
der both lighting conditions. Under standard lighting, the
full model achieves 87.5% average success, compared with
85.0% without VASE and 78.8% without either tactile verifi-
cation or the Gate Head. Under unseen lighting, removing
VASE, tactile verification, or the Gate Head yields 42.5%,
62.5%, and 40.0%, respectively, compared with 73.8% for
the full model. The larger performance drop without VASE
under unseen lighting supports its role in illumination robust-
ness, while tactile verification and gating benefit standard-
light performance. Combining both reliability cues yields an
11.3-point gain over the variant without tactile verification
under unseen lighting.
Action refinement under illumination changes.
Fig. 5
shows decreasing qv and larger refinement gains as illumina-
tion perturbations intensify. At −3 and +3 EV, the full model
reduces trajectory RMSE by 24.2% and 13.0% relative to
disabling feature correction; retaining forecast verification
further reduces error. These results support the complemen-
tary cues motivating the four regimes in Fig. 1. With fore-
cast agreement, reliable vision favors nominal preservation,
while low visual reliability motivates visual compensation.
Forecast disagreement instead motivates contact correction
with reliable vision and joint correction with low visual
reliability.
5. Conclusion
We proposed TipDex-VTLA, a policy that addresses action
drift induced by changes in the visual environment during
dexterous manipulation. The framework comprises two
modules: VASE and TACE. VASE estimates visual action
reliability from policy sensitivity to illumination changes.
TACE combines this estimate with tactile prediction discrep-
ancies to regulate execution-time refinement of unexecuted
actions. Evaluation on four real-world tasks and the UniV-
TAC benchmark supports the effectiveness of this approach,
with improved real-world full-task success under unseen
lighting. The current reliability estimate remains a policy-
dependent proxy learned from illumination perturbations.
Future work will explore broader visual disturbances and
task-level feedback to improve reliability estimation and ex-
tend tactile-guided refinement to more diverse manipulation
scenarios.
