Abstract
Low-bit LLM quantization is useful only if a model preserves service-relevant accuracy,
executes through supported GPU kernels, and meets measured memory and latency limits. We
study Qwen3-8B on NVIDIA B200 with vLLM, including INT2/INT3, GPTQ, NVFP4, and
mixed-precision configurations. We verify native packed execution and precision on all 144 fused
linear modules and evaluate Korean, English, numerical, and reasoning quality under preregistered
gates. Packed INT2 and INT3 execute, but all seven tested INT2 grids degenerate on generation.
Isolated module-restoration effects are strongly non-additive; Korean and reasoning sensitivity
rankings disagree, and a promising INT4/INT8 development candidate fails independent final
evaluation. A configuration with 16-bit activations, 98 NVFP4 weight-only modules, 46 INT8
modules, and an INT8 output head passes all gates on the untouched FINAL-81 set (5,168
items): 5.917 GiB of weight-loading memory (limit 5.98), Korean teacher-correct retention 0.904
(gate 0.90), and TPOT ratio 0.939 relative to BF16. It improves paired accuracy over NVFP4
W4A4 by +1.4 percentage points (Bonferroni-adjusted interval [+0.2, +2.6]), compared with
+3.4 points on the selection set. This comparison changes both activation precision and INT8
protection; exploratory analyses suggest that the activation change contributes substantially, but
the isolated allocation effect is not established. Final checkpoints were not retained, and bitwise
rebuild reproducibility remains unverified.

Introduction
Low-bit quantization is the standard way to cut the memory footprint and serving cost of large
language models. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024b) showed that weight
precision can be reduced substantially while preserving most of a model’s capability, and mixed-
precision methods (Zheng et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025) assign different precisions to different
components to improve the accuracy–compression trade-off. GPU hardware has meanwhile added
native support for microscaling formats such as NVFP4, although accuracy and speed depend
strongly on the format, its numerical details and the runtime implementation (Egiazarian et al.,
2026).
A gap therefore opens between quantization feasibility and deployment feasibility. A compact
checkpoint can still need more GPU memory than expected once loaded. A configuration can run
through an optimized packed kernel and still produce unusable text. A format can deliver high
output-token throughput while the first token arrives late, or while one language degrades far more

than another. Language-aware calibration has been studied (Chimoto et al., 2026; Waskito et al.,
2026); here we evaluate its task-specific effects alongside measured runtime memory and kernel

We study these interactions on Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) served by vLLM (vLLM Project,
2026) on an NVIDIA B200 GPU, through three preregistered experiment stages (D79, D80 and
D81). Instead of optimizing a single compression ratio or benchmark score, each candidate must
jointly satisfy four conditions: it runs through the intended packed kernel for every fused module;
its measured weight-loading memory stays below a fixed limit; its Korean, English, numerical and
reasoning quality stays within preregistered tolerances of the BF16 teacher; and its time per output
token (TPOT) does not exceed that of BF16 by more than 10%.
One instructive result is a failure. In D80, a mixed INT4/INT8 configuration ranked first on the
development set and then failed the quality gates on the independent final set. D81 was designed
around that lesson, with separate diagnostic, selection and final sets and a rule that no research-only
candidate may be promoted to a deployment candidate on the strength of a final score. Under that
protocol one configuration, which combines NVFP4 weight-only quantization with INT8 protection,
passed every gate on both the selection set and the untouched final set.
Contributions.
• An end-to-end validation workflow that checks packed execution and weight type per
fused module, measured weight-loading memory, per-axis quality against a teacher, and serving
latency, instead of relying on nominal bitwidth (Section 3).
• A characterization of low-bit failure modes on B200: INT2 and INT3 execute natively,
yet INT2 degenerates on generation under every grid we tried and INT3 leaves large Korean
and reasoning losses (Sections 5.1–5.3).
• Evidence against isolated sensitivity heuristics: restoration effects are strongly non-
additive, Korean and reasoning rankings largely disagree, and a development-set winner did
not survive held-out evaluation (Sections 5.4–5.5).
• A deployment-constrained configuration confirmed on a held-out set, together with
exploratory evidence that activation precision is a major contributor to its advantage; the
independent effect of the INT8 allocation remains unidentified (Sections 5.6–5.7).
We did not invent GPTQ, NVFP4, mixed-precision quantization or any kernel. The contribution
is an empirical study under deployment constraints and a configuration-selection protocol whose
failures we report alongside its successes.

Background and related work
Weight-only quantization and rounding.
Early deployment-oriented quantization work es-
tablished the importance of exceptional activations, per-channel scaling, and hardware-aware
compression. Dettmers et al. (2022) isolate outliers in 8-bit transformer inference, while Yao et al.
(2022) co-design a quantization pipeline and its inference backend. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023)
uses second-order error compensation for weight-only quantization, and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024b)
exploits activation statistics to protect salient weights. Nontrivial rounding and clipping choices are
studied by AdaRound (Nagel et al., 2020) and OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2024). SpQR (Dettmers
et al., 2024) and SqueezeLLM (Kim et al., 2024) protect difficult weights using sparse or mixed
2


representations. We use established GPTQ and RTN recipes; we neither modify their optimizers
nor compare them directly with the full range of these alternatives. Our emphasis is on whether the
resulting configuration is executable, satisfies a measured memory ceiling, and preserves preregistered
task capabilities.
Extreme low-bit representations.
QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) and QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024)
combine incoherence processing with adaptive rounding and, in the latter, lattice codebooks. AQLM
(Egiazarian et al., 2024) uses additive codebook quantization. ParetoQ (Liu et al., 2025b) studies
training and scaling across extremely low bitwidths. Quantization-aware training also offers a
different recovery path: LLM-QAT (Liu et al., 2024a) and EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2025) use
training or layer-wise optimization to reduce quantization damage. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023)
addresses efficient adaptation of models with quantized frozen weights, a distinct setting from
our post-training inference experiments. These methods are relevant alternatives to our native
INT2/INT3 diagnostics; their reported success prevents generalizing our INT2 failures into a claim
that two-bit language models are inherently unusable. We did not implement or benchmark these
alternatives on our fixed B200 runtime.
Activation precision and FP4.
SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) transfers quantization difficulty
from activations to weights. QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024), SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2025a), DuQuant
(Lin et al., 2024a), and FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) employ different transformations or rotation
strategies to control activation outliers. Recent microscaling FP4 work (Egiazarian et al., 2026)
directly examines the gap between the accuracy and hardware promise of FP4 formats. These
methods make an important distinction for our study: NVFP4 W4A4 and NVFP4 W4A16 are
not equivalent comparisons, because they use different activation precision. Our FINAL-81 A81
versus W4A4 result changes both activation precision and weight protection; it is therefore a
configuration-level comparison, not an identified allocation effect.
Mixed-precision allocation and interaction effects.
Classical mixed-precision methods such
as HAWQ (Dong et al., 2019) and HAWQ-V2 (Dong et al., 2020) use sensitivity information to help
allocate precision, including second-order and Pareto-based approaches. Atom (Zhao et al., 2024)
co-designs low-bit quantization and runtime throughput. More recent LLM approaches include
MixLLM (Zheng et al., 2026), which mixes precision across output features, and IMPQ (Zhao et al.,
2025), which considers interactions between layers. Our non-additive restoration results (Section 5.4)
motivate such interaction-aware methods, but our frozen allocation procedure is a greedy heuristic,
not an interaction-aware optimizer. Without matched-memory comparisons to these methods we
make no methodological superiority claim.
GPU serving and memory accounting.
The PagedAttention/vLLM system (Kwon et al.,
2023) addresses dynamic KV-cache memory; Marlin (Frantar et al., 2024) optimizes weight-only
quantized linear inference, and FlashInfer (Ye et al., 2025) studies customizable GPU serving kernels.
QServe (Lin et al., 2025) explicitly co-designs low-bit quantization and serving, whereas KIVI (Liu
et al., 2024b) targets KV-cache precision rather than model-weight precision. We separately measure
the vLLM weight-loading allocation increment and token latency. We do not treat the former
as total peak serving memory, nor report KV-cache or concurrency improvements that were not
measured. The Humming kernels (Ant Group and the vLLM Community, 2026) provide the native
INT2/INT3 execution path in our pinned vLLM environment (vLLM Project, 2026); we implement
no matrix-multiplication kernel of our own.

Multilingual calibration and evaluation.
Chimoto et al. (2026) investigate multilingual
calibration for GPTQ and AWQ, while Waskito et al. (2026) examine calibration language, bitwidth
and cross-lingual effects. These studies motivate a per-language rather than solely aggregate quality
analysis.
Our Korean-enriched calibration experiment (Section 5.8) is a configuration-specific
counterexample to assuming that adding more target-language calibration text necessarily improves
target-language performance without harming reasoning. The observation does not contradict
benefits reported in other models or calibration settings.
3
Deployment-constrained quantization
3.1
Model and quantization units
Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) has 36 transformer layers. vLLM executes each layer through four
fused linear modules: the fused query–key–value projection (qkv), the output projection (o), the
fused gate and up projection (gate_up) and the down projection (down), giving 144 fused modules
in total. We assign precision at these boundaries because the runtime requires one scheme per fused
module. In a negative control, assigning W4 to the query projection and W8 to the key and value
projections inside one fused qkv module made the vLLM weight loader reject the checkpoint with a
shape assertion. All configurations below therefore use one precision per fused module. The output
head (lm_head) is treated separately.
3.2
Formats and runtime paths
Table 1 lists the families we evaluate and the kernels vLLM 0.29.0 selected for them on B200. Its
WNA16 path accepts symmetric integer weights from 2 to 8 bits and asymmetric weights only at 4
and 8 bits. Kernel names describe the measured configuration, not a claim that a kernel family is
generally optimal.
3.3
Memory constraint
Checkpoint size, the sum of registered parameter bytes and the GPU memory allocated while loading
can all differ. Our memory gate uses vLLM’s raw weight-loading counter, the change in allocated
GPU memory while the model loads:
M(c) = weight_load_bytes(c)/230 < 5.98 GiB,
(1)
with strict inequality. The limit is a fixed product requirement of this study. During allocation we
predict M(c) from earlier measurements with a 32 MiB safety margin, but a prediction never counts
4


as passing: each candidate is first loaded in a smoke job that does not read the selection set, and
only a measured counter below the limit admits it to selection. The counter excludes KV cache and
runtime activations.
3.4
Quality and latency constraints
Let T be the BF16 teacher and Q a quantized configuration. For an evaluation axis a we measure
teacher-correct retention and the change in accuracy,
ra(Q) = P(Q correct | T correct) ,
∆a(Q) = acca(Q) −acca(T).
(2)
Retention measures how many of the teacher’s correct decisions survive; the accuracy change also
credits items the teacher gets wrong and the quantized model gets right. The tier is retention pooled
over all items of the four gate axes (Korean, English, numerical, reasoning). A configuration passes
the quality gates if tier ≥0.90, rko ≥0.90, overall, numerical and reasoning accuracy changes are
each at least −0.03 (three points), and the rate of malformed outputs on generation items rises
by at most 2 points. It passes serving if M(c) < 5.98 GiB and its median TPOT is at most 1.1
times that of BF16. Execution must match the expected packed kernel for every fused module, with
no layer silently expanded to floating point, and the software environment must match the frozen
manifest. Time to first token (TTFT) is reported but not gated. These thresholds were fixed before
D78 and not changed in any later stage.
3.5
Execution verification
Kernel names alone are not enough: INT4 and INT8 both run through Marlin, so the stored weight
type is checked separately, and an NVFP4 scheme name does not reveal whether activations are
quantized. For every candidate the checker records the kernel and weight type of each fused module,
the activation policy, the output head, the absence of floating-point fallbacks, the model revision
and the raw memory counter.
3.6
Sensitivity diagnostics and allocation
Diagnostic runs, on a set reserved for them, measure how much each fused module contributes:
starting from a fully quantized base, one module is restored to BF16 at a time and the change in
teacher-correct retention is recorded per axis. We use these measurements to rank modules, not to
predict the quality of a combination. A frozen script orders modules by their joint restoration effect
(or a Korean-weighted variant) and a second frozen script fills the memory budget from the top of
the list, stopping at the first module that does not fit. Both scripts and their output hashes were
fixed before the selection set was read. Restoration is a proxy: the deployed protection format is
INT8, not BF16. We do not claim that this greedy procedure is optimal.
4
Experimental protocol
4.1
Hardware and software
All runs use one NVIDIA B200 with vLLM 0.29.0, PyTorch 2.11.0 and transformers 5.16.1. Check-
points are produced with llm-compressor 0.15.0 and compressed-tensors 0.20.1 from a pinned
Qwen3-8B revision. Each job verifies its environment against the stage’s frozen manifest before
evaluating. Serving latency is measured at batch size 1 over 32 prompts with 256 forced output
5


tokens. Measured GPU time for the D81 stages was 4.98 (diagnostic), 2.39 (selection) and 1.35
(final) B200 GPU-hours, excluding queueing.
4.2
Evaluation sets
Items come from KMMLU (Son et al., 2025), KoBEST (Jang et al., 2022), MMLU (Hendrycks
et al., 2021), ARC (Clark et al., 2018), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), BIG-Bench Hard (Suzgun
et al., 2023), BIG-bench (Srivastava et al., 2023), MuSR (Sprague et al., 2024), HRM8K (Ko
et al., 2025) and a seeded generator of numerical problems. Multiple-choice items are scored by
log-likelihood; generation items use greedy decoding with output limits of 1,536 tokens (numerical),
4,096 (reasoning) and 768 (Korean math). Each stage builds its sets with a script that removes
every item used in earlier stages, so no item is reused across D78–D81.
D80 used diagnostic, development and final sets of 1,662, 3,003 and 4,061 items. D81 used a
diagnostic set of 1,998 items, a selection set (SEL-81) of 5,052 items and a final set (FINAL-81) of
5,168 items. FINAL-81 contains 4,798 gate-axis items (Korean 1,565, English 1,043, numerical 1,320,
reasoning 870), of which 2,190 are generation items, plus 370 report-only items, 300 of them HRM8K
Korean math. No evaluation item is reproduced in this paper. Dataset access and redistribution
rights are handled separately from the manuscript and must be checked against each source’s terms
before any evaluation-set release.

Selection and final evaluation
The diagnostic set builds candidates, the selection set chooses them under the frozen rule, and the
final set is read once for the chosen candidates and their references. A track with no candidate that
passes all gates has no deployment candidate; a research-only candidate is never promoted because
it scores well on the final set.

5.2
Changing the INT2 grid does not stop degeneration
We then asked whether the INT2 failure comes from the particular symmetric grid. Seven grids
were evaluated on the D80 diagnostic set, including symmetric, ternary, balanced four-level and
asymmetric variants (Table 3). Asymmetric and balanced grids recovered some multiple-choice
decisions, the best reaching a retention of 0.243, but every grid looped and truncated on all generation
items and retained none of the teacher’s correct generations. This rules out the simplest explanation,
that changing the grid alone restores generation. It is not an impossibility result for INT2: we did
not try quantization-aware training or other models.

5.3
Mixed INT3/INT4 precision helps but stays below the gates
The D80 mixed configuration keeps 41 fused modules in INT4 (Marlin) and the remaining 103 in
INT3 (Humming), at a measured 5.191 GiB (the allocator had predicted 5.159 GiB). On the D80
final set it beat GPTQ W3 by +10.3 points [+8.6, +12.0], with gains of +19.4 points on numerical
items, +12.4 on reasoning and +5.8 on Korean. Its Korean retention was still only 0.730 and its tier
0.804 (Table 4). A relative improvement over a weak baseline is not deployment feasibility.
5.4
Isolated sensitivity predicts combined restoration poorly
The D81 diagnostics, run on five quantized bases, show two related effects (Table 5). First, Korean
and reasoning sensitivities rank modules very differently: Spearman correlations between the two
rankings range from −0.046 to 0.256. A single ranking cannot protect both capabilities. Second,
effects do not add up. On the two track-A bases, summing the Korean retention gains from restoring
each module alone gives 5.4 to 7.6 times the gain from restoring all of them together; on the INT3
bases the ratio is negative. A sensitivity ranking can nominate candidates, but the quality of the
resulting mixture has to be measured directly.
5.5

5.6
D81: selection and final evaluation
D81 built five candidates from the diagnostics under frozen rules. In track A, A81 protects 46 fused
modules (23 qkv, 18 o, 1 gate_up, 4 down) in INT8 on an NVFP4 W4A16 body with an INT8
output head, using 0.499 of its 0.50 GiB budget; its reference is NVFP4 W4A4 (n4). A parallel
candidate on a GPTQ W4 body (53 INT8 modules) has GPTQ W4 as its reference. In track B, B81
upgrades 44 modules of a GPTQ W3 body to INT4; a second candidate uses a Korean-weighted
ranking and a third repeats B81 with Korean-heavy calibration. The track-B allocator stopped at
0.251 GiB under every budget it was given (0.30, 0.55 and 0.80 GiB), because the next modules
in rank order were excluded as harmful; the two smaller budgets therefore yielded the identical
artifact and were treated as aliases. Track B was preregistered as underpowered: the planned
interval half-width needed about 6,100 items, more than SEL-81 provides, so non-significant track-B
differences are not evidence of no effect.
On SEL-81, A81 was the only candidate that passed every gate (tier 0.935, Korean retention 0.924,

5.7
What the gain over W4A4 consists of
The comparison with n4 changes two things at once: activations go from FP4 to 16 bits, and
46 modules gain INT8 protection.
The D80 development set provides a partial, exploratory
decomposition (Table 8); it does not identify the effect of the D81 allocation on an independent
set. Moving from n4 to NVFP4 W4A16 alone raised paired accuracy by +2.8 points [+1.6, +4.0];
adding INT8 modules on top of it changed accuracy by −0.3 points [−1.3, +0.8], which is not
significant. These exploratory comparisons suggest that much of the gain relative to W4A4 is
associated with keeping activations at 16-bit precision. The catch is memory. NVFP4 W4A16 with
a BF16 output head measured 6.009 GiB, above the limit. Quantizing the output head to INT8
saves about 0.57 GiB (a smoke load measured 5.44 GiB for this combination), and the 46 INT8
modules then add 0.499 GiB, giving the final 5.917 GiB. The INT8 head is what makes 16-bit
activations fit, and the INT8 modules occupy the remaining headroom. We never measured the
quality of NVFP4 W4A16 with an INT8 head and no INT8 modules, nor a random choice of 46
INT8 modules, so the contribution of the allocation itself is not identified. On the GPTQ W4 body
the picture was similar: an INT8 head alone did not help (−0.6 [−1.7, +0.6]), INT8 modules on top
of it did (+1.6 [+0.4, +2.8]), and that configuration later failed on the final set (Section 5.5).

Korean-heavy calibration damaged reasoning
Calibration sets had 128 sequences of 2,048 tokens. The default set contained no Korean text; the
Korean-enriched sets replaced 25% or 50% of the rows with Korean web text. On the diagnostic
set, 50% Korean calibration raised Korean retention of the W3 base by +12.2 points [+2.3, +22.1]
and reasoning by +7.4, so it was chosen for the calibration test.
These diagnostic intervals
were exploratory (independent-sample approximations). The preregistered paired test on SEL-81
compared B81 with the same allocation recalibrated on that set. The Korean difference was +2.2
points [−0.9, +5.4], not significant, while reasoning fell by −12.3 points [−16.8, −7.9]. The diagnostic
gain did not transfer. Calibration-language changes have to be evaluated against every required
capability; a calibration improvement for one language should not be declared from that language
alone.
5.9
Serving behaviour
Formats differ in where they are fast (Table 9). NVFP4 W4A4 produced 387 output tokens per
second against 255.4 for BF16, but its median TTFT was 42.3 ms against 15.3 ms. A81 delivered
272 tokens per second with a median TTFT of 15.4 ms, close to BF16 on both. These are batch-1
measurements on short prompts and do not generalize to other batch sizes, prompt lengths or
concurrency levels.

Aggregate scores hide language-specific failures.
Several configurations had acceptable

More precision on selected modules is not reliably better.
The D80 INT4/INT8 result,
the non-additivity of restoration and the weak INT8 contribution on the NVFP4 body all point the
same way. Which modules to protect depends on interactions, the task mix, the activation path
and the supported formats; a ranking from isolated measurements is a weak guide.
Runtime support shapes the search space.
Fused-module boundaries, kernel availability,
loading overhead and activation precision determine which mixtures can actually run. Among the
configurations we tested, a feasible path was to retain 16-bit activations and use an INT8 output
head to create memory headroom; no global cost-optimality claim follows. Including such constraints
in the search changes which configuration wins.

Limitations
One model, one GPU.
All results are for Qwen3-8B on B200 with vLLM 0.29.0. Other models,
scales, architectures and runtimes may behave differently.
Attribution is incomplete.
The gain of A81 over n4 combines activation precision and INT8
protection. We have neither a quality measurement of NVFP4 W4A16 with an INT8 head and no
INT8 modules nor a random-allocation control, and we compared against no published allocation
method.
Small margins.
A81’s Korean retention passed its gate by 0.0036, and its superiority lower bound
was +0.2 points. A modest shift in the item mix could change either verdict.
Effect shrinkage.
The advantage over n4 fell from +3.4 points on SEL-81 to +1.4 on FINAL-81,
consistent with some selection optimism even under a frozen protocol.
Checkpoints not preserved.
Final-stage checkpoints were built, fingerprinted and served inside
the evaluation job and not retained. The SEL-81 and FINAL-81 builds of A81, from the same recipe,
calibration and code, have different SHA-256 digests, so rebuilds are not bitwise reproducible. We
have not measured how much quality varies between rebuilds.
Allocator prediction.
The allocator predicted 5.947 GiB against an internal planning limit of
5.949 GiB, leaving 2.01 MiB; the measured counter was 5.917 GiB. The prediction was conservative
here, but it is a planning aid, not a guarantee.
Underpowered track B.
Track B was preregistered as underpowered; its null results are not
evidence of no effect.
Serving scope.
Latency was measured at batch size 1 with short prompts. Throughput under
concurrency, long contexts, KV-cache capacity, peak memory and energy were not measured.
Study-specific gates.
The thresholds are requirements of this study, not industry standards.
No release.
Passing the preregistered gates is not a product release. No checkpoint accompanies
this paper.
8
Future work
The most important next step is a causal ablation under one protocol on a fresh held-out set: NVFP4
W4A4, NVFP4 W4A16 with an INT8 head and no INT8 modules, the same with 46 randomly chosen
INT8 modules, and the ranked allocation, all at matched memory. Interaction-aware allocators
(Zhao et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026) should be compared under the same runtime constraints.
Repeating the study on other model families and sizes, measuring rebuild variance, and extending
the serving study to concurrency, long contexts and memory headroom would show how far these
findings extend.

9
Conclusion
We studied low-bit and mixed-precision quantization of Qwen3-8B on NVIDIA B200 under execution,
memory, latency and multilingual quality constraints. Native INT2 and INT3 execution did not
ensure usable output, isolated module sensitivity was a poor guide to mixtures, a development-set
winner failed on held-out items, and Korean-heavy calibration damaged reasoning. A configuration
with 16-bit activations, NVFP4 weights, 46 INT8 modules and an INT8 output head passed all
preregistered gates on an untouched final set, with 5.917 GiB of weight-loading memory, Korean
retention 0.904 and a TPOT ratio of 0.939, and outperformed NVFP4 W4A4 by +1.4 points
[+0.2, +2.6]. Exploratory development-set comparisons suggest activation precision is the larger
contributor, but the effect of the selected INT8 allocation remains unidentified. Quantization for
deployment is a joint numerical and systems problem, and the measurement protocol, including
what it rejects, matters as much as the format.

B
Ablations needed for an allocation claim
The present results do not isolate the allocation procedure. At minimum, the following arms are
needed on a fresh held-out set: NVFP4 W4A4 (reference); NVFP4 W4A16 with an INT8 head and
no INT8 modules (activation precision only); the same with 46 randomly chosen INT8 modules
(protection budget only); the same with the 46 ranked modules (allocation); and a published
allocation method at matched measured memory. Where memory differs, both accuracy at matched
memory and the full measured quality–memory frontier should be reported. Items consumed by
FINAL-81 must not be reused for tuning.
C
Reproducibility
For each stage we keep the set manifests and hashes, the pinned model revision, the quantization
recipes and calibration hashes, the frozen ranking and allocation code with its output hashes,
the per-module precision assignments, environment manifests, raw memory counters, kernel and
weight-type verification records, decoding settings and the preregistration records. Every number in
this paper is generated from those ledgers by one script. Final-stage checkpoints were not preserved
(see Limitations). Infrastructure identifiers and credentials are not part of the record.
