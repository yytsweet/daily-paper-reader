ABSTRACT
Microscaling (MX) quantization enables lower-precision large language model (LLM) inference with limited
accuracy degradation, reducing memory requirement and multiplication cost. However, despite reduced operand
precision, accumulation is often retained at high floating-point precisions, leaving a substantial hardware cost that
operand quantization alone does not address. In particular, systolic arrays, as used in tensor accelerators such as
Google TPU and AWS Trainium, distribute accumulation across distinct processing elements but conventionally
use uniform accumulator precision throughout the array. This spatial organization offers an opportunity to reduce
hardware cost by tailoring precision to individual accumulation stages.
In this work, we present MIAO, a hardware-software co-design framework that jointly selects product preci-
sion and stage-dependent accumulator widths to minimize hardware area and energy cost under an end-to-end
perplexity constraint. MIAO first calibrates MX block-scale shifts to reduce intermediate magnitudes while pre-
serving operand quantization accuracy, then assigns narrower accumulators to earlier stages and progressively
wider accumulators to later stages. To efficiently explore the resulting design space, MIAO combines hardware
cost estimates derived from logic synthesis with a calibrated accuracy predictor to identify promising precision
configurations, then validates the accuracy of promising candidates through bit-exact end-to-end LLM evalua-
tion. Across MXFP8, MXFP6, and MXFP4, MIAO reduces processing element (PE) array area by up to 38.7%
and energy by up to 36.1% relative to the all-BF16 accumulation baseline, with at most 1.60% additional per-
plexity degradation over operand-only MX quantization across six LLMs.

INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) impose substantial com-
putational and memory demands, motivating the devel-
opment of specialized accelerators for efficient inference
(Jouppi et al., 2017). A key technique for improving hard-
ware efficiency is quantization, which reduces the precision
of weights and activations to lower memory requirements
and allows less costly arithmetic operations (Horowitz,
2014). Aggressive low-bit quantization can introduce sub-
stantial numerical error due to the wide dynamic ranges
of weights and activations, particularly in the presence of
outliers (Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2023).
Mi-
croscaling (MX) formats mitigate this error by sharing a
scale across small blocks of low-precision floating-point el-
ements, adapting their representation to local value ranges
and enabling aggressive operand quantization with limited

do not explicitly optimize floating-point accumulator width

Our key insight is that accumulation precision can be tai-
lored to individual hardware stages rather than chosen uni-
formly for the entire array. Conventional designs neverthe-
less use the same accumulator format throughout the array.
For example, in weight-stationary systolic arrays, as used
in tensor accelerators such as Google’s TPU and AWS’s
Trainium, each processing element multiplies an incoming
activation by a stationary weight and adds the product to a
partial sum received from the preceding row (Kwon et al.,
2019; Sze et al., 2017). Successive accumulation stages
therefore map to distinct hardware units whose widths can
be selected independently. Earlier stages accumulate fewer
products, creating an opportunity to use narrower formats,
while later stages can retain wider formats to accommodate
growing partial sums and limit rounding error (Higham,
2002). We exploit this spatial organization to reduce hard-
ware cost while preserving end-to-end model accuracy.
Realizing this opportunity, however, requires more than
just independently narrowing each accumulator; naively re-
ducing intermediate arithmetic precision in MX accelera-
tors can lead to substantial accuracy degradation.
First,
intermediate precision depends on MX operand scaling.
Conventional MX block scaling places quantized operands
near the upper range of their operand format to limit un-
derflow, producing large products and partial sums that re-
quire wide floating-point accumulation to avoid overflow.
Reducing the block-scale factor can lower these magni-
tudes but may increase operand quantization error. Sec-
ond, rounding errors introduced at different accumulation
stages propagate through subsequent computations and af-
fect end-to-end model accuracy. As a result, product and
accumulation precisions must be selected jointly, creating
a combinatorial design space that is expensive to explore
through exhaustive LLM evaluation (Dong et al., 2019).
To address these limitations,
we present MIAO, a
hardware-software co-design framework that jointly se-
lects product precision and stage-dependent accumulator
widths to minimize hardware area and energy cost under
end-to-end perplexity constraints.
MIAO first calibrates
MX block-scale shifts to reduce intermediate product and
partial-sum magnitudes while preserving operand quanti-
zation accuracy. MIAO then combines hardware cost esti-
mates derived from logic synthesis with a calibrated accu-
racy predictor to search over configurations with narrower
accumulation in earlier stages and progressively wider ac-
cumulators in later stages. The predictor guides the search
toward promising configurations, while bit-exact end-to-
end LLM evaluation verifies whether each candidate meets
the perplexity constraint.

1. We propose stage-dependent floating-point accumula-

of the reduction. Together with block-scale calibration
and product precision selection, this enables narrower

BACKGROUND
2.1
Microscaling Formats and Arithmetic
Microscaling (MX) formats represent tensors using low-
precision floating-point elements with a shared scale for
each small block.
Compared to coarser-grained quanti-
zation, smaller blocks allow scales to better match lo-
cal value ranges, reducing quantization error and enabling
lower-precision representations of LLM weights and acti-
vations. We consider MXFP8 (E4M3), MXFP6 (E3M2),
and MXFP4 (E2M1), following the Open Compute Project
(OCP) MX specification (Open Compute Project, 2023).
Each MX block contains 32 elements and a shared 8-bit
exponent-only (E8M0) scale, representing powers of two
as x = 2E−127, where E is the encoded exponent. This
enables efficient hardware scaling through exponent adjust-
ment rather than general floating-point multiplication.

shared scale x, quantized elements P, and reconstructed

its output is requantized into MX blocks before use by sub-
sequent MX-quantized operations.
2.2
Floating-Point Accumulation in Systolic Arrays
Systolic arrays accelerate matrix multiplication using grids
of processing elements (PEs) performing parallel multiply-
accumulate (MAC) operations. Common dataflows include
output-stationary (OS), where each PE accumulates a local
output, and weight-stationary (WS), where weights remain
fixed in PEs while activations propagate horizontally and
partial sums propagate vertically (Chen et al., 2016). We
focus on WS arrays (Figure 2), where successive accumu-
lation stages map to different PE rows. This allows each
row to use a fixed accumulator precision, enabling stage-
dependent precision allocation through differently sized
arithmetic hardware.
Figure 2. Weight-stationary systolic array dataflow, PE datapath.
In the WS dataflow, each PE multiplies an incoming ac-
tivation by its stationary weight and adds the product to
the partial sum received from the preceding row. While
low-precision operands reduce multiplier costs, accumula-
tors typically retain wider floating-point formats to accom-
modate the growing range of partial sums and limit round-
ing error. Floating-point accumulation requires exponent
alignment and significand arithmetic, contributing substan-
tially to hardware cost (Mach et al., 2021). As shown in
Fig. 1(c), BF16 accumulation accounts for approximately
65% of PE-array area in our baseline with MX operands
and BF16 intermediate arithmetic.
Because each successive row adds another product, partial-
sum magnitudes generally increase with accumulation
depth. This suggests that different stages may have differ-
ent precision requirements, motivating the characterization
of stage-dependent accumulation precision in Sec. 3.

OPTIMIZATION
We first examine why conventional OCP block-scaling
limits opportunities for intermediate precision reduction
(Sec. 3.1), then show how calibrating the prescribed block-
scale shifts can reduce precision requirements (Sec. 3.2).
We then use a weight-stationary systolic array as a case
study to investigate how reduced product precision and
row-dependent floating-point accumulation can translate
into hardware area and energy savings (Sec. 3.3). These
observations motivate the automated precision optimiza-
tion method in Sec. 4.
3.1
Limitations of Existing Microscaling
The intermediate precision requirements of MX arithmetic
depend not only on the operand format, but also on the
block-scale shift d (Eq.
1). The OCP specification sets
d = emax, with emax = 8, 4, and 2 for MXFP8, MXFP6,
and MXFP4, respectively (Open Compute Project, 2023).
This places the largest scaled element near the upper range
of its format to limit underflow. However, because OCP’s
scaling rule is defined independently of intermediate arith-
metic precision, it can impose substantial precision require-
ments on the hardware datapath.
Large intermediate dynamic range.
Increasing d by
one doubles the magnitude of each scaled operand and
increases product magnitudes by a factor of four, before
rounding and saturation. For MXFP8, the OCP shift d = 8
therefore increases nominal product magnitudes by a fac-
tor of 216 relative to d = 0. This amplification carries into
intermediate partial sums, which must be represented be-
fore the block scales are applied to rescale the dot-product
result.
Figure 3(a) shows partial-sum magnitudes under OCP scal-
ing across a 16-row weight-stationary systolic array. For
TinyLlama, partial sums reach approximately 220, 212,
and 28 for MXFP8, MXFP6, and MXFP4, respectively.
These magnitudes impose substantial exponent-range re-
quirements on intermediate accumulation, limiting oppor-
tunities to narrow accumulator formats without risking
overflow.
Operand quantization error. The OCP scaling rule can
also introduce saturation error. Setting d = emax places
the block maximum in the highest power-of-two range of
the element format, which is not fully representable. Con-
sequently, scaled elements exceeding the maximum repre-
sentable value are clipped. For TinyLlama under MXFP8,
the maximum element is clipped in 14-16% of activation
blocks and 9-13% of weight blocks, with an average clip-
ping magnitude of 8%. This error persists even when inter-
mediate arithmetic uses high precision.
Together, these limitations make conventional OCP scaling
poorly suited to reducing intermediate arithmetic precision:
large partial-sum magnitudes restrict accumulator narrow-
ing, while operand saturation introduces accuracy loss even
with high-precision arithmetic. Exploiting narrower prod-
uct and accumulator formats therefore requires reconsider-
ing the OCP-defined block-scale shift d.

3.3
Precision-Configurable Systolic Datapath and
Hardware Characterization
To investigate the hardware benefits of the precision re-
duction opportunities identified in Sec. 3.2, we parame-
terize a weight-stationary systolic array with configurable
floating-point formats for products and row-wise accumu-
lation. This serves as a hardware case study for characteriz-
ing how intermediate arithmetic precision affects area and
energy.
Precision-Configurable Datapath.
We extend the open-source Gemmini accelerator (Genc
et al., 2021) to support MX operands and configurable in-
termediate floating-point precision in its weight-stationary
systolic array (Fig. 4). Specifically, we support two config-
urable precision parameters:
1.
Configurable product precision.
Each PE multi-
plies an MX-quantized weight and activation, rounding its
product to configurable floating-point format fp, which is
shared across all PEs.
2. Row-wise accumulation precision. For an n-row array,
the n−1 successive accumulation stages use independently
configurable formats f1, . . . , fn−1. At stage i, the incom-
ing partial sum is added to the product, and the result is
rounded to format fi. This allows accumulator precision to
vary across rows.
3. MX requantization and scale handling. At each tile’s
output, the accumulated result is rescaled using the corre-
sponding MX block scales (Eq 2). Rescaled outputs from
multiple tiles are accumulated in BF16. Once the full ma-
trix multiplication is complete, the result is requantized to
the target MX format before being used by subsequent MX-
quantized operations.

FRAMEWORK AND SEARCH
Building on the precision-configurable datapath and hard-
ware characterization in Sec. 3, we develop an automated
method for selecting product and accumulation precisions
under end-to-end accuracy constraints. Sec. 4.1 formulates
the optimization problem, Sec. 4.2 describes the hardware-
software evaluation method, and Sec. 4.3 presents the auto-
mated search, loss-model prediction, and certification pro-
cedure.

4.2
Hardware-Software Evaluation Framework
Evaluating each precision configuration requires end-to-
end LLM inference and hardware cost characterization,
making exhaustive exploration expensive. To reduce eval-
uation overhead, we combine bit-exact arithmetic simu-
lation, accelerated LLM inference, and synthesis-derived
hardware area and energy modeling.
Bit-Exact Arithmetic Simulation. We implement a con-
figurable arithmetic simulator that reproduces the numeri-
cal behavior of the systolic datapath in Sec. 3.3. The sim-
ulator models MX operand quantization, product rounding
to the selected format fp, row-wise accumulation using the
specified formats fa,r, and BF16 inter-tile accumulation.
Operations follow the datapath’s order of execution, in-
cluding intermediate rounding, saturation, and underflow
behavior. We validate the simulator against hardware arith-
metic outputs, obtaining bit-identical results across 65,536
evaluated elements for MXFP8.
End-to-end LLM integration. We integrate the simulator
into LLM inference by replacing selected linear-layer ma-
trix multiplications with configurable MX arithmetic while
retaining BF16 for other operations. This enables direct
measurement of perplexity for each precision configura-
tion. An optimized Triton implementation preserves the
simulator’s bit-exact outputs and accelerates repeated end-
to-end perplexity evaluations by 2.2–7.4× on TinyLlama.
Hardware area and energy estimation. We construct a
hardware area and energy model from logic synthesis and
gate-level power analysis of parameterized processing el-
ements (PEs) in a 16 nm technology. We sweep product
formats with exponent widths of 3–8 bits and significand

costs according to each configuration’s product precision
and row-wise accumulator formats, enabling efficient area
and energy estimation across precision configurations and
array sizes without synthesizing each candidate.
4.3
Automated Precision Search and Certification
We develop an automated framework (Algorithm 1) that
takes a target perplexity constraint and selects a low-cost
product and accumulator precision configuration without
manual tuning.
1. Search Space. We first constrain the search space to
keep exploration tractable. For an n × n weight-stationary
array, we impose two restrictions:
(i) We use a shared product format and a non-decreasing
ladder of n −1 accumulator formats, reflecting increas-
ing partial-sum magnitudes and rounding sensitivity across
rows.
(ii) We fix accumulator exponent widths to five bits, suffi-
cient for the observed dynamic range, and vary mantissa
widths from three to seven bits to capture the accuracy-
hardware cost trade-off (Sec. 3.3).

EVALUATION
We evaluate our framework’s ability to reduce hardware
cost while preserving end-to-end LLM accuracy. Our se-
lected configurations reduce PE-array compute area by
32.0-38.7% and estimated whole-accelerator energy per
operation by 23.7-36.1% relative to all-BF16, with at most
1.06% additional perplexity degradation over operand-only
MX quantization.
We first describe the experimental setup, then examine
hardware-accuracy trade-offs and selected precision con-
figurations. Finally, we evaluate cross-model generaliza-
tion and downstream task accuracy.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Software.
We evaluate TinyLlama-1.1B and five addi-
tional LLMs on WikiText-2, using 16 windows of 2,048
tokens per model. We optimize precision configurations on
TinyLlama and apply the selected configurations to the re-
maining models without retuning. Perplexity is measured
through end-to-end inference with bit-exact emulation of
MX operands, products, and accumulation.
Hardware.
We evaluate 8 × 8, 16 × 16, and 32 × 32
weight-stationary systolic arrays. Hardware costs are es-
timated using the synthesis-derived model in Sec. 4.2,
targeting a 16 nm technology and a 2 ns clock period.
We additionally use FireSim FPGA-accelerated simula-
tion (Karandikar et al., 2018) to compare hardware arith-
metic outputs against the bit-exact software reference.
Baselines and Metrics. We use two accuracy baselines:
(1) an unquantized BF16 model with BF16 products, ac-
cumulation, and inter-tile additions, and (2) operand-only
MX quantization with BF16 products, accumulation, and
inter-tile additions. We report perplexity relative to both
baselines, along with PE-array area, estimated whole-
accelerator energy per operation, and power.
Hardware
costs are compared against BF16 intermediate arithmetic
with the same MX operands and array dimensions.

RELATED WORK
Mixed-precision allocation.
Mixed-precision quantiza-
tion assigns more bits where the model is most sensitive.
HAQ (Wang et al., 2019) uses reinforcement learning with
hardware feedback to choose layer-wise weight and activa-
tion bitwidths, and HAWQ-V2 (Dong et al., 2020) ranks
layers by Hessian trace.
For LLMs, AWQ (Lin et al.,
2024) protects salient weight channels through activation-
aware scaling, Atom (Zhao et al., 2024) keeps outlier chan-
nels at higher precision within 4-bit serving kernels, SliM-
LLM (Huang et al., 2025) allocates bitwidths to weight
groups by salience, MixLLM (Zheng et al., 2026) assigns
4- or 8-bit precision to individual output features, and
FGMP (Hooper et al., 2025) selects FP8 or NVFP4 per
weight and activation block using Fisher-weighted sensi-
tivity, with matching datapath support. All of these allocate
precision to operands. MIAO instead allocates precision to
intermediate arithmetic: the product and each accumulator
row of a systolic array, at fixed operand formats.
Precision search. Because the space of per-layer preci-
sion assignments grows exponentially, recent work reduces
search cost. FLIQS (Dotzel et al., 2024) runs a one-shot
reinforcement-learning search over integer and floating-
point formats, AMQ (Lee et al., 2025) prunes the search
space and guides an iterative search with a learned quality
predictor, and KVTuner (Li et al., 2025) prunes per-layer
key and value precision pairs offline using attention-output
error. MIAO shares the goal of avoiding exhaustive evalu-
ation, but its search space is ordered by construction: a fit-
ted rounding-loss model proposes accumulator ladders, and
every selected configuration is certified by bit-exact end-to-
end evaluation.
Hardware cost models.
Analytical models such as
Timeloop (Parashar et al., 2019) and Accelergy (Wu et al.,
2019) estimate accelerator energy and area from archi-
tectural actions and per-component costs, enabling fast
design-space exploration. MIAO prices each configuration
with a compositional area and energy model calibrated on
post-synthesis processing elements, so that the cost of one
additional mantissa or exponent bit in a single accumulator
row is resolved.
7
CONCLUSION
We present MIAO, a hardware-software co-design frame-
work for jointly optimizing product and accumulator pre-
cision in MX-based systolic arrays.
By modeling row-
dependent rounding sensitivity and hardware cost, MIAO
selects and certifies non-uniform accumulation ladders un-
der a target perplexity constraint. Across MXFP8, MXFP6,
and MXFP4, the selected configurations reduce PE-array
area by 32.0-38.7% and estimated whole-accelerator en-
ergy per operation by 23.7-36.1% relative to all-BF16,
with at most 1.06% additional perplexity degradation be-
yond operand-only quantization. Configurations selected
on TinyLlama transfer to five additional LLMs without re-
tuning, adding at most 1.6% perplexity beyond operand-
only quantization. Future work includes cross-model cali-
bration and extending the optimization to other accelerator
architectures and workloads.

A.4
Zero-Shot Task Accuracy
Table 11 evaluates seven tasks on TinyLlama-1.1B. Se-
lected MXFP8 and MXFP6 configurations change mean
accuracy by at most 0.91 points relative to all-BF16;
MXFP4 loses 2.31, 2.03 and 2.46 points at 8 × 8, 16 × 16
and 32 × 32. Across all nine configurations, selected prod-
uct and accumulator precisions change mean accuracy by
at most 0.7 points relative to operand-only MX.
A.5
Code Generation
Table 5 tests whether the selected configurations hold up
on generation, where errors can compound over many de-
coding steps.
We run Llama-3.1-8B (base) on all 164
HumanEval problems (lm-eval, 0-shot, greedy decoding,
pass@1) at 16×16, with the MLP linears and lm head
through the array and attention in BF16, as in the cross-
model runs. No selected configuration differs significantly
from operand-only MX: the product and accumulator pre-
cisions change pass@1 by +3.7, −3.0 and +0.6 points for
MXFP8, MXFP6 and MXFP4, against a paired 95% inter-
val of about ±5.6–6.7 points. MXFP8 and MXFP6 are no
worse than all-BF16. MXFP4 loses 5.5 points, and that loss
comes from operand quantization (operand-only MXFP4
loses 6.1), not from the selected product and accumulators.
The +6.1 for operand-only MXFP6 is within the spread ex-
pected over these comparisons and is not an improvement
we claim.
