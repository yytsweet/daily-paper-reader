We introduce Z-sight, a unified robot foundation model that couples vision-language-action (VLA) and generative latent
world modeling. Video-based world action models (WAMs) show the value of joint future-and-action generation for control,
yet inherit the heavy cost of video generators. Recent latent-space policies avoid pixel generation, yet typically align
intermediate features or rely on frozen target encoders, decoupling future prediction from the VLM’s native feature space.
Z-sight instead predicts the future directly in the visual token space in which its VLM perceives the present. Coupling a
foresight expert and a continuous action expert with the VLM in a mixture-of-transformers, Z-sight jointly denoises future
spatial tokens and actions at inference without pixel reconstruction or external encoders. Trained on human video and
robot trajectories, Z-sight achieves 81.9% success on SIMPLE (+9.6 points over the strongest baseline) and 73.3% across
six Unitree G1 tasks (+20.0 points) at 110 ms per chunk, while remaining competitive on tabletop benchmarks.

1 Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models transfer visual and linguistic knowledge to robotic control by building policies
on pre-trained VLMs (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b). Recent architectures extend this
paradigm by coupling a VLM backbone with a continuous action expert (Black et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025; Wei
et al., 2026a), combining multimodal reasoning with high-frequency action generation.
In parallel, world action models (WAMs) (Ye et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026) adapt
pre-trained video generators to jointly model future observations and actions, capturing physical dynamics at the cost
of large video diffusion backbones. Recent methods avoid pixel generation by moving future supervision into latent
space, yet either align intermediate policy features to compact embeddings (Zheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) without
generating a future stream, or predict features of frozen target encoders (Zhang et al., 2026b; Sun et al., 2026; Zhang
et al., 2026a) that decouple from the VLM’s native feature space.
We introduce Z-sight, a unified model that brings joint future-and-action generation into the policy’s co-evolving native
visual space (Figure 1). A VLM encodes observations into spatial visual tokens. By supervising future predictions against
an exponential-moving-average (EMA) teacher of this same vision encoder, perception and prediction remain aligned
throughout pre-training and downstream adaptation, without introducing a video generator or an external target encoder.
As shown in Figure 2, Z-sight couples the VLM stream with a foresight expert and a continuous action expert in a
mixture-of-transformers (MoT) (Liang et al., 2025). Conditioned on the VLM’s instruction, state, and observation history,
the foresight expert predicts future spatial tokens via flow matching while the action expert attends directly to these
predicted features. During inference, both experts jointly denoise future visual tokens and actions, informing control with
online latent dynamics at high efficiency.
We train Z-sight on egocentric human video and cross-embodiment robot trajectories, then adapt it across single-arm,
bimanual, and humanoid settings spanning LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026), SIMPLE (Wei
et al., 2026b), and six physical Unitree G1 tasks. Z-sight achieves strong humanoid loco-manipulation performance,

2 Related Work
Vision-language-action models. Vision-language-action (VLA) models adapt pre-trained VLMs to output discrete
action tokens (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b; Pertsch et al., 2025) or continuous actions
via flow-matching experts (Black et al., 2025a; Bjorck et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026a). Leveraging internet-scale
multimodal pre-training, these policies exhibit strong semantic reasoning and instruction following. To preserve
pre-trained representations while learning continuous control, Driess et al. (2025) employ knowledge insulation, training
the VLM with discrete action tokens while a flow expert learns continuous actions from detached VLM features. While
effective at mapping current context to actions, standard VLAs rely primarily on action labels, providing a relatively
sparse supervision signal. Z-sight builds upon this foundation and augments it with generative latent world modeling.

future video alongside actions, either jointly denoising video and actions on video diffusion backbones (Ye et al.,

Latent world modeling and predictive policies. Joint-embedding predictive architectures learn representations by
predicting features rather than pixels (Baevski et al., 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024) against EMA
teachers (Grill et al., 2020), enabling planning with pre-trained visual features (Zhou et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025).
In robotic control (Lyu et al., 2026; Nilaksh et al., 2026), implicit alignment methods align internal policy activations
with compact future embeddings (Zheng et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025) or spatial and world-model features (Li et al.,
2026; Dao et al., 2026) without generating a spatial future stream. Explicit latent predictors instead supervise world
tokens with frozen external teachers in DreamVLA (Zhang et al., 2026b), or frozen V-JEPA 2 and SigLIP encoders
in VLA-JEPA (Sun et al., 2026) and UniJEPA (Zhang et al., 2026a). Instead, Z-sight models future states as dense
spatial tokens in the co-evolving perceptual space of the policy’s own vision encoder, jointly denoising future tokens and
continuous actions via flow matching (Appendix G).
3 Methodology
Z-sight predicts the future in the same visual representation space in which it perceives the present and uses that prediction
to generate actions. At time t, given a language instruction ℓ, proprioceptive state st, and multi-camera observation
history Ot, the policy outputs an action chunk At = (at, . . . , at+H−1). During training, the future observations within
the chunk, O+
t , serve as an additional supervision signal that is unavailable at test time.

3.2 Training Recipe
We train Z-sight in three stages, comprising VLM pre-training on egocentric human video, full-model mid-training on
cross-embodiment robot data with human replay, and post-training on the target embodiment (Figure 4, Appendix B).
During pre-training and mid-training, human hands and humanoids use a 48-D task-space hand representation (Wei et al.,
2026a), while other robots use end-effector poses with gripper or hand commands. Actions are expressed relative to the
current state within each chunk and padded to a shared dimension with per-channel validity masks.
Pre-training on human video. We train only the VLM with LFAST on ∼2,000 hours of video from EgoDex (Hoque et al.,
2026) and EgoVerse (Punamiya et al., 2026). Hand trajectories are expressed in the current frame to factor out head
motion, low-confidence joints are masked, and trajectories are smoothed with a validity-weighted temporal Gaussian
filter to suppress frame-to-frame tracking jitter in FAST tokens (Appendix B.2).

Mid-training on cross-embodiment data. We introduce the foresight and action experts and optimize LFAST + Lflow +
λvLview under knowledge insulation on ∼2,750 hours from AgiBot World (AgiBot-World-Contributors et al., 2025)
and Humanoid Everyday (Zhao et al., 2026), deriving task-space poses for joint-only humanoid logs via forward
kinematics (Wei et al., 2026a) and filtering idle segments. Replaying human video at 20% of the sampling mass retains
pre-trained priors and supplies diverse visual dynamics.
Post-training on the target embodiment. On target demonstrations, we drop LFAST and optimize Lflow + λvLview.
Action gradients update the VLM, state projection, and experts, while Lview updates only the foresight expert with its
gradient into the VLM blocked. The EMA teacher continues tracking the adapting vision encoder.
4 Experiments
Z-sight jointly denoises future visual tokens and continuous actions within the VLM’s native feature space. Our
experiments evaluate the practical benefits and underlying mechanisms of this design. Specifically, we aim to answer four
key questions. (1) How does Z-sight perform compared to strong VLAs and video-based WAMs? (Section 4.2) (2) How
much do continued future supervision and the co-evolving native target design contribute relative to implicit alignment
and frozen external encoders? (Section 4.3) (3) What scene changes does the foresight stream capture, and how does the
action expert rely on its content and uncertainty during execution? (Section 4.4) (4) Can Z-sight retain online foresight at
high efficiency? (Section 4.5)
4.1 Evaluation Setup
Benchmarks. We evaluate Z-sight on three simulation benchmarks and a physical humanoid. SIMPLE (Wei et al.,
2026b) evaluates whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation across three levels of variation in materials, distractors,
lighting, and target positions (L0–L2). On a physical Unitree G1, we evaluate six tasks spanning tabletop manipulation,
object transport, and multi-stage loco-manipulation, reporting success rates and stage-based progress scores (Appendices C
and C.2). LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) tests single-arm manipulation across spatial, object, goal, and long-horizon suites,
while RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026) evaluates bimanual manipulation over 50 tasks trained on clean demonstrations
and tested under clean (C2C) and randomized (C2R) conditions.
Baselines. Our primary baselines span representative VLAs (π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a), GR00T N1.7 (NVIDIA, 2026),
and Ψ0 (Wei et al., 2026a)) and a video-based WAM (Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026)). Additional tabletop baselines and
adaptation protocols are detailed in Appendices D and E.
4.2 Performance across Embodiments
Because our pre-training and mid-training incorporate human egocentric video and humanoid trajectories, we first
evaluate whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation in simulation and the real world, then test whether the same model
extends to single-arm and bimanual tabletop manipulation.
Humanoid loco-manipulation in SIMPLE. All four methods are adapted on the same 17-task SIMPLE dataset and
evaluated over three passes on the 12 teleoperation tasks (full protocol and motion-planned task results in Appendix E.1).
Figure 5a shows that Z-sight achieves 81.9% overall success, outperforming GR00T N1.7 by 9.6 points, Fast-WAM by
15.9 points, and Ψ0 by 16.1 points, while leading at every difficulty level (86.7% on L0, 85.8% on L1, and 73.1% on L2).
Across individual tasks in Figure 5b, Z-sight ranks first or tied on 11 of the 12 tasks, with the largest margins on tasks
requiring coupled navigation, whole-body bending, or articulated contact such as LocomotionPickBetweenTables (84.4%
vs. 73.3% for Ψ0 and 53.3% for GR00T N1.7), PickAndPlaceAndHugContainer (53.3% vs. 38.9%), and OpenFaucet
(71.1% vs. 58.9%). While action-only VLAs and test-time foresight-free Fast-WAM degrade sharply under target-position
randomization at L2, co-denoising future spatial tokens helps Z-sight sustain coordinated whole-body reaching and
contact transitions.
Real-world humanoid loco-manipulation. Figures 6 and 7 present our evaluation across six everyday tasks on a
physical Unitree G1 with 10 trials per task. Z-sight reaches 73.3% average success (44/60 trials) and 81.7% progress,
outperforming the strongest baseline Fast-WAM (53.3% success, 63.8% progress) as well as Ψ0 (40.0%), GR00T N1.7
(36.7%), and π0.5 (26.7%), while matching or exceeding every baseline on every task. The advantage is most pronounced
on the four multi-stage tasks (Chair, Book, Trash, and Fruit), where Z-sight averages 72.5% success compared with
50.0% for Fast-WAM and at most 32.5% for the VLA baselines, completing both four-stage tasks (Trash and Fruit) in 7
of 10 trials. Whereas VLA baselines frequently stall after completing the initial subgoal, online native-space foresight
helps Z-sight carry sequential stage transitions through to completion.

4.3 Ablating Foresight and Prediction Design
Starting from the shared mid-trained checkpoint, we first isolate the effect of continued future-feature supervision during
target-domain adaptation, holding demonstrations and optimization schedules fixed within each benchmark. No view loss
keeps the foresight expert and token stream while setting λv = 0, so the branch is updated only by action-loss gradients.
No foresight branch removes the foresight stream altogether, reducing the policy to a standard VLA.
Across all four settings in Table 3, removing the view loss consistently degrades performance, dropping success from
73.1% to 25.0% on SIMPLE Hard, from 90% to 30% on G1 Dog, from 97.70% to 74.65% on LIBERO, and from 40.1%
to 11.6% on RoboTwin C2R. Removing the foresight branch entirely also trails the full model (e.g., 68.3% vs. 73.1% on
SIMPLE Hard and 22.0% vs. 40.1% on RoboTwin C2R). Because the no-view-loss variant preserves parameter count and
architecture, these gains stem from continued predictive supervision rather than extra model capacity.

4.4 Foresight Content and Policy Reliance
Prediction quality and action error. On 11,676 held-out observation-to-future windows across all six physical G1 tasks,
Table 5 compares the predicted future tokens α against the EMA target encoder’s ground-truth future tokens y. Z-sight
achieves 0.955 cosine similarity, outperforming both copying present tokens β (0.921) and an unrelated future γ (0.850),
while reaching 0.646 cosine similarity on feature changes (α −β, y −β). Moreover, holding initial action noise fixed on
the same trajectories, replacing α with ground-truth future tokens y lowers normalized action MSE against demonstrations
by 11.9% (from 0.0495 to 0.0436), confirming that more accurate future features directly improve sampled actions.
Closed-loop interventions. To disentangle reliance on predicted content from the stream’s noise-level cue, Table 6
evaluates test-time interventions on the foresight stream with fixed weights, with extended benchmarks in Appendix F.
Injecting Gaussian noise while labeling the stream unreliable (σ = 1) retains 61.7% success on SIMPLE Hard and
79.0/33.9% on RoboTwin, as training with σ ≥τ teaches the policy to fall back on current observations under high declared
uncertainty. Changing only the label of that noise to the shared schedule σ = τ drops success to 16.7% and 50.5/15.3%.
Under the same σ = τ schedule, replacing the stream with another episode’s valid prediction (Swap) preserves token
statistics and the denoising schedule yet collapses success to 11.7% on SIMPLE and 7.5/4.0% on RoboTwin, matching the
severe drops from asserting a static scene (Copy, 5.8% and 7.8/2.0%) or blocking action-to-foresight attention (Detach,
7.5% and 5.8/1.8%). Thus, closed-loop control depends on both declared uncertainty and observation-specific predicted
content.
Joint versus sequential denoising. Because Z-sight updates future tokens and actions simultaneously, a natural question
is whether conditioning actions on intermediate noisy predictions hurts control compared with waiting for a fully denoised
future estimate. Table 7 evaluates the same weights under sequential inference, completing 10 future-denoising steps
before running 10 action-denoising steps. Sequential inference yields similar results on LIBERO (97.05% vs. 97.70%)
and RoboTwin (82.6/37.9% vs. 82.4/40.1%), but drops SIMPLE Hard success by 8.9 points from 73.1% to 64.2%

4.5 Inference Efficiency
We time in-process action-chunk generation on a single deployment RTX 4090 over 100 CUDA-synchronized calls on
480 × 640 observations after 100 warm-up calls (Appendix D.2). As shown in Table 8, Z-sight computes a 30-step
chunk in 109.5 ms, faster than π0.5 (120.5 ms), Ψ0 (149.7 ms), and Fast-WAM (155.9 ms), even though Fast-WAM skips
future generation at test time while Z-sight retains online foresight. GR00T N1.7 runs in 60.0 ms using four denoising
steps without future generation. At our 10 Hz control rate, a 30-step chunk spans 3 s of execution for 110 ms of model
compute.
5 Conclusion
We presented Z-sight, a unified vision-language-action and latent world model. By jointly denoising future spatial tokens
and continuous actions in the VLM’s co-evolving perceptual space, Z-sight unifies semantic reasoning, physical foresight,
and control without video generators or external encoders. Across simulation and real-world humanoid tasks, Z-sight
substantially improves whole-body loco-manipulation over VLA and WAM baselines at high efficiency, and controlled
interventions confirm that the policy actively relies on its predicted dynamics. More broadly, our findings show that a
VLM’s perceptual representations, when adapted jointly with foresight and control, provide an effective substrate for
embodied world modeling.
Limitations. The foresight stream currently predicts the future from the observation alone. Conditioning it on candidate
actions would let Z-sight compare the outcomes of different actions and open the door to test-time planning in its own
representation space.

C.1 Hardware and SONIC Teleoperation-Deployment Pipeline
We deploy real-world policies on a Unitree G1 humanoid equipped with two 7-DoF Dex3-1 dexterous hands and a
single egocentric head camera. The default head-mounted Intel RealSense D435i provides a narrow field of view suited
to tabletop tasks (Dog and Puffs), whereas tasks involving locomotion (Chair, Book, Trash, and Fruit) require wider
peripheral visibility and use a head-mounted ZED 2i camera. Each task uses the same camera during data collection and
evaluation.
Rather than outputting raw whole-body joint targets, high-level policies act through SONIC (Luo et al., 2026), a learned
whole-body motion-tracking controller. Each 78-D action comprises a latent SONIC motion token paired with joint
targets for both dexterous hands, while the 43-D proprioceptive state comprises body joint positions and commanded
hand targets. Demonstrations are collected through the identical control interface by a teleoperator wearing a VR headset
whose tracked upper-body poses, hand commands, and locomotion inputs drive SONIC at 50 Hz, recording synchronized
camera frames, proprioception, motion tokens, and hand targets at 10 Hz. During deployment, Z-sight runs on a single
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU in synchronous closed-loop execution, streaming complete 30-step action chunks at 10 Hz
while the SONIC decoder tracks each token in a 50 Hz balance and whole-body control loop.
C.2 Evaluation Tasks, Scoring, and Stage-Wise Outcomes
We collect 557 teleoperated G1 demonstrations in total, comprising 355 demonstrations across six evaluated tasks
(49–70 per task, Table C.1) and 202 demonstrations across four additional household skills (such as picking up a bottle,
stacking plates, and pouring water) that supply related manipulation priors during multi-task training. We selected the six
evaluation tasks for skill diversity and difficulty, spanning short tabletop pick-and-place, object transport, articulated-object
manipulation, and locomotion coupled to contact. Within each task, episodes are partitioned at the episode level using a
deterministic hash (seed 42, nominal 10% validation allocation with the remainder used for training), and all methods are
evaluated from their final training checkpoint over 10 trials per task from matched initial configurations.
As summarized in Table C.1, the six evaluation tasks span short tabletop pick-and-place (Dog, Puffs, 16–28 s mean
duration) and long-horizon multi-stage loco-manipulation (Chair, Book, Trash, Fruit, 35–84 s mean duration). Each
stage carries equal partial progress credit (0.5 for two-stage tasks and 0.25 for four-stage tasks), and full task success
requires completing all stages. Table C.2 reports the complete trial-count distributions over completed subgoals across all
six tasks and five evaluated policies.

E Simulation Benchmark Protocols and Detailed Results
E.1 SIMPLE Whole-Body Loco-Manipulation
SIMPLE (Wei et al., 2026b) evaluates whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation through a 50 Hz decoupled whole-body
controller (36-D actions, 32-D proprioceptive state). All methods train on the same 17-task dataset and are evaluated
across three levels of domain randomization, where L0 varies materials and distractors, L1 adds lighting variation,
and L2 (i.e., Hard) varies target initial positions (10 episodes per task and level per pass). The dataset comprises 12
teleoperation (Teleop) tasks and 5 motion-planned (MP) tasks. Across all trained policies, we observe a substantial
performance gap between the Teleop and MP tasks, which we attribute to their distinct demonstration distributions. For a
meaningful comparison, our main experiments focus on the 12-task Teleop suite, averaged over three evaluation passes of
one checkpoint per method (1,080 episodes total, measuring evaluation variability). For computational tractability across
multiple trained variants, the matched mechanism ablations and interventions on SIMPLE in Tables 3, 4, 6, and 7 evaluate
one pass on the 12 Teleop tasks at L2 (10 episodes per task, 120 episodes per entry). For completeness, Tables E.1
and E.2 report aggregate and per-task success rates across both Teleop and MP suites for all 17 tasks.

F.1 Held-Out Prediction Quality and Offline Action-Error Protocol
For the offline evaluation in Table 5, we test the deployed physical G1 checkpoint and its EMA target encoder on
episode-level held-out trajectories across all six G1 tasks. Out of 12,480 candidate observation-to-future windows, 804
extend past the end of an episode without valid future frames and are excluded, leaving 11,676 valid windows. Across
individual tasks, the predicted-over-present similarity gain cos(α, y) −cos(β, y) is positive on every one of the six tasks,
with α −β capturing both object displacement and egocentric head-camera motion.
To isolate how future-token accuracy affects action generation on the same held-out windows, we compute normalized
open-loop action-chunk MSE against demonstration chunks while holding the initial Gaussian action-noise sample
fixed across conditions and varying only the future-feature stream supplied to the action expert (Table F.1). Noising
ground-truth EMA future tokens y to the shared denoising level σ = τ reduces action MSE on every task individually,
whereas supplying clean present-frame tokens β (σ = 0) raises action MSE by 28.3%.

G Discussion of Related Predictive Robot Policies
While recent robotic policies increasingly incorporate future visual signals beyond pixel reconstruction, they differ
fundamentally in how future states are represented and coupled to action generation. Implicit world-modeling methods
such as FLARE (Zheng et al., 2025) (adopted in GR00T N1.5 (NVIDIA, 2025)) align intermediate activations of an action
diffusion transformer toward compact action-aware embeddings via cosine loss. This shapes internal representations
during training without constructing an explicit spatial future stream that participates in action denoising. Conversely,
video-based WAMs (Ye et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026) and codec-based unified policies such as F1 (Lv
et al., 2025) and InternVLA-A1 (Cai et al., 2026a) generate discrete tokens or continuous latents of a separate pre-trained
image or video tokenizer. Even when explicit pixel decoding is omitted along the action path at test time, future generation
operates in a separate generative codec space rather than the visual representation space that grounds VLM perception.
Among policies that explicitly predict latent visual features, existing designs rely on frozen target encoders.
DreamVLA (Zhang et al., 2026b) supervises world queries with dynamic regions, depth, and semantic features from
frozen external teachers and omits its prediction decoders at deployment, while VLA-JEPA (Sun et al., 2026) predicts
latent states against a frozen external V-JEPA 2 video encoder. Closer in architecture is UniJEPA (Zhang et al., 2026a),
which also attaches a feature-prediction expert and an action expert to a PaliGemma VLM in a mixture-of-transformers,
yet differs from Z-sight across target space, prediction objective, and action coupling. First, UniJEPA predicts future
features in the space of a frozen target encoder (an initial SigLIP copy or DINOv3), while fine-tuning the policy’s own
vision encoder on robot data. Its action expert therefore reads current observations and predicted futures from two
different spaces that drift apart during training. Second, its predictor uses deterministic MSE regression, collapsing
multimodal futures toward their conditional mean rather than modeling their distribution. Third, its noise-free foresight
stream is computed once in a prefix pass and cached prior to action denoising. Z-sight differs on all three axes: an online
EMA of the policy’s own causal video encoder keeps current observations and predicted futures in one co-evolving
feature space, foresight tokens are generated via conditional flow matching, and foresight and continuous actions are
denoised jointly across shared Euler steps.
