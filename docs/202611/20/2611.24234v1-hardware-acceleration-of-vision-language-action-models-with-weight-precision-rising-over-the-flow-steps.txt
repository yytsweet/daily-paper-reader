Hardware Acceleration of Vision-Language-Action Models with
Weight Precision Rising over the Flow Steps
Robin Geens, Simon Haepers, Jonas Crols, Chao Fang, Marian Verhelst
MICAS, KU Leuven, Belgium
Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as GR00T control robots
through an action head: a diffusion transformer that turns noise into
a chunk of actions in a few flow-matching steps. Every step re-reads
all of the head’s weights from DRAM but applies each weight to
only about fifty tokens. Inference is therefore memory-bound, and
its latency and energy are largely determined by the total number
of transferred weight bits. We show that later flow steps attenuate
the quantization errors of earlier steps, so the weight precision of
an action head should rise over the steps. With plain per-channel
rounding and without calibration or training, widths rising over
the steps from 2 to 4 bits preserve the closed-loop success of robot
policies. These widths transfer 25% fewer bits than 4-bit weights at
every step. However, we show that on Jetson AGX Thor, current
GPU kernels cannot exploit this per-step precision due to fixed
overheads. We therefore present a programmable accelerator whose
latency and energy both scale with the transferred weight bits,
because it keeps its DRAM link saturated throughout an inference
at every width. It decodes entropy-coded weights for bit-serial
sub-arrays at the DRAM rate. Because the width changes per step,
a run-time allocator divides these sub-arrays between the linear
layers and the attention to balance their workloads. The same sub-
arrays are thus capable of running action heads of different families
and sizes, as well as a vision-language model. On the DRAM side, a
requantizer and an entropy encoder at each bank let one stored 8-
bit copy replace the copies of all widths. Simulated with LPDDR5X
timing, our RTL implementation runs the GR00T N1.6 action head
in 8.02 ms per inference with 2, 3, 3 and 4 bits in its four flow steps.
With a copy per width and 50% of Thor’s memory bandwidth, the
accelerator runs this schedule 1.7× faster than Thor in NVFP4, at
9.1× less energy and equal closed-loop success.

Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models are the state-of-the-art in
general-purpose robot manipulation [8, 9, 43, 67]. Most VLAs split
the policy in two, as Figure 2 shows: a vision-language model (VLM),
System 2, interprets the scene and instruction, encoding them into
tokens. Conditioned on these tokens and the robot state, a diffusion-
transformer (DiT) action head, System 1, turns noise into a chunk
of actions in a limited number of flow-matching steps [54, 57, 69].
System 1 is the latency-critical part of a dual-system policy,
since it closes the control loop once per chunk. Even when the
robot executes one chunk while the next is computed [10, 75], the
latency of System 1 bounds how often the robot can react to a new
observation. To react before every action, System 1 must run at the
control rate, such as 50 Hz [9]. System 2, in contrast, tolerates more
latency and runs at 10 Hz or less [21, 67]. Like recent accelerators
for robot policies [58, 103], this work therefore accelerates System 1.

The action head is memory-bound: every flow step re-reads all
of its weights but applies each weight to only the roughly fifty
tokens of one chunk. As a result, the GR00T N1.6 head streams
gigabytes of weights per inference in BF16, as Table 1 shows. Today,
robot policies run on edge GPUs [21, 64, 94] such as NVIDIA’s
Jetson AGX Thor, which draws up to 130 W [65]. NVIDIA reports
10.9 Hz for GR00T N1.6 on Thor with TensorRT, and the action head
takes over half of each inference [64]. One inference per action at
50 Hz would need more than the GPU’s whole memory bandwidth
in BF16. Moreover, the GPU of an autonomous mobile robot can
draw over twice the power of its motors [56]. A lower energy per
inference thus extends how long the robot runs on one charge.
Weight quantization reduces the bits per weight. Prior work
quantizes current policies to uniform 4-bit weights, with the same
width at every flow step [12, 31]. Below 4 bits, however, uniformly
rounded weights lose closed-loop success with and without calibra-
tion.
We observe that the flow steps do not need equal precision. Be-
cause an action head refines a wide range of starting noise into
almost the same action, its later steps also attenuate the quantiza-
tion error of an early step. An error of the last step, in contrast,
reaches the robot in full. The weight width should thus rise over the

2.2
Memory-Bound Inference
Since a robot serves one observation at a time, every flow step
applies all weights of the action head to the roughly fifty tokens of
a single chunk. With about fifty multiply-accumulate operations
(MACs) per weight and step, the operational intensity lies far below
the roofline ridge point of GPU tensor cores and accelerator arrays.
The inference is therefore memory-bound and takes at least the
DRAM floor, the time to read its bytes once at the peak DRAM
bandwidth. Its energy is at least that of these reads, which can cost
79× more energy per bit than an INT8 multiplication in 7 nm [39,
42, 61].
We measure the weight traffic of an inference in bit-steps, the bits
read per weight summed over all 𝑁steps. A schedule [𝑏1, . . . ,𝑏𝑁]
of per-step widths reads Í

steps. For example, the schedule [2,3,3,4] reads 12 bit-steps, 5.3×
fewer than BF16. Entropy-coded weights (Section 5.2) take even
fewer bit-steps than the sum of the widths.
Insight 1. Reading weight bits from DRAM dominates the la-
tency and energy of an inference, so reducing its bit-steps can
reduce both proportionally.

3.2
Attenuation of Early Quantization Errors
Every flow step reads the same weights, but the steps do not neces-
sarily need the same precision. An action head’s output depends
on the observation but barely on the starting noise. Across noise
draws, the actions of the three policies differ by only a few percent
of the noise magnitude. The flow thus maps a wide range of start-
ing points to almost the same action. It must therefore attenuate
any deviation of the intermediate states, including the quantization
error of an early step.
To test this intuition, we quantize the weights of only one step
at a time to 3 bits and compare the final action with that of the
unquantized model. We run this comparison open loop on 45 to
60 model calls per benchmark in Figure 3, recorded in closed-loop
episodes. The error is the root-mean-square difference between the
two actions over four noise draws, averaged geometrically over the
calls. Every action dimension enters this error normalized by its
range in the training data, so that no single dimension dominates
it. Figure 3a shows that the error due to quantizing the last step
exceeds that of the first by one to two orders of magnitude. A
uniform width thus spends most of its bits on steps whose errors
barely reach the action.
Two effects can make the last step more sensitive than the first.
Quantizing step 𝑘perturbs its velocity and leaves an error 𝑠𝑘in
the state x𝑘(Equation 1). The later steps carry this state error to
the action, where it becomes the output error 𝑒𝑘. First, quantizing
a late step may leave a larger state error. Second, the later steps
may attenuate the state error of an early step before it reaches
the action. To separate the two effects, we define the gain of step
𝑘as 𝑔𝑘= 𝑒𝑘/𝑠𝑘, with both errors taken from the same run. The
state after the last step is the output action, so 𝑔𝑁= 1. On all three
policies, the state error resulting from quantizing the last step is
only two to three times that of the first step, as Figure 3a shows.
Figure 3b shows that the remaining steps, in contrast, attenuate an
error of the first step to 23% or less. This attenuation thus accounts
for most of the difference in sensitivity between the first and the
last step. The larger state error of the last step accounts for the rest.
Action heads differ in this respect from image diffusion models,
whose output depends strongly on the starting noise. The images of
the flow-matching DiT SANA-1.6B [91], for example, differ across
noise draws by 54% of the noise magnitude. Figure 3a shows that
the output error of SANA-1.6B exceeds its state error at every step
but the last. Its remaining steps thus amplify an early error. Per-step
quantizers for image diffusion accordingly give the noisiest steps
the most weight bits, as in TMPDiff [49] and TASQ [28]. An action
head, in contrast, needs its bits on the last steps.
Insight 2. Because an action head’s output barely depends on
the starting noise, its early steps tolerate more quantization than
its late ones, and bit widths should rise over the steps.

kernel overhead that does not decrease as the weights narrow.
Insight 4. Below 4 bits, current GPU kernel stacks are bound by
fixed overheads, so sub-4-bit precision needs hardware whose
entire latency scales with the bit-steps.
To remove the first factor, dedicated hardware must apply the
normalization, rounding, activation functions and residual additions
as the activations enter or leave the linear layers. It must also run
the attention in parallel with the weight stream. To remove the
second factor, it must process the weights faster than the DRAM
delivers them, at every width from 2 to 8 bits. Moreover, it must
adapt to a dynamic workload. The operational intensity of the linear
layers changes with the width of each step, and the share of the
attention changes between models and contexts. Section 5 presents
such an accelerator.
5
Accelerator Architecture
Figure 5 shows the accelerator. Every flow step streams all weights
of the action head from DRAM through a FIFO and a decoder into
the array. Only the activations of one chunk, the keys and values of
the current layer and the constants reside on chip. We design the
accelerator to keep the DRAM channels saturated at every width,
so that the time of an inference approaches its DRAM floor.
Because the best schedule differs between policies and bench-
mark suites, one datapath must run each flow step at any width
from 2 to 8 bits. An output-stationary bit-serial array [40, 72] meets
this requirement. Consuming one bit of each weight per cycle, it
runs a step of any width as the same loop of 𝑏cycles. Since stream-
ing a step’s weights also takes time proportional to 𝑏, the array
keeps the DRAM channels saturated at every width.
5.1
Memory Interface
The proposed accelerator has a 128-bit memory interface of eight
x16 LPDDR5X-8533 channels with 8 GB of capacity and a peak
bandwidth of 136.5 GB/s, 50% of Thor’s. We target 50 action-head
inferences per second, one per action at a 50 Hz control rate [9].
This target suffices for most applications, since policies typically
execute several actions of each chunk [9, 10, 67]. The weight FIFO
lets the DRAM continue to stream weights while the accelerator
processes layers that require no weights, such as LayerNorm and
the attention. A DMA engine moves the inputs of an inference and
the keys and values that a layer produces. System 2’s keys and
values arrive through the K/V FIFO.
5.2
Entropy-Coded Weight Streams
Every linear layer streams from DRAM at the width of its step, as
one contiguous stream located through an address table per layer
and width. The DRAM either stores one such stream per width or
produces it from one stored 8-bit copy, as proposed in Section 5.6.
Per-channel rounding scales each row by its largest weight, which
lies far out in the tail of the distribution. Most weights are thus
small compared with the step between two levels, and 56% of the
3-bit weights of GR00T N1.6 round to zero. We therefore Huffman-
code the streams, with code words of at most 12 bits. Each layer
and width has one canonical code, stored at the start of its stream.
Since every code word takes at least one bit, a code word covers
a pair of neighboring weights at 2 and 3 bits. A decoder between
the weight FIFO and the array decodes the code words in parallel,
faster than the DRAM delivers them at every width. Because the
code is lossless, coding does not alter the closed-loop success of the
quantized policies.
Figure 6b shows the coded bits per weight, which come within
2.3% of the rounded weights’ entropy for every policy and width.
The entropy code thus further reduces the bit-steps of a [2,3,3,4]
inference of GR00T N1.6 from 12 to 7.98, as Figure 6a shows.
Microscaling formats such as NVFP4 [66] lower the rounding
error at 4 bits with an 8-bit scale per block of 16 weights instead
of one per row. Each block maps its largest weight to the top level,
so its weights spread over all levels and barely compress. Weights
rounded per channel to 5 bits, in contrast, concentrate near zero,
so the entropy code saves more than one bit per weight. Coded
per-channel rounding at 5 bits therefore reads fewer bits than coded
NVFP4 with its scales, at a lower output error.
Insight 5. Microscaling formats lower the output error but are
less compressible, so entropy-coded weights reach the same
error in fewer bits with one scale per row.

5.3
Flexible Bit-Serial Fabric
The compute fabric is a bit-serial tree array of processing elements
(PEs), 128 output channels by 51 tokens, enough for the chunk of
every action head in Table 1. Per cycle, the array consumes bit 𝑘of
16 weights of every output channel. As Figure 6b shows, the code
stores a weight in as little as 55% of its width. At the 1 GHz clock,
the decoder thus emits up to 1.8× the 1092 bits that the DRAM
delivers per cycle, about 2000 bits. We therefore size the array to
consume 2048 weight bits per cycle, so that it keeps up with the
decoder at every width. Since every weight bit meets 51 tokens, the
array performs 104448 bit-MACs per cycle, each of which multiplies
one weight bit with an INT8 activation. With per-step precision,
the compute that the weight stream requires changes between flow
steps, because the decoder emits the most bits per cycle at the
most compressible widths. The compute of the attention varies
between model families. For example, 𝜋0.5 performs seven to nine
times as many attention MACs per flow step as GR00T N1.6. To
maximize flexibility and hardware utilization, the weight stream
and the attention share the array. We split the array into eight
sub-arrays of 16 output channels each, which a run-time allocator
divides between the two.
Figure 5a shows a PE, which serves one output channel and
one token. Per cycle, it multiplies bit 𝑘of 16 weights of its output
channel with the INT8 activations of its token at the same 16 input
channels. An adder tree sums the 16 products. The PE shifts the sum
to the significance of bit 𝑘and adds it into a 32-bit accumulator,
or subtracts it for the two’s-complement sign bit. Because one
accumulator serves 16 input channels, the PE takes less area and
energy per bit-MAC than a bit-serial PE that accumulates every
product separately. The array computes a layer in passes, each over
one group of output channels. Two accumulator banks let one pass
drain while the next fills. Since the allocator assigns each sub-array
to the weight stream or to the attention, a sub-array runs every
pass in one of two modes, stream mode or attention mode.
Stream mode. For the projections, a sub-array takes the decoded
weights of its 16 output channels and the activations from the read
port, as Figure 5 shows. The read port feeds the activations of all
51 tokens from the token buffers to every sub-array of the stream,
16 input channels per token and cycle. It rounds the activations of
each token to INT8 with a scale from the token’s largest magnitude.
The drain epilogue (Figure 5c) applies the elementwise operations
that a GPU runs as separate kernels. It processes one token row per
cycle while the array computes the next pass, so these operations
do not stall the weight stream. A LayerNorm unit of the same width
normalizes the token rows with statistics that the drain gathers, so
that normalization occupies no sub-array. The output projection is
pipelined with the attention in four tiles of input channels, so that
earlier tiles run in parallel with the attention of later heads.
5


Attention mode. For the attention, the keys or values take the
place of the weights, and the queries or the softmax probabilities
P take that of the activations. A sub-array takes one bit of each
key or value per cycle from a 16 × 16 block in its block register, as
Figure 5b shows. Its row register holds the matching 16 elements of
the query or of P for each of the 51 tokens. A pass of 𝑄𝐾⊤puts 16
keys on the output channels, and a pass of 𝑃𝑉puts 16 columns of
the head dimension there. A Q/P port rounds Q to 8 bits and loads
Q or the two bytes of P into the row registers. Each tile of a head’s
keys passes in order through 𝑄𝐾⊤, an online-softmax engine and
𝑃𝑉. Because the integer accumulators hold both products exactly,
the attention matches a dedicated attention unit bit for bit.
Control core. To run action heads of different sizes and ar-
chitectures, a Snitch RISC-V core [99] runs each inference as a
program that issues commands to the engines without delaying
them. Through double-buffered command registers, the core writes
the next command of an engine while the engine still executes the
current one. Each command thus starts in the cycle that its engine
becomes free. A command can even start before the command that
produces its inputs has finished. Each of its reads then waits at
a read gate only until the pass that produces the read’s data has
drained. A dependent GPU kernel, by contrast, reads its inputs only
after the whole kernel before it has finished. The attention thus
runs in parallel with the weight stream instead of adding to the
fixed overheads of Section 4. A new schedule, step count or context
length changes only the commands that the program issues. A new
model family is thus a new program, so a single fabricated chip can
run GR00T N1.6, GR00T N1.5 and 𝜋0.5.
5.4
Run-Time Sub-Array Allocation
To keep the DRAM channels saturated, the attention runs in parallel
with the weight stream, on the sub-arrays that the projections do
not need. At each width, a projection needs its sub-array minimum,
the fewest sub-arrays that keep up with the DRAM. A hardwired
partition, as in Figure 7a, would be the same for every policy and
width. Since the needs of the weight stream and the attention vary
with both, it would leave the sub-arrays of one idle while the other
lacks sub-arrays. Alternatively, the compiler can allocate the sub-
arrays statically, as in Figure 7b. Each projection then takes as many
sub-arrays as its sub-array minimum, starting from the first, and
the attention takes the others. Whenever the projections halt, the
DRAM keeps filling the weight FIFO and idles once it is full. For
example, a tile of the output projection multiplies the outputs of a
group of attention heads, so it halts until their attention ends. After
the halt, the FIFO regains room only if the projections consume the
weights faster than the DRAM delivers them. At their sub-array
minimum, however, they consume them about as fast. With the
static allocation, the FIFO thus remains full after the first halt, and
the DRAM idles during every later halt.
Our run-time allocator (Figure 7d) instead decides at the start of
every pass how many sub-arrays the weight stream takes, while
each attention pass takes the free ones. A weight FIFO at least
25% full shows that the DRAM runs ahead of the projections. If
the attention then has no pass ready and no softmax in flight, the
stream’s pass also takes the free sub-arrays beyond its minimum,
up to the first busy one. Otherwise, it runs on its minimum.

5.5
Running System 2
The same accelerator can also run the VLM of GR00T N1.6 bit-
exactly. This System 2 consists of SigLIP2 on each camera’s image
and the first 16 layers of Qwen3-1.7B. Its 1.33 billion weights apply
to hundreds of tokens per layer instead of the 51 tokens of the array.
Run in groups of 51 tokens, System 2 would re-stream every weight
for every group. It therefore runs weight-stationary: each weight
tile is loaded once into a buffer per sub-array and replayed for every
group. The activations stream in from DRAM, whereas the partial
sums stay on chip. Since every layer writes its outputs to DRAM,
System 2 can pause at any layer boundary, so that action-head
inferences can run in between.
5.6
Quantization-in-DRAM
A deployment that switches between step counts and precision
schedules may use any width from 2 to 8 bits. Our per-channel
rounding uses a separate scale for every width, so a narrower weight
is not a truncation of a wider one. Such a deployment therefore
stores a copy of every width in DRAM, 3.3 GB for the copies of
GR00T N1.6 against 0.85 GB for one 8-bit copy. The 𝑏-bit weight,
however, depends only on the 8-bit weight and the 𝑏-bit scale of its
row. Every width can thus be derived from one stored 8-bit copy
while the weights are read. The capacity saved can hold the weights
of a larger model or of System 2.

We therefore propose Quantization-in-DRAM: a DRAM system
that stores one 8-bit copy and requantizes and encodes the weights
at every bank, as illustrated in Figure 8. At every step, the requan-
tizer behind each row buffer rounds the stored bytes to the step’s
width, and the encoder Huffman-codes the result.
Requantizer and encoder. At the 2-bit step, the banks read at
least 4× as many bits as leave the DRAM. The banks of a channel,
however, share one bus to its interface, with the same bandwidth
as the interface. A requantizer at the interface would thus use at
most a quarter of its bandwidth. Every bank therefore holds its
own requantizer and encoder, so that only coded weights cross the
shared bus.
Multiplication in the DRAM. Quantization-in-DRAM adds
only a requantizer and an encoder to each bank. Processing-in-
memory DRAMs instead add MAC units to the banks, as Sam-
sung’s HBM-PIM [48] and LPDDR5X-PIM [34] do, so that no weight
crosses the memory interface. Logic in a DRAM process, however,
is about 10× less dense than in a logic process [17], so MAC units at
every bank are large. They also perform one MAC per weight read,
whereas GR00T N1.6 applies every weight read to 51 tokens, so they
would take far longer than the accelerator. Quantization-in-DRAM
therefore leaves the MACs to the accelerator.

Experimental Methodology
We evaluated the closed-loop success of the precision schedules in
simulated environments (roughly 1700 H100 hours) and the time,
area and energy of the accelerator from its RTL. In closed loop, the
simulated robot executes a task over many model calls.
Policies. GR00T N1.6-3B [23] runs its base checkpoint on Robo-
Casa GR1 and the RoboCasa kitchen. A third-party LIBERO fine-
tune [1] runs LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. NVIDIA’s Bridge, Fractal
and G1 checkpoints run on their benchmarks, and its DROID check-
point on one scene of the DROID simulation. GR00T N1.5 [7] runs
NVIDIA’s LIBERO-Long checkpoint on LIBERO-Long and the Robo-
Casa team’s checkpoint on RoboCasa365. 𝜋0.5 [8] runs LeRobot’s
LIBERO fine-tune [13] at its ten steps. Every quantized policy runs
in a released inference harness, simulator and control loop [13, 64].
Only its action head is replaced by one with the data types and
roundings of the accelerator.
Benchmarks. To compare many schedules, we composed two
screening benchmarks of four tasks each. The first consists of the
four LIBERO-Long [55] tasks on which the released model suc-
ceeded least, since such tasks reveal a loss with few episodes. The
second consists of four RoboCasa GR1 tabletop tasks [63, 67], two
from each of its two task families. We also evaluated eight full
suites in which we ran all tasks of RoboCasa GR1, LIBERO, LIBERO-
Plus [19], the RoboCasa kitchen, SimplerEnv [11, 50, 85] Bridge
and Fractal, and G1. Of RoboCasa365, we ran the 34 target tasks on
which its checkpoint was trained. A task runs at least 50 episodes
per seed. We report the change in success rate against the released
model as the mean over the tasks, each weighted equally. The 90%
interval of this change extends 1.645 standard errors to either side.
Where both policies start each episode from the same state, the
variance of a task’s change comes from the per-episode differences.
Otherwise, it comes from the binomial variances of both success
rates. A schedule preserves success when this interval lies above −3
points, following the equivalence margin of Sujit et al. [82].
RTL simulation. The accelerator was written in Chisel [6],
parameterized in the sizes of its array, token buffers, FIFOs and ports,
and simulated in Verilator [76]. The cycle-accurate DRAM simulator
Ramulator 2 [60] models its LPDDR5X-8533 channels under JEDEC
timing [37]. Each simulated inference replays a model call recorded
in closed loop. Its outputs are bit-exact with an executable model
that follows the roundings of the closed-loop simulations.
Area. Every block of the accelerator was synthesized with Ca-
dence Genus in TSMC 7 nm at 1 GHz. The SRAM came from the
foundry’s memory compiler, and each block’s clock tree was esti-
mated in Cadence Joules. The accelerator area excludes long wires,
the pads and the physical interfaces (PHYs), whose area we esti-
mated from a die shot [68].
Energy. We simulated the same netlists and clock trees at gate
level with FlashReplay in Cadence Joules. The simulation replays
the VCDs of a LIBERO inference at [2,3,3,4] and of System 2 from
the RTL, at the typical corner of 0.75 V and 25 ◦C. We added the
reads, writes and leakage of the SRAM macros from the foundry’s
memory-compiler tables. From Micron’s LPDDR5X datasheet [61]
and Samsung’s breakdown of LPDDR5 energy [42], a bit read from
the DRAM costs 5.5 pJ. Of these, 3.4 pJ go to the DRAM’s row activa-
tion, read path and output drivers, and 2.1 pJ to the PHY and mem-
ory controller of the accelerator. We added the DRAM’s standby
and refresh power, 0.43 W for the eight channels at the datasheet’s
maximum currents, over the RTL time of each inference.
Baselines. On Thor, we measured three implementations of the
GR00T N1.6 action head as replayed CUDA graphs: the released
BF16 head, FlashRT’s NVFP4 head [81] and our kernels. Every im-
plementation runs after a warm-up, with the GPU and memory
clocks pinned at their maximum. We measured the energy at the
input of the Thor module, which also feeds its CPU, memory and
regulators, and subtracted the module’s idle power. For System 2,
we also measured NVIDIA’s VLM backbone, as the median of 20
synchronized calls. The open-source cycle model of DiTPA [103]
runs the GR00T N1.6 action head with its skips disabled. For a fair
comparison, every action-head time and energy excludes the pro-
jection of System 2’s keys and values, which we count in System 2.

Closed-Loop Task Success
Rising bit order. In Figure 9, the GR00T N1.6 schedules of 12
nominal bit-steps show that the order of the bits decides whether
a schedule preserves success. Only the rising [2,3,3,4] preserves
success on both screening benchmarks. Uniform 3-bit weights and
the falling [4,3,3,2] lose success on both. On GR00T N1.5 and on
𝜋0.5 at four steps instead of ten, [2,3,3,4] also preserves success,
whereas every falling schedule loses success.
Rule of thumb. From the measured gains, the rule of thumb
of Section 3.3 predicts [2,2,3,5] for GR00T N1.6, [2,2,2,6] for GR00T
N1.5 and [2,2,3,3,3,3,3,3,4,4] for 𝜋0.5. The schedules for GR00T N1.5
and 𝜋0.5 preserve success, and that for GR00T N1.6 narrowly misses
the margin on RoboCasa GR1. The gains measured on a policy
thus predict a schedule that preserves or nearly preserves success,
without a search over schedules in closed loop.
Full benchmark suites. Figures 9a and b give every full suite
we evaluated. [2,3,3,4] preserves success on five of the eight suites.
The rising order thus holds beyond the screening benchmarks.
Step count. At two steps, the rising [2,4] preserves success on
both screening benchmarks. The uniform [3,3] and the falling [4,2]
of equal nominal bit-steps, in contrast, lose success on RoboCasa
GR1. The order of the bits thus matters at two steps as well as at
four. Whether fewer steps preserve success, however, depends on
the suite. [2,5] and [2,4] each preserve success on five of the eight
suites. The step count is therefore a program input.
7.2
Trajectory Quality
Smoothness of the commands. A success rate alone could hide
noise that the rounded weights add to the commands, since a sim-
ulated controller absorbs jitter that a real arm would execute. On
real robots, abrupt changes of velocity between action chunks also
cause jerky motion and shift the observed dynamics away from
the training data [10, 102]. We therefore measured the jerk of the
commanded arm positions, their third time derivative, and the jump
of their velocity between consecutive chunks. On RoboCasa GR1,
LIBERO and the RoboCasa kitchen, [2,3,3,4] lowers the jerk by 6
to 16% and the jump by 7 to 16% relative to the released model.
Every 90% interval of these changes lies below zero. On G1, where
[2,3,3,4] loses success, its jerk is 1.9× that of the released model. On
these four suites, the success rate thus hides no loss of smoothness.
Episode length. A quantized policy that preserves its success
rate may still need more inferences per episode. On the suites where
[2,3,3,4] preserves success, its successful episodes change in length
by −1% on average and by at most 2% relative to the released model.
Per task, [2,3,3,4] thus still reads at least 7.8× fewer weight bits
than the released model, against 8.0× fewer per inference.
Insight 8. Quantization can lengthen a policy’s episodes, so its
time and energy must also be compared per task.

7.5
Energy and Area
Figure 10b breaks down the energy per inference into the DRAM
reads, the DRAM’s standby and the parts of the chip. [2,3,3,4] costs
78 mJ per inference, or 3.9 W at 50 Hz. The DRAM reads take 51%
of this energy. The energy of the chip also scales with the bit-steps,
from 91 mJ at uniform 8-bit weights to 35 mJ at [2,3,3,4]. Most of
this reduction comes from the bit-serial sub-arrays, whose cycles
scale with the width of each step. [2,3,3,4] needs 31% of the energy
of uniform 8-bit weights, and [2,4] at two steps needs 18%. On
the accelerator, the per-step precision thus reduces the energy per
inference as well as the time.

NVFP4 head [81] on Thor. Figure 1b shows that Thor is faster only
at uniform 8-bit weights, where its DRAM floor lies at half of ours.
Scaled to Thor’s bandwidth, however, our accelerator is 1.3× faster
than Thor even at uniform 8-bit weights, and 3.1× faster at [2,3,3,4],
as Figure 10a shows. Our accelerator leads at equal bandwidth
because its time stays close to its DRAM floor, whereas fixed over-
heads keep Thor’s time far above its DRAM floor. Figure 1c shows
that, above its idle power, Thor uses 543 mJ per [2,3,3,4] inference,
against 78 mJ for our accelerator. As shown in Figure 12, FlashRT’s
NVFP4 head uses 9.1× the energy of our accelerator.
DiTPA (ISCA’26 [103]) is an accelerator for DiT-based action
planners that skips redundant actions, flow steps and tokens. With
its skips disabled, DiTPA reads 8-bit weights at every flow step and
runs 1.3× faster than our accelerator with uniform 8-bit weights,
as Figures 12 and 10a show. Its INT8 array at 500 MHz, however,
is too slow to keep pace with its DRAM, whose bandwidth is 1.4×
ours. Narrower weights would therefore not shorten the inference
on DiTPA, since its array takes the same number of cycles at every
width. DiTPA’s skips reduce these cycles but leave the bits that
each flow step reads unchanged. Even with all its skips, DiTPA
reports 60 Hz on GR00T N1.5 and 84 Hz on 𝜋0.5. Our accelerator
runs GR00T N1.5 at [2,3,3,4] 1.7× faster than DiTPA without its
projection of System 2’s keys and values. It runs 𝜋0.5 4.4× faster at
[2,5], two flow steps instead of ten. On these action heads, reducing
the bit-steps thus shortens the inference more than DiTPA’s skips
do.

every linear layer of the previous call in DRAM. On GR00T N1.6,

Programmability Across Models
Other action heads. As Figure 10a shows, the same RTL runs
[2,3,3,4] on GR00T N1.5 in 5.16 ms per inference and [2,5] on 𝜋0.5
at two flow steps in 2.70 ms. One accelerator thus runs action heads
of different sizes and architectures, each above 50 Hz.
Vision-language model. Our accelerator’s fabric is flexible
enough to also run System 2, the vision-language model of the
policy. We ran the System 2 of GR00T N1.6 on the RTL with 8-bit
weights and INT8 inputs, bit-exact against the executable model of
the accelerator. On the accelerator, a policy inference runs System 2
followed by the action head at [2,3,3,4]. Figure 13 compares these
inferences with NVIDIA’s VLM backbone and the released BF16
head on Thor.
On a RoboCasa GR1 call with one camera, the accelerator runs
the action head 3.9× faster than the released BF16 head on Thor.
System 2, however, takes 1.2× as long as on Thor. System 2 is

7.8
Quantization-in-DRAM
On one [2,3,3,4] inference of GR00T N1.6, Figure 14 compares two
options of Quantization-in-DRAM with the per-width copies that
all other experiments read. With requantizers alone, the DRAM
transfers every width uncoded. With an encoder behind every
requantizer, it sends the weights in the Huffman code of the per-
width copies. We verified that the RTL of the requantizer and the
encoder is bit-exact with these copies and synthesized it in TSMC
7 nm at the 3.75 ns column cycle of LPDDR5X-8533. We scaled both
the area of the netlists, placed at 60% density, and their energy by
the 10× lower density of logic in a DRAM process (Section 5.6).
Only with the encoders does Quantization-in-DRAM keep the
time per inference close to that of the per-width copies. Without
them, every width crosses the interface uncoded, so an inference
takes 1.4× as long, as Figure 14a shows. With them, an inference
takes 2.4% longer, since the banks’ tags and padding add 3.6% to the
DRAM traffic. In a DRAM timing model that we validated against
Ramulator 2, the reads of the 8-bit copy inside the banks add another
0.4%. With the encoders, Quantization-in-DRAM raises the DRAM
energy by 51% (Figure 14b), mainly because every bank reads all 8
bits of every weight instead of only the coded bits.
Samsung’s HBM-PIM [48], a fabricated chip, places logic at each
bank of its 20 nm DRAM. Figure 14c shows that the requantizer and
encoder of a bank need fewer gates than this logic. Their critical
paths in 7 nm take at most 0.88 ns of the column cycle, so they meet
it even with three times slower DRAM transistors [17]. The requan-
tizers and encoders take 5.0% of a two-channel 16 Gb LPDDR5X die
of 37 mm2 [16], or 19.4% at the area per gate of HBM-PIM. A DRAM
die can therefore hold a requantizer and an encoder at every bank.
With encoders at the banks, Quantization-in-DRAM thus trades
DRAM energy and die area for capacity, at almost the time per
inference of the per-width copies.

Related Work
Quantized robot policies. Prior work quantizes current VLAs
to uniform 4-bit weights, with plain rounding and with calibrated
methods [12, 31, 35, 87, 96]. SACQ [46] preserves success on LIBERO
at 2 bits per weight with vector codebooks trained for every suite.
BitVLA [86] trains a ternary backbone and ActQuant [2] rounds
the backbone to about 3 bits from calibration demonstrations, both
with a full-precision action head. QbD-VLA [95] selects the widths
of layer groups by short closed-loop rollouts. QuantWAMs [105]
and DyQ-VLA [104] vary the activation precision per step and per
policy query, respectively, and SteerQuant [88] keeps static 4-bit
weights. None of these works varies the weight width over the flow
steps of one inference. The methods that hold below 4 bits train or
calibrate on target data, whereas our schedules need neither.
Time-aware quantization of diffusion models. Quantizers
for image diffusion calibrate activation ranges per timestep [51, 71,
77, 90], mix precision per layer [101] or choose the steps together
with a per-layer precision [4, 83]. PTQD [30] raises the activation
width over the steps, and AdaTSQ [100] and MASQ [44] vary it per
step or per image region. Our rising widths are those of the weights,
which determine the DRAM traffic at batch size one. TMPDiff [49]
and TASQ [28] vary the weight precision per step on images (Sec-
tion 3.2). The analysis of TASQ sets the Jacobian of the denoiser
to zero and thereby ignores how the remaining steps attenuate an
early error. Let It Be Simple [15] shows that an action head’s flow
attenuates differences in its starting noise, whereas the per-step
gains and the rising widths derived from them are new.
Step counts. One or a few flow steps often suffice for action
heads [15, 18, 82], depending on how many actions run per in-
ference [82, 98]. Several works choose the step count per infer-
ence [27, 29] or distill the head to one or two steps [59, 89]. We
treat the step count, like the widths, as a parameter.
Accelerators for robot policies. SpecVLA [70] verifies long
chunks with the same network applied to quantized changes of its
inputs. DaDu-Corki [33] predicts a trajectory per inference, so that
the VLA runs less often. Both run every full inference of the policy
on a GPU. SpecVLA’s hardware runs the verifier, and DaDu-Corki’s
converts the predicted trajectories into joint torques. They reduce
the number of inferences, which is orthogonal to reducing the time
and energy of each.
Bit-serial and precision-scalable arrays. Bit-serial and bit-
flexible designs scale their cycles with the operand widths [3, 40, 47,
72–74, 84]. BitMoD [14] serializes the weights of language models at
3 to 8 bits, and low-bit formats adapt the data type to the values [25,
26]. Run-time partitioning divides an array between tenants by their
deadlines [24]. To the best of our knowledge, no design partitions
an array at run time by what a memory-bound stream needs.
Logic in memory. Processing-in-memory DRAMs compute
beside the banks with one MAC per weight read [34, 45, 48, 53] or
evaluate lookup tables inside the sub-arrays [20]. An extension of
HBM-PIM [41] and StreamDQ [38] widen quantized weights to 16
bits, for the MAC units or in the logic base die of an HBM stack.
JIT-Q [36] quantizes a master copy with the PIM units of HBM
and writes the result back for the GPU. In a CXL memory device,
SmartQuant [93] and TRACE [92] read only the top bits of one
stored copy, which TRACE compresses with a generic codec. The
closest DRAM system is WIFiV-LPDDR [32], a modeled LPDDR
that narrows a stored 8-bit copy on read by a clamp and a shift. It
neither specifies where in the DRAM this narrowing takes place
nor encodes the weights, whereas we place a requantizer and an
encoder at every bank.

Conclusion and Future Work
Every flow step of a robot action head re-reads all of its weights but
applies each to only about fifty tokens, so its inference is memory-
bound. Because later flow steps attenuate early quantization er-
rors, the head’s weight precision should rise over the steps. With
plain per-channel rounding, [2,3,3,4] preserves the closed-loop suc-
cess of three policies, but current GPU kernels cannot exploit this
per-step precision due to fixed overheads. We therefore propose a
programmable accelerator whose time and energy scale with the
bit-steps, because it keeps its DRAM link saturated throughout an
inference at every width. It decodes entropy-coded weights at the
DRAM rate for eight bit-serial sub-arrays, which also run the VLM.
A run-time allocator divides them between the linear layers and
the attention, whose compute requirements change with the width
and the model. Quantization-in-DRAM, with a requantizer and an
encoder at each bank, lets one stored 8-bit copy serve every width.
With a copy per width and 50% of Thor’s memory bandwidth, the
accelerator runs GR00T N1.6 at [2,3,3,4]. An inference then com-
pletes 1.7× faster than on Thor in NVFP4, at 9.1× less energy and
equal closed-loop success. On a one-camera call, one inference per
action at 50 Hz takes 39% of each control period and leaves time to
run the VLM at 12.5 Hz.
Future work. A run-time selector such as that of VQVLA [79]
could set the widths of every inference as the battery, the thermal
budget or the task demands.
Limitations. Every closed-loop result comes from simulation,
and quantized policies that hold in simulation have lost success on
real robots [95, 96]. Quantization-in-DRAM needs a DRAM mode
that no commercial part offers. The energy of the chip comes from
VCD-annotated netlists with a clock tree but before placement and
routing, and that of the DRAM from a model.
