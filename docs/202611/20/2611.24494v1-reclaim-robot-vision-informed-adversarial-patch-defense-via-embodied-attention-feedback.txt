RECLAIM: Robot-Vision-Informed Adversarial Patch Defense via Embodied
Attention Feedback

Zelong Yu2, Yize Tian1, Jirong Liu1, Zifeng Kang1,∗

Abstract
Vision-language-action policies control embodied agents
through a perception–action loop grounded in physical in-
teraction, where adversarial patches can corrupt action pre-
dictions and thereby alter subsequent observations. To mit-
igate these threats, many existing defenses rely on policy
adaptation or auxiliary models, but require additional train-
ing or inference. We introduce RECLAIM, a training-free,
inference-time defense that uses a frozen VLA’s own atten-
tion response to local visual interventions to guide purifi-
cation during closed-loop embodied manipulation. Our key
insight is that attention changes induced by local edits pro-
vide feedback for choosing where and how to purify as the
robot interacts with its environment. RECLAIM combines
this feedback with robot geometry to refine patch localiza-
tion and select local treatments, then revalidates them as the
agent’s embodied actions change the observed scene. We
evaluate RECLAIM against patches generated by four ad-
versarial attack methods across multiple manipulation task
suites under frozen VLA control. Under adversarial patch
attacks, RECLAIM succeeds in at least one rollout on 95%
of the evaluated tasks and raises rollout success on LIBERO-
Object from 0% without defense to 70.0%. Its mean deci-
sion time is only 548.9 ms per control step. These results
highlight policy-guided visual purification as an effective
approach to defending embodied agents against adversar-
ial patches and recovering closed-loop task execution.
1. Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models use visual observa-
tions and language instructions to predict robot actions [11,
25]. Their visual representations determine the scene infor-
mation available for action prediction, so corrupted percep-
tion can change how the robot interacts with its surround-
ings. The security of embodied deployment therefore de-
pends on the integrity of visual perception.
Adversarial
patches introduce localized patterns to redirect visual pre-
dictions [2]. In VLA control, they exploit this dependence
by altering the camera observation: the altered pixels change
the encoded visual features and can redirect action predic-
tions without changing policy parameters [21, 22]. The re-

defense that guides localization and treatment selection us-
ing the VLA’s available responses.
This requirement raises a spatial challenge of distinguish-
ing suspicious attention from legitimate manipulation re-
sponses, and a temporal challenge of maintaining suitable
interventions as the scene changes. Analyses of VLA vi-
sual attention show that patches can attract responses away
from the arm and relevant objects [22]. Yet the gripper and
manipulated object also attract attention, making response
strength alone ambiguous.
To interpret the robot-related
part of this response, known robot geometry can account
for attention associated with the arm and gripper, leaving
residual concentrations for inspection. For each proposed
region, the defense must then determine the most suitable
treatment while retaining visual evidence needed for ma-
nipulation. As motion changes the robot–object relation-
ship, earlier choices require renewed assessment so that use-
ful treatments remain available and unsuitable ones can be
replaced. Sustained recovery therefore depends on spatial
interpretation, treatment selection, and temporal updating.
Our key insight is that the defended VLA can assess local
interventions through changes in its own attention and action
predictions. Comparing alternative views of the same obser-
vation with the same frozen policy makes these responses
comparable in the current task context, even when a corre-
sponding clean observation is unavailable. Repeating this
assessment as interaction progresses connects purification
to the policy’s changing perception and action.

components for inference-time protection of the perception–
action loop.
Geometry-Guided Attention Localization
(GAL) combines persistent attention with projected arm
and gripper geometry to expose suspicious residual concen-

We evaluate RECLAIM on frozen OpenVLA across 40
tasks in four LIBERO suites [14] and four patch attacks [15,
21, 22].
Under matched UADA conditions, RECLAIM
raises rollout success from 0.0% without defense to 52.92%,
versus accelerated PAD’s 40.83%. It also improves mask
alignment and uses 77.31% less mean decision time than
accelerated PAD. With unchanged defense settings, success
ranges from 38.33% to 54.58% across the four attacks. Ab-
lations support the contributions of response-guided treat-
ment selection and temporal revalidation.
Our contributions are as follows.
• We formulate input-side defense for a frozen VLA as state-
ful local visual intervention, using the policy’s attention
and action responses to guide recovery of instruction-
following control.
• We develop RECLAIM, coupling geometry-guided lo-
calization, response-guided purification, and memory-
assisted temporal revalidation to counter patch interfer-
ence and sustain instruction-following control as embod-
ied interaction changes the scene.
• We demonstrate task recovery across four patch attacks,
quantify intervention alignment and decision cost, and ex-
amine component contributions through ablations.
2. Related Work
Adversarial patches in VLA control.
Wang et al. [21]
expose the vulnerability of VLA-controlled manipulation to
localized visual patches. Their Untargeted Action Discrep-
ancy Attack (UADA) maximizes action deviations, while
UPA disrupts movement directions and TMA induces tar-
geted manipulation errors. UADA reaches a maximum aver-
age task failure rate of 100% in their simulation experiments
and an attack success rate exceeding 43% in real-world tri-
als. The resulting erratic movements show how image cor-
ruption can propagate into physical execution, threatening
both task completion and safe interaction. These risks un-
derscore the importance of defending the perception–action
loop for reliable embodied deployment. This attack frame-
work underlies our primary evaluation.
Complementing
these attacks on action outputs, Xu et al. introduce the Em-
bedding Disruption Patch Attack (EDPA), which generates
patches by disrupting visual–textual alignment and separat-
ing perturbed visual representations from clean ones [22].
They counter this representation-level interference by ad-
versarially fine-tuning the visual encoder, requiring clean–
perturbed training pairs and parameter updates. Lu et al. de-
velop UPA-RFAS, combining feature disruption, attention
dominance, and image–text misalignment to improve uni-
versal patch transfer [15]. We denote this attack as RFAS to
distinguish it from Wang et al.’s UPA [21].

Input-side adversarial patch purification.
Patch purifi-
cation methods locate and remove corrupted image con-
tent before prediction. PAD uses semantic independence
and spatial heterogeneity for localization, followed by pre-
trained segmentation to refine removal masks [8].
DIF-
Fender couples localization and restoration through text-
guided diffusion with few-shot prompt tuning [9], while
APEX combines region extraction, blur and boundary cues,
and structure-guided inpainting [20]. These methods evalu-
ate purification through visual recognition performance and
patch-localization quality. Such criteria do not determine
whether a treatment preserves the visual cues needed for a
robot’s next action or remains appropriate as manipulation
changes the scene. Their auxiliary processing also intro-
duces deployment dependencies: PAD invokes a segmen-
tation model, while DIFFender requires diffusion inference
and prompt adaptation. Building on local patch purification,
RECLAIM addresses these control and deployment require-
ments by using the defended VLA itself to assess treatments
in the current task context, without auxiliary perception or a
separately trained defense model.
Policy feedback for run-time intervention.
BYOVLA
uses action sensitivity to guide observation editing with-
out policy fine-tuning [5]. External VLM and segmentation
models identify task-irrelevant regions; regional perturba-
tions measure their influence on predicted actions and guide
inpainting or background recoloring. RECLAIM adopts the
principle of consulting the controlled policy, but extends
the feedback beyond action sensitivity. Sensitivity to a re-
gion does not alone establish which purification to prefer.
RECLAIM compares treatments through regional attention
reduction and action uncertainty, requiring cross-operator
attention agreement outside the edited region. It therefore
assesses both suppression within the candidate region and
consistency over the remaining scene, enabling purification
selection without first assigning the suspected patch an ex-
ternal task-irrelevance label.
Spatial information in attention.
Attention studies pro-
vide evidence that frozen vision-language models retain
spatial information useful beyond image-level prediction.
Kang et al. [10] identify a small set of heads whose re-
sponses localize text-referred objects. ResCLIP finds that
intermediate-layer query–key attention captures spatial cor-
respondences between image regions; incorporating these
responses into the final layer improves localization for dense
visual prediction [23]. Together, these findings support us-
ing native attention as a source of spatial cues for local-
ization. In manipulation, strong responses can also arise
from the arm, gripper, and task objects. Wang et al. [21]
suggest robot-joint relationships as a potential defense cue.
Robot geometry can therefore help interpret attended re-
gions, while comparing attention before and after local ed-
its provides feedback about their effect on the policy’s vi-
sual processing. These complementary insights motivate
grounding patch purification in embodied policy feedback,
connecting spatial localization and treatment selection to
continued task execution.
3. RECLAIM
3.1. Problem Formulation
For deployment on unfamiliar patches and changing tasks,
RECLAIM operates without patch masks, paired clean im-
ages, or clean reference actions. It uses attention and action-
token probabilities from a frozen vision-language-action
(VLA) policy πθ to select visual interventions. At step t,
it receives a potentially patch-corrupted RGB image It ∈
RH×W ×3, instruction ℓ, robot joint configuration qt, and
trusted parameters κ specifying robot geometry and camera

deployment.

We evaluate task recovery, computational eﬀiciency, com-

4.1. Experimental Setup
Tasks and placement.
We evaluate frozen OpenVLA
policies [11] on the four LIBERO suites [14], covering ten
tasks per suite to test defense across scene layouts, object
interactions, goals, and long-horizon manipulation. Differ-
ent fixed initial states per task and three patch placements
per state vary the starting configuration and spatial interfer-
ence while preserving matched conditions across methods.
This gives 80 clean rollouts and 240 rollouts per patch condi-
tion. OpenVLA fuses DINOv2 [17] and SigLIP [24] visual
features [11]; their pretrained weights remain frozen during
defense. Control horizons are 220, 280, 300, and 520 steps
for Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, with early termination
on success.
To vary spatial interference under these matched task
conditions, we follow UADA’s initial-visibility placement
principle [21].
Seeded sampling (20261116) distributes
50 × 50 patch footprints across spatially separated admissi-
ble background regions, relocating infeasible slots under the
initial-view constraints. Positions are frozen before evalua-
tion and remain fixed in image coordinates.
Comparison methods and input controls.
PAD [8] com-
bines MI/compression heatmaps, SAM ViT-L [12] refine-
ment, and black-mask removal. NumPy [6] sliding-window
views and histogram reductions accelerate MI while pre-
serving heatmaps and mask selection; all PAD results use
this implementation. APEX [20] uses SAM-based region
extraction and LaMa inpainting.
The shuffle control applies one fixed joint RGB-pixel per-
mutation to the UADA texture (seed 202). Grey replaces it
with RGB (127, 127, 127). Both preserve UADA’s patch
area and placements, testing intervention effects on non-
optimized visual perturbations alongside clean inputs.
RECLAIM configuration.
We set n = 20, M = 5,
α = 0.2, γ = 1, λH = 0.3, B = 3, and Tmem =
120. The nine operators are gray blending at strengths 0.5
and 0.8, hard gray replacement, Gaussian-blur blending at
strengths 0.6 and 1.0, global-mean blending at strength 0.8,
neighborhood-median filling, median filtering, and Telea in-
painting [19]. Blur uses a 7 × 7 kernel with standard devi-
ation 2, median filtering a 7 × 7 kernel, and Telea radius 3.
Telea-only provides a representative inpainting comparison
by restricting the executable operator while retaining the re-
maining ARP and MTR rules.

5. Conclusion
Generalist policies increasingly span diverse tasks and em-
bodiments [1, 4, 16], making reliable visual feedback im-
portant for sustained task execution.
We presented RECLAIM, a training-free defense that
uses a frozen VLA’s attention and action responses to guide
local visual interventions. Robot geometry contextualizes
attention, while treatment comparisons and temporal reval-
idation support continued task execution. On four LIBERO
suites, RECLAIM recovers OpenVLA performance across
four patch attacks and outperforms accelerated PAD under
UADA in both success rate and mean decision cost. The ac-
companying improvement in mask alignment indicates more
accurate placement of visual interventions along the eval-
uated trajectories.
These findings highlight how embod-
ied attention feedback can guide visual purification as robot
actions change subsequent observations, connecting adver-
sarial patch defense to continued task execution within the
perception–action loop. This motivates further study of pol-
icy feedback for robust embodied control.
