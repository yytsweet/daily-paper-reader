Abstract
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) are
obtained by fine-tuning pre-trained Vision-
Language Models (VLMs) on robot demon-
stration data, enabling action prediction from
visual observations and language instructions.
However, this fine-tuning induces catastrophic
forgetting, substantially degrading the semantic
and visual grounding capabilities of the original
VLM. We address this by merging the parame-
ters of a VLM and its VLA derivative without
retraining either model. Concretely, we extract
the task vector — the difference between VLA
and VLM weights — and decompose it via
Singular Value Decomposition. For each com-
ponent, we learn a scalar mixing coefficient
controlling how much of the VLA adaptation
is retained, optimised to jointly preserve ac-
tion prediction and vision-language capabilities.
Experiments show that our merged model per-
forms well on both axes simultaneously, consis-
tently outperforming established merging base-
lines.

Introduction
The ability to reason about complex environments
and to plan sequences of actions has long been a
central challenge in artificial intelligence. Large
Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language
Models (VLMs) have made significant strides in
this direction, combining reasoning, visual percep-
tion, and scene understanding into unified models
capable of high-level instruction following, task
decomposition, and environment description (Wei
et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023; Radford et al., 2021;
Liu et al., 2023b; Dai et al., 2026; Aissi et al., 2026)
— capabilities that make them compelling building
blocks for autonomous robotic agents. However,
these models remain confined to perception and rea-
soning, and cannot directly produce the low-level
motor commands required for physical interaction.
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) bridge
this gap by fine-tuning pre-trained VLMs on robot
demonstration data (O’Neill et al., 2025; Walke
et al., 2023), enabling them to predict actions
directly from visual observations and language
instructions (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al.,
2024). However, this fine-tuning comes at a signifi-
cant cost: the action objective progressively over-
writes the weight-space directions encoding visual-
language understanding (Hancock et al., 2025),
yielding a specialised model that excels at low-
level control but can no longer reliably perform the
high-level semantic reasoning needed for robust,
generalist robotic agents.
This tension between action specialisation and
semantic preservation is a fundamental obstacle to
building robotic systems that are both capable of
language modelling and fine-grained motor control
(see Figure 1). Existing approaches to mitigating
this degradation typically require architectural mod-
ifications or costly co-training on mixed datasets
(Zawalski et al., 2024; Hancock et al., 2025), mak-
ing them difficult to apply post-hoc to already fine-
tuned VLAs. Model merging offers a compelling
alternative (Li et al., 2025; Yadav et al., 2025): by
combining the parameters of a VLM and a VLA,
one can in principle recover a model that retains
both action prediction and vision-language capabil-
ities. However, naive uniform blending treats all pa-
rameters equally and fails to account for the hetero-
geneous roles played by different weight matrices
across the two models, yielding suboptimal trade-
offs between semantic and action performance.
In this work, we investigate how to merge a VLM
and a VLA such that the resulting model retains
the action capabilities acquired during fine-tuning
while recovering the vision-language understand-
ing of the original VLM. To this end, we extract the
task vector by subtracting the VLM weights from
those of the VLA and decompose it using Singu-
lar Value Decomposition to expose the individual
components of this adaptation. For each such di-
rection, we learn a scalar coefficient that controls

Our contributions are as follows:
• A post-hoc, lightweight merging method that
decomposes the VLM–VLA task vector via SVD
and learns per-component coefficients to jointly
recover language understanding and preserve ac-

of a small set of scalar merging parameters while
keeping both source models fully frozen.
• An ablation study justifying fine-grained merg-
ing, showing per-component coefficients are neces-

• A structural analysis of the fine-tuning direc-
tions at multiple scales of the SVD decomposition,
identifying which components of the adaptation
must be preserved to reconcile vision-language un-
derstanding and action prediction in the merged
model.
Together, these contributions offer a practi-
cal and substantially cheaper path toward robotic
agents that combine reliable low-level control with
the high-level semantic reasoning of their underly-
ing VLM.

Related Work
2.1
Vision Language Action Models
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models extend
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by fine-tuning
them on large-scale robot demonstration data
(O’Neill et al., 2025), enabling them to act as
generalist policies that directly generate low-level
actions (Brohan et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024;
Kim et al., 2024). Actions are produced either
autoregressively (Kim et al., 2024; Pertsch et al.,
2025) or via continuous decoding such as flow
matching (Black et al., 2024). However, this fine-
tuning causes them to largely lose their original
vision–language capabilities through catastrophic
forgetting.
Some works sidestep this issue by keeping the
VLM frozen and training only a lightweight ac-
tion module (Shukor et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025),
but this limits the model’s ability to fully adapt its
representations for embodied control. Others rein-
troduce text tasks during training either by training
VLAs to generate both actions and text simultane-
ously (Zawalski et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025)
or by aligning action representations with the lan-
guage objective (Hancock et al., 2025). Both ap-
proaches require large-scale co-training to recover
knowledge already present in the initial VLM. In
contrast, our approach operates post-hoc on an al-
ready fine-tuned VLA, requiring no additional train-
ing of either source model.

2.2
Model Merging
Model merging has emerged as a cost-effective
alternative to full retraining, enabling the combi-
nation of specialised models into a single capable
model without additional data or compute. Model
Soup (Wortsman et al., 2022) averages the weights
of models fine-tuned from the same checkpoint.
SLERP (Jang et al., 2024) interpolates along the
spherical geodesic rather than linearly, better pre-
serving parameter norms.
TIES (Yadav et al.,
2023) resolves sign conflicts across multiple task
vectors before averaging, requiring at least two
independently fine-tuned models and thus not di-
rectly applicable to our setting. DARE (Yu et al.,
2024) randomly drops a fraction of task vector pa-
rameters before rescaling the remainder. Fisher
Merging (Matena and Raffel, 2022) weights each
parameter by its diagonal Fisher information, but
requires computing and storing a matrix as large as
the full model, making it costly at billion-parameter
scale. AdaMerging (Yang et al., 2024) learns task-
wise or layer-wise merging weights but does not
decompose the task vector into finer-grained com-
ponents. All these methods operate with fixed or
coarse-grained coefficients and none have been ap-
plied to reconciling a VLM with its VLA derivative.
Closer to our work, decomposing task vectors via
SVD has already been proposed to reduce inter-
ference between models trained on different tasks
Gargiulo et al. (2025). We build on this insight and
extend it to the VLM–VLA setting, where the ob-
jective is to reconcile two fundamentally different
capabilities — language understanding and action
prediction — rather than reduce task interference.
3
Method
3.1
Problem Formulation
Let θVLM and θVLA denote the parameters of a pre-
trained Vision-Language Model and a fine-tuned
Vision-Language-Action model, respectively. Both
models share a common backbone architecture.
The VLM initially possesses strong language and
visual grounding capabilities, while the VLA has
been further trained to predict robotic actions, al-
beit at the cost of partial degradation of its original
vision-language abilities. Our merging procedure
is applied to all layers that are common to both
architectures; VLA-specific components such as
the action head are left unchanged and carried over
directly into the merged model.
Our goal is to construct a merged model θmerged
that jointly retains the action prediction capabilities
of the VLA and the vision-language understanding
of the VLM (Figure 2a). We define it as a weighted
combination of both source models, controlled by
a set of mixing coefficients α:

3.3
Training Objective
To optimise the mixing coefficients α, we define
a composite objective that captures both language
fidelity and action prediction accuracy, while penal-
ising excessive deviation from either source model:
L(α) = λ1Ltext(α) + λ2 · DKL(πα ∥πVLM)
+ λ3Lact(α) + λ4 · DKL(πα ∥πVLA)
(7)
where λ1, λ2, λ3, λ4 ≥0 are tunable weight-
ing hyperparameters (details can be found in Ap-
pendix A.1). Ltext measures language generation
quality via negative log-likelihood over a language
dataset Dtext. Lact measures action prediction qual-
ity over a robot trajectory dataset Dact; its form de-
pends on the VLA action head — for an autoregres-
sive architecture it is the negative log-likelihood
over action tokens, while for flow matching it is
the conditional flow matching loss (Lipman et al.,
2023). The KL divergence terms act as regularis-
ers, penalising deviation from the pure VLM and
VLA distributions respectively. Since θ(l)
merged is a
differentiable function of α via Eq. (6), L is min-
imised directly with gradient descent using Adam
(Kingma and Ba, 2017) (details can be found in
Appendix A.2).
4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Models and datasets.
We apply our merging
method to PaliGemma-3B (Beyer et al., 2024) as
the VLM, paired with two VLA variants sharing the
same architecture: π0-FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025),
an autoregressive VLA that tokenises actions, and
π0.5 (Black et al., 2025), a flow matching VLA
that predicts continuous action chunks via iterative
denoising. Both VLA variants are obtained by fine-
tuning PaliGemma on robot demonstration data.


All models are kept frozen — only the mixing coef-
ficients α are optimised via Adam (Kingma and Ba,
2017) on two complementary datasets built from
the LIBERO dataset available on LeRobot (Liu
et al., 2023a; Cadene et al., 2026). For the action
signal, we relabel existing expert demonstrations
from the LIBERO dataset with the pure VLA’s own
predicted action chunks, producing Dact. For the
language signal, we prompt PaliGemma to caption
each observed frame, producing a paired dataset
Dtext = {(xi, ci)} of observations and scene de-
scriptions.
Evaluation protocol.
We evaluate the merged
model along three axes: vision-language capabili-
ties, embodied reasoning, and action capabilities.
For
vision-language
capabilities,
we
as-
sess captioning performance on the COCO
dataset (Lin et al., 2015) using Rouge-L (Lin,
2004), METEOR (Banerjee and Lavie, 2005) and
BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) metrics, and visual
question answering performance on VQAv2 (Goyal
et al., 2017).
For embodied reasoning, we evaluate accu-
racy on the EQA benchmark across three ques-
tion categories — Object State, Spatial, and At-
tribute Recognition — which probe whether the
model can answer language questions grounded
in a robotic setting, bridging the gap between the
general-domain vision-language evaluations above
and the action benchmarks below.
For action capabilities, we evaluate task suc-
cess rate on the LIBERO task suites — LIBERO-
Spatial, LIBERO-Goal and LIBERO-Object (Liu
et al., 2023a) — and report the average success rate
across tasks for each suite (details can be found in
Appendix B; additional results on the RoboCasa
kitchen tasks are also reported in Appendix B.1).

To analyse

compare two levels of granularity for α. Under
per-layer α, a single coefficient α(l) ∈[0, 1] is
learned for each layer l and applied uniformly

k

are entangled within the same weight matrix and
a layer-wide α(l) must trade them off uniformly.

coarser parameterisations lack the expressivity to
reconcile both objectives.

Conclusion
In this paper, we present a post-hoc method for
merging a VLM and a VLA by decomposing the
task vector via SVD and learning per-component
mixing coefficients through gradient descent. We
apply this method to two distinct VLA architec-
tures, an autoregressive and a flow matching model,
demonstrating that it generalises across different
action decoding strategies. Both source models
remain frozen throughout, and only the mixing
coefficients are optimised, making the approach
substantially cheaper than retraining or co-training
a VLA from scratch and broadly applicable to al-
ready fine-tuned VLAs. Our approach recovers a
model that retains the action prediction capabilities
of the VLA while restoring the vision-language
understanding of the original VLM, a balance that
uniform interpolation fails to achieve. Through ex-
tensive ablations, we show that this fine-grained
control over the merging process is essential, and
our analysis further reveals that language and ac-
tion capabilities are encoded at different scales in
weight space, offering new insight into the nature
of catastrophic forgetting in VLA fine-tuning. Fu-
ture work could leverage the language and reason-
ing capabilities restored by our method to equip
VLAs with high-level planning, enabling them to
decompose and reason about long-horizon tasks
rather than merely reacting to immediate instruc-
tions. Code and merged model checkpoints will be
released upon acceptance.


Limitations
Our method assumes the VLM and VLA share
a common backbone, so it does not directly ap-
ply to mismatched architectures or tokenizers. It
also still requires a training loop to optimise α,
albeit a much cheaper one than full fine-tuning.
Our action and language datasets are both derived
from a single benchmark (LIBERO) and from the
source models themselves, so performance on out-
of-distribution tasks, embodiments, or real-world
deployments remains untested. Finally, our experi-
ments are limited to VLMs and VLAs at the 3–4B
parameter scale, and whether our findings hold at
larger scales remains untested.
Ethical Considerations
This work aims to mitigate catastrophic forgetting
in robot learning models, helping robotic agents
retain language-based communication alongside
low-level control rather than sacrificing one for the
other. All experiments are conducted in simula-
tion using publicly available models and datasets;
no human subjects or physical robot deployments
are involved. As with any method that enhances
action capabilities, thorough safety validation is
recommended before real-world deployment.

A.2
KL Regularisation Terms
Language side.
For the VLM side, πVLM and πα denote the token-level output distributions of the pure
VLM and the merged model, respectively, computed over the language vocabulary at each generated
position given the same visual observation and prompt. For π0-FAST, whose vocabulary jointly contains
both language and action tokens, this distribution is restricted to the language sub-vocabulary, masking
out action-token logits before computing the divergence. DKL(πα∥πVLM) is the standard categorical
KL divergence between these two (vocabulary-restricted) token distributions, averaged over sequence
positions, and is well-defined for both VLA variants since the language head is shared and discrete in
both π0-FAST and π0.5.
Action side — autoregressive VLA.
For π0-FAST, actions are represented as discrete tokens drawn
from a dedicated action sub-vocabulary, and πVLA, πα denote the corresponding token-level distributions
restricted to this action sub-vocabulary, with language-token logits masked out. DKL(πα∥πVLA) is
therefore also a standard categorical KL divergence, computed analogously to the language term above
but over the disjoint action sub-vocabulary, ensuring that each KL term reflects only the corresponding
modality.
Action side — flow matching VLA.
For π0.5, actions are continuous and generated via iterative
denoising, so no closed-form categorical distribution exists over the action space. Following prior work on
distribution matching for diffusion- and flow-based visuomotor policies (Jia et al., 2024; Ping et al., 2026),
we approximate πVLA and πα as Gaussian distributions over the predicted velocity field. Concretely, for

A.3
Dataset Collection
Action dataset Dact.
For each VLA variant, we construct Dact by relabeling existing expert demonstra-
tions from the LIBERO dataset available on LeRobot (Liu et al., 2023a; Cadene et al., 2026). Concretely,
we take the visual observations, proprioceptive states, and task instructions from the expert trajectories
and query the pure VLA to obtain its predicted action chunks for each observation, storing the resulting
pairs. This collection is performed once per VLA variant and reused throughout training and ablation, and

A.4
Training Cost
Training the mixing coefficients α for our main results takes approximately 3 hours on a single NVIDIA
H100 GPU for 20,000 steps. Both source models (θVLM and θVLA) remain frozen throughout, and only
α is updated. At our chosen practical rank, this amounts to a total of 381,587 learnable parameters for
Oursπ0F ast and 381,605 for Oursπ0.5 across all layers weight matrices — in both cases roughly four
orders of magnitude smaller than the ∼3B frozen parameters of the VLA backbone alone. This makes our
approach substantially cheaper than retraining or co-training a VLA from scratch.
B
Environment and Task Definitions
LIBERO.
LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a) is a simulation benchmark for lifelong robot manipulation
built on the Robosuite simulator, comprising several task suites each containing 10 tasks with 50 expert
demonstrations per task. We evaluate on three of its suites: LIBERO-Spatial, where the robot must place
a bowl on a plate among visually identical bowls that differ only in their spatial location relative to other
objects, testing spatial reasoning under a fixed set of objects; LIBERO-Object, where the robot must
pick and place a different, unique target object into a basket across tasks while the layout remains fixed,
testing object-centric generalisation; and LIBERO-Goal, where both the objects and their spatial layout
are held fixed while the task goal varies, testing the transfer of procedural, goal-directed behaviour. Each
suite is designed to disentangle a specific axis of generalisation (spatial, object, or goal knowledge), and
observations consist of third-person and wrist-camera RGB images.
Robocasa.
RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2026) is a large-scale simulation benchmark for household robot
manipulation in photorealistic kitchen environments, comprising a set of atomic skills (e.g. pick-and-
place, opening/closing doors and drawers, turning knobs, pressing buttons) together with longer-horizon
composite tasks. We evaluate on three atomic kitchen tasks: Open Fridge, requiring the robot to open a
refrigerator door; Setup Cup, requiring the robot to pick up a cup and place it in a target location; and
Navigate Kitchen, requiring the robot to move within the kitchen environment to reach a target location
before completing a manipulation sub-goal. Compared to LIBERO, RoboCasa tasks are evaluated across
a larger number of randomised scene layouts and object instances, making the benchmark substantially
more challenging: even strong VLA baselines such as π0.5 average only around 39% success rate across
these tasks in our setting, reflecting the benchmark’s greater diversity in scene configurations and the
longer effective horizon of some tasks (e.g. Navigate Kitchen).

B.1
Additional Results on RoboCasa
Table 4 reports task success rate on three RoboCasa kitchen tasks — Open Fridge, Setup Cup, and
Navigate Kitchen — complementing the LIBERO results presented in the main paper. The overall trends
are consistent with those observed on LIBERO: the pure VLM achieves zero success rate everywhere, both
pure VLAs establish strong baselines, and all fixed-coefficient merging methods (Model Soup, DARE,
SLERP) collapse entirely to zero success rate across all tasks. Our merged models retain meaningful
action performance on both VLA variants: Oursπ0F ast reaches 56/61/78 (versus 78/68/89 for the pure
π0-FAST), and Oursπ0.5 reaches 65/49/21 (versus 71/58/25 for the pure π0.5). The performance drop
relative to the pure VLAs is more pronounced on RoboCasa than on LIBERO, reflecting the greater
difficulty of this benchmark: even the pure VLAs average only around 50–78% across tasks, and Navigate
Kitchen proves particularly challenging for π0.5 (25% for the pure VLA, 21% after merging). Despite
this increased difficulty, our method remains the only merging approach that retains non-trivial action
performance while simultaneously recovering the vision-language and embodied reasoning capabilities
reported in the main paper.

Figure 7 shows the same overall pattern for both VLA variants: mean α is near 0 for the most dominant
directions of the task vector and rises monotonically toward the tail of the spectrum. For Oursπ0.5 (top),
mean α is exactly 0 for k < 200 and rises to a peak around 0.8 near k ≈1050 before declining slightly
toward the tail. For Oursπ0F ast (bottom), the trend is similar, with mean α starting close to, though not
exactly at, 0 (∼0.1 at k = 0) and plateauing around 0.65–0.7 from k ≈600 onward. The consistency of
this monotonic pattern across both VLA architectures indicates that the low-rank/high-rank asymmetry
exists between language and action recovery. This pattern directly complements the rank ablation of
Section 4.3: when the model is restricted to a low rank, only the dominant directions are available,
leaving the optimisation with little room to inject VLA-relevant adaptation, which is consistent with action
prediction degrading sharply at low rank while language recovers comparatively quickly, for both VLA
variants. Conversely, the gradual rise of mean α toward the tail of the spectrum in both panels shows that
the directions necessary for action prediction are spread broadly across the weaker components, consistent
with the diffuse nature of the VLA adaptation observed throughout this work.
C.3
Additional Qualitative Examples
Figure 8 shows six additional captioning examples spanning diverse scenes, from tabletop manipulation
to kitchen environments. Across all examples, the pure VLA output degrades into either a single
repeated keyword (e.g. “Wood Wood Wood ....”, “Robot Bot Bot ...”), a short fragment stripped of
grammatical structure (e.g. “Colored”, “@Water @@”), or garbled non-linguistic tokens (e.g. “˙p.w˚Γ
f tools”), consistent with the catastrophic forgetting of language modelling capabilities discussed in
Section 4.2. In contrast, our merged model consistently recovers full, grammatically coherent sentences
that correctly identify the objects and setting present in the scene (e.g. “A collection of tools including
screwdrivers, wrenches, and bolts.”, “A 3D rendering of a kitchen scene featuring a stovetop and oven,
including a sink.”). We note that our merged model’s captions occasionally diverge stylistically from
the pure VLM’s — favouring phrases such as “3D rendering of...” — but remain semantically accurate
and fluent, indicating that language coherence is recovered even when the exact phrasing differs from
the original VLM. These qualitative observations are consistent with the quantitative captioning scores
reported in Table 1, where the pure VLA collapses on ROUGE-L, METEOR and BERTScore while our
merged model recovers most of the pure VLM’s performance.
