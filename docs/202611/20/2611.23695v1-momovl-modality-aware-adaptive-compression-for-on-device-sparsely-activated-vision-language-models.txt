Abstract
The rise of the agentic web has been accompanied by growing de-
mand for on-device Vision-Language Models (VLMs) that perform
diverse cross-modal web tasks with enhanced privacy and reduced
latency. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are widely adopted in
modern VLMs for compute-efficient scalability, yet their multi-
expert architecture poses deployment challenges due to substantial
memory demands. Mixed-precision quantization is an effective com-
pression technique that reduces bit-widths while adapting to the
heterogeneous hardware budgets of edge devices, particularly well-
suited for MoE due to its inherent structural sparsity, which causes
imbalanced importance among experts. While prior work has ap-
plied quantization for efficient MoE inference at the edge, we point
out that in MoE-based VLMs, the modality-specific characteristics
of visual and text tokens further impact expert quantization. To
address this issue, we present MoMoVL, a modality-aware adaptive
compression framework for MoE-based VLMs that brings vision-
language inference within reach of on-device resource budgets.
Motivated by our empirical findings on modality-induced dispar-
ities across experts, MoMoVL jointly exploits the per-expert acti-
vation profile and per-modality quantization sensitivity to guide
bit-width assignment. Experimental results show that MoMoVL
improves the accuracy-compression trade-off relative to state-of-
the-art mixed-precision baselines and reduces memory footprint by
up to 15.44% with a modest drop in accuracy over its uniform-
precision baseline. Our code and data are publicly available at
https://github.com/sco-edge/MoMoVL.

Introduction
The rise of the agentic web, where web agents autonomously search,
interact with, and perform tasks across diverse web applications,
has been accompanied by increasing interest in on-device deploy-
ment of the underlying foundation models. At the heart of web
agents [44, 56, 60] are Vision-Language Models (VLMs), equipped
with multimodal capabilities to understand webpage content and
perform visual reasoning [2, 29, 38]. While VLMs are being widely
adopted due to their strong performance in handling various cross-
modal tasks [46, 55], deploying them solely in the cloud raises
concerns around privacy and data security [40], as well as latency
and service cost [47], driving growing demand for their on-device
execution [3, 16, 50]. Recent studies on optimizing VLMs for edge
environments have focused on model pruning [45], knowledge dis-
tillation [17], quantization [33], and hardware acceleration [37].
Despite these efforts, these VLMs are yet to be practically deployed
on edge devices not only due to resource-limited settings, but also
the high heterogeneity in hardware budgets available per device.
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are computationally well-
suited for edge deployment. Scaling up model capacity generally
involves increasing model parameters, which leads to higher com-
putation overhead and latency at inference. Unlike traditional dense
models, in which all parameters are activated for every input, MoE
models activate only a subset of experts for each input token based
on gating decisions [26, 39]. This conditional computation enables
them to achieve performance competitive with dense models of
larger active parameter count, while requiring substantially fewer
FLOPs per input [14]. The sublinear scaling of computation cost
exhibited by MoE models has led to their wide adoption across
various domains including information retrieval [61], automatic
speech recognition [52], and vision-language tasks [3, 9, 43, 49].
The computational advantage of sparse activation, however, does
not extend to memory. Because the multi-expert architecture re-
quires all experts to remain resident in memory regardless of their
activation status, the memory footprint scales with the total param-
eter count rather than the active one. MolmoE-1B [9], for instance,
activates roughly 1.5B parameters per token yet requires all 7.2B
to be held in memory. Such disparity grows with model scale, as
the number of experts increases while the number activated per
token remains relatively fixed. Mitigating the resulting memory
overhead requires compression techniques that reduce bit-widths
while adapting to the differing resource budgets of target devices.
MoE’s multi-expert, sparsely-activated design provides two
unique opportunities for efficient quantization. First, while expert
weights account for the majority of model parameters, e.g., 89.21%
for MolmoE-1B, quantizing expert Feed-Forward Network (FFN)
layers is far less damaging to model quality than quantizing other

[23]. The robustness of experts to quantization allows them to be
aggressively compressed to 2 bits [13, 42], or even to just 1 bit
[15, 52]. Second, the inherent structural sparsity causes imbalanced
importance among experts, making mixed-precision quantization
particularly effective. A small subset of “hot” experts is activated
more frequently, contributing more to the model output. For ex-
ample, the top 4 most frequently activated experts in MolmoE-1B
account for 25.30% of its total activation count. Previous studies
have utilized these opportunities to address the deployment chal-
lenges in resource-constrained edge devices, focusing on expert
quantization [12, 24] and allocating different bit-widths to each
expert based on its importance [13, 19, 42, 51]. Yet these approaches
target a single-modality architecture, i.e., language-only MoE mod-
els. In MoE-based VLMs, the modality-specific characteristics of
visual and text tokens further impact expert quantization.
We find that in MoE-based VLMs, experts show imbalanced
activation across modalities, leading to varying sensitivity to quan-
tization. It has been well studied that different experts tend to
become highly specialized along the input structure [39], and our
empirical analysis shows that in MoE-based VLMs, this further
extends to the modality axis. As shown in Fig. 2, there exists a clear
distinction between groups of visual-centric experts and text-
centric experts (i.e., experts frequently activated when processing
visual tokens and text tokens, respectively). When quantized, this
expert-level asymmetry is compounded by a token-level one. Text
tokens, despite being fewer in number (cf. Table 2), carry substan-
tially higher information density than spatially redundant visual
tokens [28, 57], making text-centric experts more susceptible to
quantization-induced degradation. Details on these modality-driven
expert asymmetries are discussed in Section 3.
Therefore, applying a unified quantization across these experts,
ignoring both their relative importance and their dominant modal-
ity, leads to suboptimal results. While existing studies propose
various quantization strategies tailored for VLMs, only a few recent
efforts [18, 28, 34, 57] begin to consider the varying sensitivity to
quantization across modalities. Of these, most target dense VLMs
[18, 28, 57], while VEQ [34] is the only such attempt that takes into
account the structural properties of MoE by incorporating expert
importance into the reconstruction loss. However, it operates only
under a fixed, uniform precision (3-bit or 4-bit), making it hard to
fully exploit the available on-device budget, potentially sacrificing
more of the underlying VLM’s capabilities than necessary. Con-
versely, MoPEQ [8] allocates bit-widths at the expert level based
on their relative importance, but remains unaware of the modality-
driven expert differentiation established above. To the best of our
knowledge, no prior work jointly assigns bit-widths across experts
in a modality-aware manner while adapting to the heterogeneous,
device-specific resource budgets of edge deployment.
To address this issue, we present MoMoVL, a Modality-aware
adaptive compression framework for MoE-based VLMs that brings
vision-language inference within reach of on-device resource bud-
gets. The key idea of MoMoVL is to build a per-modality activation
profile for each expert, measuring its activation statistics separately
for visual and text tokens using a small multimodal calibration set.
In addition, MoMoVL identifies how the impact of quantization
noise diverges by modality and uses gradients of the Supervised

• We propose a novel modality-aware, adaptive compression

improves the accuracy-compression trade-off over state-
of-the-art mixed-precision baselines and reduces memory
footprint by up to 15.44% with a modest drop in accuracy
relative to its uniform-precision baseline.

2.2
Serving Vision-Language Models
There have been various efforts to enhance the efficiency of VLMs
deployed in diverse environments, including production environ-
ments [36] or the cloud-edge continuum [47]. However, these chal-
lenges are exacerbated under the tighter resource constraints of
edge deployment. From an architectural perspective, SparseVLM
[58] and Focus [48] dynamically prune redundant visual tokens,
reducing computation overhead while preserving semantic fidelity.
Instead of reducing the number of input tokens, several approaches
aimed to reduce the memory overhead at runtime, focusing on Key-
Value (KV) cache that grows with the number of tokens processed.
To reduce GPU memory usage, V-Rex [22] offloads the full KV cache
to CPU memory or storage and performs selective retrieval to re-
duce data transfer latency. To further reduce memory and I/O over-
head, Ahn et al. [1] apply mixed-precision quantization to KV cache,
assigning higher bit-widths to important tokens based on attention
scores. VLCache [35] avoids costly recomputation by reusing KV
cache when the same image is encountered in a subsequent request.
Both the pruning approaches [48, 58] and the work by Ahn et al.
[1] leverage the relative importance of text tokens, which function
as semantic anchors for context understanding [25, 47, 54], over
spatially redundant visual tokens. On the other hand, several efforts
have reduced model size directly through quantization [18, 28, 57],
taking into account the separation between text tokens and visual
tokens in the latent feature space. Still, these quantization-based
studies target dense VLMs, leading to suboptimal results when
naively applied to MoE-based VLMs.
2.3
Mixture-of-Experts at the Edge
With the increasing adoption of MoE architecture across various
domains, numerous attempts have been made to deploy MoE-based
models on-device by alleviating their memory demand. Many stud-
ies have proposed expert-offloading techniques that leverage MoE’s
sparsely-activated design, storing only frequently activated weights
(e.g., attention layers or shared experts) on scarce GPU memory
and offloading others to CPU memory or SSD storage, loading them
to GPU when activated. Among these, some works perform hy-
brid CPU/GPU inference to further speed up inference [5, 6, 20],
overlapping GPU-CPU computation and I/O events using pipeline
scheduling. However, this approach may not be suitable for devices
with limited CPU resources. In addition to offloading, several stud-
ies quantize experts to reduce their transfer latency and memory
footprint. EdgeMoE [51] statically assigns bit-widths to experts
based on their impact on model accuracy after being quantized. In-
stead, MoE-APEX [42] and Fate [13] dynamically allocate bit-widths
to experts based on runtime signals obtained from current inputs:
gating weights and the popularity of each expert, respectively.

Separately from offloading, a range of techniques have utilized
expert quantization for model compression. MoE-CSP [24], CMoE
[52], and QMoE [15] quantize experts in uniform precision, down
to a minimum of 1 bit, while MC-MoE [19] and MxMoE [11] assign
bit-widths based on reconstruction error, each at the expert level
and the linear block level. Still, these approaches target text-only
MoE-based LLMs, with only a handful of studies aiming at MoE-
based VLMs [8, 34]. MoPEQ [8] analyzes each expert’s importance
using Hessian trace approximation and allocates bit-width to each
expert group based on the mean importance, yet it neglects the
modality-induced disparities across experts. Meanwhile, VEQ [34]
incorporates both expert importance and the sensitivity imbalance
between modalities into its error minimization objective. However,
its static, uniform quantization lacks flexibility under heterogeneous
resource constraints common in edge environments. In this paper,
we combine the modality-specific characteristics of MoE-based
VLMs with per-expert activation profiles to adaptively allocate
bit-width budgets under per-device resource constraints, jointly
addressing what prior studies have left unresolved.
3
Insight: Modality-Specific Characteristics of
MoE-based VLMs
We begin by presenting two key findings from studying modality-
induced disparities across experts in terms of activation statistics
and quantization sensitivity. Our results demonstrate that modality-
specific characteristics of MoE-based VLMs are an effective basis
for bit-width assignment.
3.1
Imbalanced Activation of Experts Across
Modalities
In this section, we analyze the routing behaviors within each MoE
layer to measure how the expert activation pattern differs between
visual and text tokens. We use a multimodal calibration task that

exercises both the VLM’s visual understanding and language mod-
eling capabilities. We choose the improved COCO caption dataset
from ShareGPT4V [7] and randomly sample 128 image-caption
pairs as the calibration dataset. For each visual and text token, we
record its top-k index, i.e., the indices of experts selected based
on gating weights, and compute the activation frequency of each
expert separately for each token modality.
Fig. 2 shows the modality-specific activation statistics from
MolmoE-1B. Both modalities demonstrate a skewed activation pat-
tern, while visual tokens exhibit a more concentrated routing than
text tokens. When averaged across layers, the 8 most frequently
activated experts take 51.49% of the total activation count when pro-
cessing visual tokens, while the top 8 take 29.63% when given text
tokens. This expert specialization diverges by modality. As shown
in Fig. 2(c), there is a clear distinction between visual-centric experts
and text-centric experts, which are experts frequently activated
when processing visual tokens and text tokens, respectively. Of the
1024 experts across all 16 layers, 61 are activated in more than 50%
when given visual tokens. However, among these 61 visual-centric
experts, only 12 also reach even a 25% activation rate for text to-
kens, indicating that most visual-centric experts contribute less to
processing text. Still, it is worth noting that there are “generalist”
experts that are frequently activated on both modalities. At Layer
0, 4 experts (Expert 7, 9, 41, and 43) are activated more than 20%
for both visual and text tokens, an average of 3.5 experts per layer.
Given that visual tokens vastly outnumber text tokens among all
input tokens (cf. Table 2), a modality-unaware activation profile
would dilute the measured importance of text-centric experts, lead-
ing to suboptimal bit-width assignment. These results indicate that
the expert activation profile should be built on a per-modality basis.
Insight 1: Per-modality activation profiles are required to
capture the true importance of modality-centric experts.

3.2
Modality-Dependent Sensitivity to Expert
Quantization
To study the impact of each expert’s dominant modality on quanti-
zation performance, we select important experts per modality and
quantize them to INT4, while quantizing the remaining experts
to INT2. Based on the per-modality activation statistics measured
in Sec. 3.1, we quantize experts in descending order of modality-
specific popularity, separately prioritizing either text-centric or
visual-centric experts for higher precision. We use Half-Quadratic
Quantization (HQQ) [4], a fast, calibration-free quantization algo-
rithm that supports various bit-widths.
Fig. 3 shows the result of increasing the proportion of experts
quantized to INT4 (i.e., INT4 Proportion) based on per-modality
activation frequency. MMBench-V1.1 [30] is used as the benchmark.
While the overall trend shows an increase in accuracy with higher
INT4 proportion, it is evident that selectively quantizing text-centric
experts to higher bit-width yields higher accuracy than favoring
visual-centric experts, with one exception at an INT4 proportion of
80%. Note that for both visual-centric and text-centric selection, the
accuracy is the same at 0% and 100% as all experts are quantized
to INT2 and INT4, respectively. Prioritizing text-centric experts
improves accuracy by up to 4.98% at an INT4 proportion of 30%. We
attribute this result to the differing quantization sensitivity between
text tokens and visual tokens. Text tokens, while fewer in number,
carry higher information density and play a central role in a VLM’s
context understanding and semantic initialization [28, 47, 57]. This,
in turn, makes text-centric experts that predominantly process text
tokens more susceptible to quantization-induced degradation than
visual-centric experts.
Insight 2: Text-centric experts are more susceptible to quanti-
zation and deserve higher bit-widths than visual-centric experts.

4
MoMoVL Design
Motivated by our findings on modality-driven imbalance across
experts, we propose MoMoVL, a novel modality-aware, adaptive
compression framework tailored for on-device MoE-based VLMs, a
step toward locally deployed web agents that fit within the memory
budgets of edge hardware.
4.1
Framework Overview
The overall workflow of MoMoVL is depicted in Fig. 4. MoMoVL
first builds an activation profile of each expert separately for visual
and text tokens using a small multimodal calibration set (➊). To
measure expert importance, MoMoVL uses one of two types of
activation statistics: Top-K Weight or Top-K Index. Top-K Weight
denotes the 𝑘-highest routing probabilities assigned by the gat-
ing function to the 𝑘selected experts, given the current input. As
shown in Eq. (1), the contribution of each routed expert to the
output is proportional to its gating weight, i.e., an expert with a
higher gating weight has a greater impact on the final output. Mean-
while, Top-K Index denotes the indices of the 𝑘selected experts. An
expert with a higher activation frequency is treated as more impor-
tant. We refer to the implementation of MoMoVL based on Top-K
Weight as MoMoVL-Weight, and the version based on Top-K Index
as MoMoVL-Index. In addition, MoMoVL measures how the impact
of quantization noise diverges by modality, using the gradients

The resulting assignment profile is stored as a JSON file specifying
the target bit-width for each expert. Finally, MoMoVL quantizes the
base model according to this quantization configuration, deploying
the quantized model on the target device (➌).
4.2
Measuring Modality-Specific Sensitivities
In this section, we discuss how MoMoVL measures the per-modality
sensitivity. As shown in Sec. 3.2, visual tokens and text tokens
carry different information density and thus have varying impacts
on quantization performance. The question is how to quantify
their impact so that we can make a balanced measure of expert
importance based on visual- and text-centric activation statistics.
Motivated by MBQ [28] that incorporates gradient-based sensitivity
indicators to balance reconstruction loss across modalities, we opt
to use the modality-level aggregate of each token gradient derived
from the SFT loss as a per-modality sensitivity measure.
As in Sec. 3.1, we use image-caption pairs from the COCO dataset
and apply each model’s official chat template to the input image-
caption pair to create an instructional format. The SFT loss is com-
puted only from the assistant’s response tokens [29], while the
resulting gradient backpropagates to earlier tokens via causal at-
tention. For MoE layer 𝑙, we compute the gradient of the token at
position 𝑡as its mean absolute value over all hidden dimensions
and define the modality-specific sensitivities for a given sample 𝑛as
the sum of gradients of visual tokens and text tokens, respectively:

last prompt token’s hidden state, which is a text token in correctly
formatted prompts. Moreover, during the decode phase, every input
token is a text token generated at the previous forward pass. This
finding leads MoMoVL to assign higher bit-widths exclusively to
text-centric experts at the last layer, by design.

Given a set of bit-widths, each with its own budget share of the
total resource budget, MoMoVL ranks experts by this importance
score in descending order and processes them in sequence. MoMoVL
assigns each expert the highest available bit-width, proceeding to
the next lower bit-width once the accumulated importance exceeds
that bit-width’s threshold, i.e., its allocated fraction of the total
importance score. As discussed in Sec. 4.1, the hardware budget
and each bit-width’s share can be generally instantiated by taking
into account diverse deployment challenges. MoMoVL can also
assign bit-widths at finer granularity as long as those bit-widths
are supported by the quantization algorithm used. In this work, we
use INT2 and INT4, which are widely used in expert quantization
[13, 42, 51], and set the fraction of the combined importance metric
as the device-specific budget share of each bit-width.
5
Evaluation
In this section, we evaluate how MoMoVL enhances on-device
vision-language understanding. Our evaluation is guided by the
following three key research questions:
• RQ1: Does MoMoVL improve the accuracy-compression
trade-off over state-of-the-art mixed-precision baselines?
• RQ2: How do different types of activation statistics affect
the accuracy-compression trade-off?
• RQ3: How does each component of MoMoVL contribute to
overall performance?
5.1
Experimental Setup
Models. We evaluate MoMoVL using two popular MoE-based
VLMs: MolmoE-1B [9] and DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny [49]. As summa-
rized in Table 1, both models employ 64 experts per layer, while
DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny additionally uses 2 shared experts that are al-
ways activated. Due to limited GPU resources available, we focus
on models under 10B parameters. We expect MoMoVL to generalize
to larger models, since they share the same structural properties
and modality-dependent characteristics that motivate our design.
Benchmarks. We evaluate MoMoVL on eight widely used public
benchmarks: MMMU [53], MMBench-V1.1 [30], SEEDBench [27],
ScienceQA [32], RealWorldQA [41], MME-RealWorld [59], AI2D
[21], and OCRBench [31]. These benchmarks encompass diverse
tasks spanning multi-discipline reasoning, question answering in
real-world and scientific scenarios, and document understanding,
which are capabilities central to how web agents interpret visual
content. For each benchmark, we use the standard evaluation proto-
col and prompts from VLMEvalKit [10], an open-source toolkit for
evaluating large multimodal models. The details of these evaluation
benchmarks are summarized in Table 2.
Baselines. We compare MoMoVL with uniform-precision quantiza-
tion methods at various bit-widths and two state-of-the-art (SOTA)
mixed-precision quantization methods to evaluate its efficacy. We
separately evaluate MoMoVL-Weight and MoMoVL-Index to study
how the type of activation statistics affects overall performance. We
note that only the LLM backbone is quantized, with all other VLM
components kept at their original precision. For uniform-precision
quantization, we quantize either the entire LLM (Full-Model) or
only its experts (Expert-Only).

popularity estimated by aggregating token-to-expert assignments.
Originally, both methods prefetch experts required at layer 𝑙+1 by
feeding layer 𝑙’s input into layer 𝑙+1’s gating function, predicting
expert importance to assign bit-widths in advance. To evaluate the
performance upper bound of these modality-unaware approaches,
we set them in an idealized condition where each layer’s expert im-
portance is assessed from that layer’s true gating output at runtime.
This isolates the effect of modality-unawareness in their bit-width
assignment by removing accuracy loss due to prediction error. We
note that their model sizes are input-dependent due to dynamic
precision allocation and are therefore not reported in Table 3.
Quantization Settings. For MoMoVL and its baselines, we use
HQQ [4] as the quantization algorithm, as discussed in Sec. 3.2.
MoMoVL is orthogonal to the underlying quantization algorithm
and could be paired with other post-training quantization methods.
For MoMoVL and its mixed-precision baselines, experts assigned a
higher bit-width are quantized to INT4 while the remaining experts
are quantized to INT2. Shared experts are fixed at INT4 across all
methods. Fate, following its original implementation, assigns INT4
to all experts during the decode phase. For a fair comparison, we
adopt the original parameters of the baselines as the budget share
for 4-bit precision, i.e., INT4 threshold, using 0.6 (MoE-APEX) for
MoMoVL-Weight and 0.5 (Fate) for MoMoVL-Index.
5.2
Main Results
Table 3 presents the detailed comparison results on MolmoE-1B
and DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny. For MolmoE-1B, MoMoVL-Weight out-
performs its mixed-precision baselines on most tasks except for
ScienceQA and OCRBench. MoMoVL-Index, whose activation statis-
tics (Top-K Index) match Fate’s frequency-based mechanism, yields
better performance except on MMMU and ScienceQA. Even though
MoE-APEX and Fate dynamically allocate bit-widths to experts
based on runtime input at each layer, their modality-unawareness
leads to suboptimal results, highlighting the benefit of modality-
aware expert importance. For DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, MoMoVL-Index
performs competitively with the baselines, achieving the highest
MMMU and RealWorldQA scores, though both MoMoVL variants
trail the baselines on several benchmarks. We reason that this result
stems from DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny’s smaller parameter count, which
could leave less capacity to absorb quantization error.
When compared with Uniform (Expert-Only), MoMoVL’s overall
performance sits between the two extremes, i.e., all experts at INT4
or at INT2, showing the impact of INT4 proportion on model per-
formance. We discuss this accuracy-compression trade-off and the
effect of activation statistics type in Sec. 5.3. The performance gap
between Uniform (Full-Model) and Uniform (Expert-Only) at INT4
shows the efficiency of expert quantization. As experts account
for the vast majority of model parameters, extending quantiza-
tion to non-expert layers results in only an 8.31% reduction in
memory footprint for MolmoE-1B, and 4.98% for DeepSeek-VL2-
Tiny, both accompanied by an overall decrease in performance.
Still, MoMoVL demonstrates its efficacy in model compression.
MoMoVL-Weight further reduces the memory footprint of Uni-
form (Full-Model), quantized at INT4, by 15.44% in MolmoE-1B and
10.49% in DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, at the cost of a modest accuracy re-
duction on most benchmarks, notably outperforming this baseline
on RealWorldQA despite its lower average bit-width.

6
Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed MoMoVL, a novel modality-aware, adap-
tive compression framework tailored for MoE-based VLMs that
run under heterogeneous, device-specific resource budgets. Built
on our empirical findings on modality-induced disparities across
experts, MoMoVL jointly exploits the structural properties and
modality-dependent characteristics of multimodal MoE models to
guide bit-width assignment. The experimental results demonstrated
the benefits of MoMoVL in achieving a better accuracy-compression
trade-off over state-of-the-art mixed-precision quantization base-
lines, and its efficacy in model compression, reducing memory foot-
print by up to 15.44% at the cost of a modest accuracy reduction.
By making multimodal inference practical under tight, on-device
resource budgets, MoMoVL brings the vision-language reasoning
capabilities central to the web agent’s perception of visual content
within reach of edge deployment.

Ethics and Privacy Statement
This work presents methodological advances in mixed-precision
quantization for MoE-based VLMs, enabling more accurate models
to run under tighter on-device resource budgets. MoMoVL’s offline
calibration relies solely on publicly available datasets and requires
no access to user data. Our method does not introduce novel ethical
risks beyond those already inherent in multimodal foundation mod-
els, such as potential biases from training data and susceptibility to
hallucination or misuse. We note, however, that broadening access
to more capable VLMs on low-cost edge hardware could also facili-
tate undesirable uses, such as unauthorized visual surveillance. Still,
we believe the benefits of enabling privacy-preserving, on-device
multimodal inference for applications such as personal web agents
or web accessibility tools outweigh this risk, and we advocate for
the responsible use of MoMoVL in benign applications.
