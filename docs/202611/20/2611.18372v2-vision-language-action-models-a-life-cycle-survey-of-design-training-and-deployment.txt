Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models adapt web-pretrained vision-language models to output
robot actions, so that web knowledge carries over to physical control. In under four years the field
has grown from RT-2, the first VLA, to open models that fine-tune on a single consumer GPU
and proprietary generalists that clean unseen homes. VLA research joins robotics, computer
vision and language modelling, so this survey is written for researchers and practitioners from
each of these fields. It reviews 89 works from December 2022 to October 2026, mainly on
manipulation and navigation, plus humanoid control, world-action models and evaluation. Seven
design axes follow a VLA’s life cycle: model design (inputs and memory, architecture, action
output, intermediate predictions), training (pretraining data, post-training) and deployment
(real-time execution). We compare 22 models on these axes, with their training data, model
size, inference speed and hardware. Three trends emerge. (i) Training data has broadened
beyond teleoperated robot data (about 10,000 hours for π0) to community-collected episodes
and egocentric human video (20,000 hours for GR00T N1.7). (ii) Model sizes have split into
two groups: most recent models have 3–7 billion parameters; a new group under one billion
runs on consumer GPUs. (iii) Generalist VLAs handling both manipulation and navigation are
emerging, such as UniVLA and Qwen-VLA. We also compare VLAs with world-action models,
which predict future observations as well as actions, and review evidence that some VLAs with
near-perfect benchmark scores fail after small scene changes. A practitioner’s guide gives a
default for each design decision, and a glossary supports readers new to the field.

Introduction
Large language and vision-language models (VLMs) acquire broad semantic knowledge from internet-
scale data. Robot learning, by contrast, has historically trained a separate policy for each robot,
task and environment, using datasets several orders of magnitude smaller. Vision-language-action
(VLA) models connect the two. A VLA starts from a pretrained VLM and fine-tunes it to emit
actions alongside, or instead of, text. The term was introduced with RT-2 [1], which expressed
robot actions as text tokens, co-fine-tuned a VLM on robot trajectories and web data, and showed
emergent semantic generalisation. Since then the design space has grown at every stage of a VLA’s
life cycle. In the model, action heads now range from discrete tokens to diffusion and flow matching,
and pipelines from single networks to dual-system and hierarchical designs. In training, data has
grown from tens of thousands of teleoperated episodes to tens of thousands of hours of multi-robot
experience, supplemented by human video, simulation and the robot’s own autonomous attempts.
In deployment, models that compute at a few hertz must still control robots smoothly at tens of
hertz.

At a glance. The seven design axes (Section 3, Figure 2) follow a VLA through its life cycle.
• Model: what it is.
– Inputs and memory: what the model observes (cameras, language, robot state, extra conditioning)
and how much of the past it keeps.
– Architecture: the structure of the whole pipeline, not the backbone network: one network, a dual
system of a slow VLM and a fast action module, or a hierarchy of separately trained models.
– Action output: how it turns vision-language features into motor commands, from discrete tokens
to diffusion, flow matching and regression, usually as a chunk of future actions.
– Intermediate predictions: what it predicts besides actions, either reasoning in text (chain of
thought, subtasks) or future observations from a world model, which adds a training signal about
physical dynamics.
• Training: how it learns.
– Pretraining data: what it learns from (robot demonstrations, data pooled across robot bodies,
web data, human video, simulation and generated trajectories) and how these are combined.
– Post-training: how it is adapted and improved for a target robot or task, from fine-tuning recipes
to reinforcement learning from deployment.
• Deployment: how it runs.
– Real-time execution: how a model that runs at a few hertz still controls a robot smoothly, through
action chunking, asynchronous inference and splits between a remote server and the robot.
Three trends emerge:
• Broader training data: from about 10k hours of teleoperated robot data (π0), to 23k community-
collected episodes (SmolVLA), to large-scale egocentric human video (20k hours for GR00T N1.7);
• Model sizes have split: most recent models have 3–7B parameters, while a new group under one billion
runs on consumer GPUs;
• Generalist VLAs: models that handle both manipulation and navigation are emerging (UniVLA, Qwen-
VLA).
This survey has five aims:
• A life-cycle taxonomy. We organise VLA research along seven design axes that follow a
VLA’s life cycle, from model design through training to deployment (Section 3), so that a new
model can be placed by the choices it makes on each; Table 3 places 22 main models this way.
• Manipulation and navigation side by side. Most VLA surveys concentrate on manip-
ulation. We treat navigation VLAs as a parallel lineage (Section 5), cover locomotion and
humanoid whole-body control (Section 6), and discuss the recent convergence of manipulation
and navigation (Section 7).
• A data-centred quantitative view. For every work we extract the training data each paper
reports and plot the field’s trajectory in robot episodes and in parameter count (Figure 7,
Tables 5 and 6).
• VLAs versus world-action models, and fair evaluation. We compare VLAs with
world-action models (Section 8) and review standard benchmarks and the recent efforts to
make VLA evaluation fair and predictive of real-world performance (Section 9).
• Open problems, including a reporting problem we encountered directly: training data is
described in incompatible units, or not quantified at all, in a large share of papers (Section 10).

Scope and selection.
We include works that condition a learned policy on vision and language
and produce robot actions or action-level intermediates. Candidates were found by starting from
widely used VLAs, following their references and the works that cite them, and searching arXiv;
this is a structured, not a systematic, review. A work is discussed in the text if it meets at least one
of three criteria:
1. it introduces an original idea, such as a new action representation, architecture or training
signal;
2. it makes clear progress on a core problem (manipulation, navigation or locomotion), such as a
large gain in real-robot success or a new capability;
3. it gives the community a tool, dataset, benchmark or method that others build on.
Works that mainly add a small performance gain or apply an existing method to a new domain are
cited but not discussed. We exclude systems announced without method details; version updates
described in release notes are included when the base model’s method is published (GR00T N1.5–
N1.7). A small number of non-VLA precursors (RT-1, ACT, Diffusion Policy, PaLM-E, Open
X-Embodiment) are included because later VLAs build directly on them. The survey covers 89

Related surveys.
Ma et al. [2] gave the first taxonomy of VLAs, with three strands: building
blocks, low-level controllers and high-level planners. Sapkota et al. [3] cover more than 80 models
with an emphasis on applications. Zhang et al. [4] classify over 300 studies into autoregressive,
diffusion, reinforcement-based and hybrid paradigms. Kawaharazuka et al. [5] take a full-stack view
that includes hardware, data collection and evaluation. Cui et al. [6] survey and empirically compare
dual-system architectures. More recent surveys take narrower or different cuts. Shao et al. [7] divide
VLM-based manipulation VLAs into monolithic models, with single- or dual-system designs, and
hierarchical models that plan through interpretable intermediates. Zhong et al. [8] organise VLAs
by the kind of action token they produce, Yu et al. [9] review efficiency across models, training
and data, and Xu et al. [10] trace VLAs from their modules through milestones to open challenges.
On the world-model side, Hou et al. [11] review world models for robot learning, Lu et al. [12]
survey world-action models, Yao et al. [13] grade world models by the capabilities that improve
behaviour, Zhang et al. [14] give a concise tutorial, and Mehta et al. [15] connect representation
learning, VLAs and world models in one taxonomy. Our survey differs in four ways: it organises the
design space along the life cycle of a VLA and places the main models on it, with a practitioner’s
guide; it treats navigation and humanoid control as lineages alongside manipulation; it compares
world-action models with VLAs from the VLA side; and it extracts training data, model size and
inference speed from each paper’s full text for a quantitative, data-centred comparison.
How to read this survey.
Section 2 sets out the problem and the precursors of VLAs. Section 3
defines the seven life-cycle axes, places the main models on them (Table 3) and collects their
deployment figures (Table 2). Sections 4–6 trace how manipulation, navigation and humanoid VLAs
evolved, and Section 7 discusses where these lines meet. Section 8 compares VLAs with world-action
models, Section 9 reviews benchmarks and fair evaluation, Section 10 sets out open challenges and
Section 11 summarises the findings. Appendix A is a practitioner’s guide to building a VLA, and
Appendix B explains the terms used. Readers new to VLAs may start with Appendix B, Figure 1
and Sections 2, 4 and 5; readers building a model with Section 3 and Appendix A; and readers
looking for research problems with Sections 9 and 10.

2.2
Precursors
RT-1 [23] trained a 35M-parameter transformer on about 130k real episodes from 13 robots of a
single type. Its ablations showed that data diversity matters more than data volume. PaLM-E [24]
injected continuous sensor encodings into a 562B-parameter language model as a high-level planner
and showed positive transfer from mixing tasks. Diffusion Policy [25] cast visuomotor control as
conditional denoising and handled multimodal action distributions, with an average improvement of
46.9% over prior methods. ACT [18] introduced action chunking on low-cost bimanual hardware
and learned fine skills from 50 demonstrations. Open X-Embodiment [26] pooled more than 1M
trajectories from 22 embodiments and 21 institutions into a standard format. It became a common
pretraining corpus for academic VLAs; most industrial models train mainly on their own data.

it learns: the data it is pretrained on (Section 3.5) and how it is adapted afterwards (Section 3.6).
The last describes how it runs (Section 3.7). Section 3.8 places 22 main models on all seven axes.

3.1
Inputs and memory
Following Equation (1), most VLAs observe one or more camera images, a language instruction and,
in manipulation, the robot’s proprioceptive state, at the current time step only. Two extensions
recur. First, the conditioning has become richer: Hi Robot accepts open-ended prompts and live user
corrections [19], π0.7 conditions on episode metadata and subgoal images [27], and cross-embodiment
models add prompts or tokens that identify the robot, dataset or camera view (Section 3.5). Second,
some models observe the past.
Memory. Most VLAs are effectively Markovian. Long observation histories are costly to process,
and policies trained on them can latch onto cues that do not cause the correct action [16, 28].
MemER [16] has a high-level VLM nominate keyframes that are consolidated by 1D clustering
into a compact episodic memory, enabling manipulation tasks that need several minutes of recall.
StreamVLN [29] combines a fast sliding-window context with a slow, 3D-aware pruned memory
and reuses the transformer’s key–value (KV) cache to bound latency. VLingNav maintains a
visual-assisted linguistic memory to avoid re-exploring the same places. NaVid [30] uses the video
history itself as spatio-temporal context.
3.2
Architecture
Figure 3 contrasts three architectural patterns. The split follows Shao et al. [7], except that we
treat dual-system designs as a pattern of their own rather than as a kind of monolithic model.
Monolithic models such as RT-2, OpenVLA and π0 map observations directly to actions. We
count π0 as monolithic even though its action expert has separate weights, because the expert is
part of the same transformer and attends to the VLM’s tokens at every layer; dual systems instead
pass a single latent from one network to another. π0.5 [31] keeps a single network but predicts a
text subtask before its actions, an internal hierarchy.
Dual-system models couple a slow VLM (“System 2”) with a fast action module (“System 1”)
through latent features and train them jointly: GR00T N1 [32], CogACT [33]. Gemini Robotics [34]
also splits a cloud-hosted VLM backbone from an on-robot action decoder, but does not describe
how the two are connected or trained. OpenHelix [6] provides a systematic empirical comparison of

Hierarchical models make the intermediate explicit and interpretable. Hi Robot [19] reasons

3.3
Action output
How a VLA turns VLM features into continuous robot actions is the most actively explored axis.
Table 1 summarises the main families.
Two refinements cut across these families. First, adding an untrained action expert to a pretrained
VLM can damage the VLM’s semantic knowledge and slow training. Knowledge insulation [51]
stops gradients flowing from the expert into the backbone while the backbone learns from discrete
action tokens. Second, the denoising schedule itself can adapt: AsyncVLA’s asynchronous flow
matching [52]1 uses a non-uniform, context-aware schedule and refines low-confidence action tokens
before execution.
3.4
Intermediate predictions
Besides actions, many VLAs predict outputs that make their decisions explicit or anticipate what
will happen next. These take two forms: reasoning in text, and predictions of future observations.
In hierarchical systems such outputs are passed from one model to another (Section 3.2); here we
focus on what is predicted.

Reasoning before acting. Embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT) [54] trains a VLA to reason
through plans, subtasks, motions, bounding boxes and gripper positions before acting, improving
OpenVLA’s absolute success rate by 28% without additional robot data. Gemini Robotics 1.5 [55]
interleaves natural-language reasoning with actions. In navigation, VLingNav [56] uses adaptive
chain-of-thought, reasoning explicitly only when needed.
Predicting the future. A growing set of VLAs predict future observations as well as actions.
3D-VLA [57] generates goal images and point clouds with aligned diffusion models. GR-2 [58] is
pretrained as a video generator before learning actions. VLA-JEPA [43], built on the joint-embedding
predictive architecture (JEPA), predicts embeddings of future frames rather than their pixels, so it
is not trained to reproduce irrelevant visual detail, and the part of the model that predicts never
sees the future frames themselves. π0.7 uses a lightweight world model to produce subgoal images.
These models are a bridge to world-action models, which jointly predict future observations and
actions; Section 8 compares the two families.
3.5
Pretraining data
Pretraining data has grown from RT-1’s 130k episodes [23], to OpenVLA’s 970k curated Open
X-Embodiment episodes [36], to about 10k hours for π0 [17], about 20k hours for LingBot-VLA [59],
and “tens of thousands of hours” for π∗
0.6 [60]. LingBot-VLA reports steady downstream gains as
pretraining grows from 3k to 20k hours. Several mechanisms allow heterogeneous action spaces to
be trained together:
• a physically interpretable unified action space (RDT-1B [45]);
• per-source learnable soft prompts, learned embeddings that tell the model which dataset and
robot each example comes from (X-VLA [48]);
• embodiment-aware text prompts (Qwen-VLA [61]);
• a Motion Transfer mechanism, whose design the paper does not describe (Gemini Robotics
1.5 [55]);
• camera-view and temporal identifier tokens for navigation (NavFoM [62]).
Figure 4 shows the robot bodies these models cover.
Co-training mixes robot actions with other data, most often web visual question answering
(VQA) and captioning. RT-2 brought it to VLAs [1], and PaLM-E, π0.5, MolmoAct, NaVid and
NavFoM follow it [24, 30, 31, 35, 62]. Its value depends on the data: in π0.5’s ablations, data from
other robots and environments was important for performance in unseen homes, whereas web data
mainly helped with objects outside the robot data; the web data’s effect on overall task success was
not statistically significant [31].
Beyond teleoperation. Training data increasingly comes from other sources (Figure 5):
• human and web video: 38M clips for GR-2 [58], Something-Something v2 [73] for LAPA and
VLA-JEPA [41, 43], Ego4D [74] for UniVLA [42], and YouTube tours for LeLaN [69];
• simulation: 780k trajectories for GR00T N1 [32] and 7.2M for Qwen-VLA [61];
• video-generated “neural trajectories” (GR00T N1);
• failed and autonomous episodes, made usable by telling the policy how good each episode or

3.6
Post-training
Supervised fine-tuning on the target robot is the default. OpenVLA-OFT [49] distils a recipe of
parallel decoding, action chunking, continuous actions and an L1 loss. It raises OpenVLA’s LIBERO
average (Section 9) from 76.5% to 97.1% and its throughput by 26×. Reinforcement learning from
deployment has recently become practical at scale. RECAP [60] trains a value function and a policy
conditioned on the advantage, that is, on how much better than expected each action turned out,
using demonstrations, autonomous rollouts and expert interventions together. It was applied to
laundry folding, espresso making and box assembly; on the hardest of these tasks it more than
doubles throughput and roughly halves the failure rate. VLingNav adds an online, expert-guided
RL stage to navigation. π0.7 [27] instead conditions on episode metadata and subgoal images, so
lower-quality data improves the model rather than degrading it.
3.7
Real-time execution
Large VLAs run at a few hertz, which leads to pauses or jerky transitions at chunk boundaries
(Figure 6). SmolVLA [47] decouples action prediction from execution with an asynchronous inference
stack. Real-Time Chunking [82] overlaps the two: while one chunk executes, it plans the next,
keeping fixed the actions that will run before the new chunk is ready and filling in the rest so the
two chunks join smoothly. It runs at inference time on existing diffusion and flow-matching policies,
without retraining. AsyncVLA (navigation) [53] runs a large VLA on a remote workstation and
a lightweight edge adapter on the robot. It tolerates communication delays of up to 6 seconds;
among pedestrians it reaches the goal without collision in 85% of trials, which its authors report as
40% above the strongest baseline. Hierarchical systems often run the two levels at different rates:
MemER runs its high-level policy at about 1 Hz and its low-level policy at about 2 Hz [16].

Locomotion and Humanoid Whole-Body Control
Manipulation VLAs usually output end-effector poses, and navigation VLAs output velocities or
waypoints; in both cases a separate controller executes them. On a humanoid this split is harder,
because the controller must keep the robot balanced while the legs, waist and arms move together,
and LeVERB argues that fixed action interfaces of this kind restrict humanoids to slow, quasi-static
tasks [110]. Suitable data is also scarce: humanoid teleoperation is expensive [65], and dynamically
consistent demonstrations are rare [66]. Two designs have emerged (Table 7).
Hierarchical: a vision-language layer over a learned whole-body controller.
This mirrors
the hierarchical VLAs of Section 3, and NaVILA’s language commands over an RL locomotion policy
are a navigation precursor [22]. Humanoid-VLA [111] pre-aligns language with motion using human
motion datasets paired with text, then adds egocentric video through parameter-efficient fine-tuning,
on top of a whole-body control architecture. LeVERB [110] trains a vision-language policy to
emit latent “verbs” that a reinforcement-learned whole-body controller turns into dynamics-level
commands. It also introduces a closed-loop benchmark of over 150 tasks in 10 categories, where
it reaches 58.5% overall success, 7.8 times a naive hierarchical baseline. SONIC [112] scales the
controller itself: a motion-tracking foundation model of up to 42M parameters, trained on 700
hours of motion capture with 21k GPU hours, whose token space serves both VR teleoperation and
VLAs. WholeBodyVLA [65] learns latent actions from action-free egocentric human video and pairs
the VLA with an RL policy specialised for loco-manipulation moves such as advancing, turning
and squatting. On the AgiBot X2 humanoid it reaches 78.0% average success, 21.3 percentage
points above the strongest VLA baseline (OpenVLA-OFT, 56.7%) and 14 points above a modular,
non-VLA baseline (64.0%).

Convergence of Manipulation and Navigation
The two communities have long used different action spaces (end-effector or joint commands versus
waypoints), benchmarks (LIBERO, SimplerEnv [114] and CALVIN versus VLN-CE) and model
families. Two recent models are early signs of unification:
• UniVLA [42] learns latent actions from manipulation data, navigation data and human video
together, and reports results on both manipulation benchmarks and R2R.
• Qwen-VLA [61] treats manipulation, navigation and trajectory prediction as one prediction
problem. A single model reaches 97.9% on LIBERO and 59.6% success on RxR. Its authors
find no significant loss from training on several robot types together, in an ablation on two
benchmarks.
Two related lines stop short of unification. Mobile manipulators such as π0.5 [31] combine moving and
manipulating on one robot, but are trained and evaluated as manipulation systems. NavFoM [62]
covers very different bodies, from quadrupeds to cars, but only for navigation. In our view, the main
obstacle to a single model for both skills may be shifting from architecture to data and evaluation.
In terms of the life-cycle axes, both unifying models act at the training stage, through pooled data
and a shared action interface (latent actions in UniVLA, one prediction problem in Qwen-VLA),
while their evaluation remains split across separate manipulation and navigation benchmarks.

VLAs and World-Action Models
A second family of robot foundation models starts from video rather than from language. A world
model predicts how the environment changes as an agent acts [115, 116]. World-action models
(WAMs) extend this idea by predicting future observations and robot actions together. The earliest
robotics use of the term we found is DyWA [117]; most current WAMs start from a model pretrained
to generate video. Where a VLA learns π(At | ot) from action supervision alone, a WAM learns to
predict future observations together with actions,

models for robot learning [11] have dedicated surveys; here we compare WAMs with VLAs. The
two families start from different pretrained models and fail in different ways (Figure 9, Table 8).
As the UniWAM authors argue [118], a VLA’s vision-language model has learned language and
object meaning from image–text data, but its only training signal about physics is robot actions.
A WAM’s video model has learned how scenes change over time, but not how to follow or reason
about language.
From video plans to joint models.
Two terms recur here: a forward dynamics model predicts
the next observation from the current one and an action, and an inverse dynamics model infers
the action that links two observations. UniPi [122] cast decision-making as text-conditioned video
generation: a model synthesises a video of the planned behaviour, and an inverse dynamics model
recovers actions from the generated frames. Video Prediction Policy [124] skips full video generation:
it feeds a video diffusion model’s internal features about what will happen next to an inverse
dynamics model, giving an 18.6% relative improvement on CALVIN ABC→D. The Unified Video
Action model [119] learns one shared representation of video and actions, with separate diffusion
heads for each. Action inference can then skip video generation entirely, and masking different
inputs lets the same model act as a forward or an inverse dynamics model. WorldVLA [120] merges
a VLA and a world model in one autoregressive model and reports that each improves the other.

diffusion model, predicting future video and actions jointly with an autoregressive transformer
trained by flow matching. After each action chunk it replaces its predicted frames with the real
observations, so errors do not accumulate, and model and system optimisations give a 38× speed-up
that allows closed-loop control at about 7 Hz. Trained on about 500 hours of diverse, non-repetitive
real-robot data, it reports more than twice the average task progress of state-of-the-art VLAs on
unseen tasks and environments, and it adapts to a new robot from 30 minutes of play data.
Beyond video diffusion.
Two 2026 preprints take WAMs in different directions. LeWAM [123]
makes them cheaper: it replaces the video diffusion backbone with predictive JEPA embeddings
and reaches 92.3% on RoboTwin 2.0 [78] with 0.4B trainable parameters. UniWAM [118] moves
them towards VLAs by adding a reasoning module, the “physical reasoner”, alongside its video
generator and action predictor in one architecture. It reports a log-linear scaling law for co-training
on human and robot data.

• As the policy backbone, as in the WAMs above.
• As a data generator. DreamGen [77] turns video world models into a source of synthetic
“neural trajectories”. With them, a humanoid learned 22 new behaviours from teleoperation
data for a single task, and DreamGen’s video benchmark correlates with downstream policy
success. GR00T N1 used about 300k such trajectories [32].
• As an evaluator. Neural simulators such as Genie Envisioner’s GE-Sim produce closed-loop

Benchmarks and Fair Evaluation
Is there a standard benchmark?
Not yet. No single benchmark is accepted as the measure of
a VLA. Instead, a small set of simulation suites has become the de facto standard (Table 9), while
real-world evaluation remains lab-specific and rarely comparable across papers.
What goes wrong with current practice.
• Saturation. LIBERO scores now cluster near the ceiling: OpenVLA-OFT reports 97.1% [49],
Qwen-VLA 97.9% [61], X-VLA about 98% [48] and SimVLA 98.6% [63]. VLA-0, VLA-Adapter
and SimVLA reach these scores without any robot pretraining [39, 50], so the benchmark can
no longer show what large-scale pretraining adds.
• Memorisation and brittleness. Two independent perturbation suites agree that high
LIBERO scores overstate robustness. In LIBERO-PRO [129], OpenVLA and π0, both above
90% on standard LIBERO, drop to 0% once objects are moved slightly from their training
positions; π0.5 holds up better, keeping 38% on one task suite. LIBERO-Plus [130] tests
models including OpenVLA-OFT and π0 under seven kinds of perturbation: changing the
camera viewpoint or the robot’s starting pose cuts success from about 95% to under 30%, and
many models largely ignore the instruction.
• Confounded comparisons. Architectural changes usually arrive together with different data,
backbones and training schedules, so many reported gains are hard to attribute; SimVLA’s
controlled baseline (Section 4) makes this concrete [63].

Figure 10 arranges these efforts from cheap and reproducible

• Robustness-oriented benchmarks. LIBERO-PRO and LIBERO-Plus replace fixed test
scenes with systematic perturbations; RoboTwin 2.0 builds domain randomisation into the
benchmark itself.
• Simulation validated against reality. SimplerEnv [114] builds simulated copies of real
robot setups, tuned to match their control behaviour and appearance, and finds that policy
results in these copies correlate strongly with real-world results.
• Scalable real-world evaluation. AutoEval [131] automates real-robot testing: it detects
success and resets the scene itself, so evaluations can run day and night, and its scores closely
match human-run trials; its WidowX scenes are open to the community. RoboArena [132]
instead spreads evaluation across seven institutions that use the same DROID robot. Evaluators
compare two policies blind, and from more than 600 such comparisons it ranks policies more
accurately than a single central evaluation.
• World models as evaluators. Video-model simulators are a newer option: DreamGen
Bench [77] reports that its video-model scores correlate with downstream policy success, and
GE-Sim [125] runs policies in a generated world, but neither has yet been checked against
real-robot results.
• Controlled baselines. SimVLA argues for reporting results against a fixed, transparent
baseline and recipe, so that gains can be attributed to the change being studied.
• Efficiency as a metric. VLA-Perf [83] provides an analytical model of inference latency
across model designs and on-device, edge and cloud deployment, making speed comparable
alongside success rate.
Together these efforts point to a shared protocol: perturbed rather than fixed test scenes, simulation
scores validated against real robots, distributed real-world comparison, matched training recipes,
and reporting of latency and training data alongside success. Appendix A turns this protocol into a
checklist for evaluating a new model.

10
Open Challenges and Research Directions
We group the open challenges by life-cycle stage, as in Section 3, and add those that cut across it.
Each ends with a research question that the evidence in this survey motivates.
10.1
Model
Memory beyond minutes.
Keyframe and slow-fast memories now support tasks that need
minutes of recall [16, 29], yet only a minority of the models in Table 3 keep any history, and those are
mostly navigation models. Open question: can memory scale from minutes to hours, and combine a
spatial map of where the robot has been with episodic recall of what it did?
World models inside VLAs.
Latent and generative world models are entering VLAs [27, 43, 58],
and WAMs are adding VLM-style reasoning [118] (Section 8). Yet only two of the 22 models in
Table 3, VLA-JEPA and π0.7, predict future observations, and controlled comparisons with and
without future prediction remain rare; WorldVLA’s report that the two improve each other is an
exception [120]. Open question: for which tasks does predicting the future repay its cost in compute
and latency, and how should world models used to generate data or evaluate policies be validated
against real robots?
10.2
Training
Data-mixture design.
Mixture weights are usually set by heuristics, for example per-dataset
weighting by n0.43 in π0 [17] or by size and diversity in Octo [44], together with ad hoc rules such
as dropping DROID late in training [38]. The few controlled ablations show that sources differ in
what they teach: in π0.5, cross-embodiment data mattered most in unseen homes and web data
mainly for unseen objects [31]. Open question: which data sources build which capabilities, and can
mixtures be designed from such measurements rather than from total hours?
Learning from deployment.
RECAP [60] and online RL in navigation [56] show that VLAs can
improve from their own experience, but only seven of the 22 models in Table 3 make a distinctive post-
training choice, and RECAP still relies on expert interventions. Open question: can a VLA improve
safely from autonomous experience across many tasks and robots, with little human supervision?
Whole-body data.
Humanoid VLAs still mostly rely on a separately trained whole-body con-
troller, and end-to-end models are trained largely on synthetic or human-motion data [64, 66],
because humanoid teleoperation is expensive (Section 6). Open question: can optimal-control and
human-motion data be combined with limited real teleoperation to give VLAs agile, contact-rich
whole-body control on real humanoids?
10.3
Deployment
Latency and edge deployment.
Asynchronous execution [47, 53, 82] hides latency, and sub-
billion models run on commodity hardware. But every model in Table 2 that names its hardware
runs on a desktop or data-centre GPU, and embedded robot computers appear only for the edge
part of a split system. Open question: can large VLAs be compressed, for example by quantisation,
which OpenVLA shows preserves real-robot performance [36], or by distillation, which the authors
of VLA-0 propose [39], to run on robot hardware without losing their generality?
25


10.4
Across the life cycle
Unifying skills.
UniVLA and Qwen-VLA unify manipulation and navigation through pooled
data and a shared action interface, but their results are still reported on separate benchmarks, and
mobile manipulators are trained and evaluated as manipulation systems (Section 7). Open question:
how should a single model that both moves and manipulates be evaluated on tasks that need both
skills at once?
Predictive evaluation.
Simulation is growing as a data source (GR00T N1, Qwen-VLA), yet few
works measure how well simulated success predicts real-world transfer, and LIBERO scores near
saturation hide brittleness (Section 9). SimplerEnv [114] validated simulated scores against real
robots for two setups. Open question: can benchmark scores be validated against real robots across
many embodiments, so that simulation results predict deployment?
Reporting standards.
While building Tables 5 and 6, we found that training data is reported
inconsistently:
• only 16 of the 43 manipulation works give a single robot-episode count;
• several report only hours, or qualitative descriptions such as “thousands of hours” or “tens of
thousands of hours”;
• eleven state no parameter count for the deployed policy;
• inference speed, when reported, is given as throughput, latency per chunk, frames per second
or effective control rate, on different GPUs (Table 2).
This makes like-for-like comparison of data efficiency and compute impossible. We suggest that
VLA papers report, at minimum, the robot episodes and hours used in each training stage, the
share of non-robot data, the number of embodiments, the total parameter count at inference, and
the latency per action chunk on named hardware; Appendix A lists these as a checklist.
Announcements without published methods.
Three systems announced in 2026 bear on
these directions but have no published method details, so we do not review them or report their
results. NVIDIA previewed GR00T N2, a robot foundation model based on DreamZero with a
world-action-model architecture, in March 2026 [133]. Google introduced Gemini 4 Argon in October
2026 [134] and Gemini 4 Hydrogen in November 2026 [135], general Gemini models that control
robot arms by planning the task and writing and running control code, rather than by outputting
actions as a VLA does.
11
Conclusion
VLA models have advanced from a proof of concept to a diverse ecosystem in under four years.
Followed through their life cycle, the main shifts are clear. In the model, manipulation VLAs now
mostly predict action chunks with flow-matching or diffusion heads, navigation VLAs mostly still
predict one step at a time, and a growing number make their reasoning explicit or predict future
observations. In training, data has broadened from teleoperated robot data to community-collected
episodes, human video, simulation and the robot’s own attempts, while some small models skip
robot pretraining altogether, although their strongest results are on simulation benchmarks. In
deployment, models have split by size: a new group under one billion parameters runs on consumer
26


GPUs, and asynchronous execution hides the latency of larger ones. Generalist VLAs that handle
both manipulation and navigation are emerging, and world-action models offer a complementary,
dynamics-first route. Evaluation is shifting from saturated simulation scores toward robustness tests
and distributed real-world comparison. The most pressing directions are memory beyond minutes,
principled data mixtures, evaluation that predicts real-world transfer, safe learning from deployment,
and consistent reporting of data and latency. We hope the life-cycle taxonomy, the data-centred
tables and the practitioner’s guide in Appendix A help researchers understand the field, build their
own models and choose where to contribute.

A.2
Model
• Inputs and memory. Start with current camera images, language and robot state. Add
history only when the task needs it: long histories cost latency and can teach the policy cues
that do not cause the correct action. When memory is needed, compress it, as with keyframes
(MemER) or a slow-fast context (StreamVLN), rather than feeding raw history.
• Architecture. Start monolithic. Choose a hierarchy when you need interpretable subtasks,
live user corrections, long memory or cheap off-domain training data for the high level (Hi
40


Robot, MemER, HAMSTER). Choose a dual system when a large VLM must coexist with
fast control (GR00T N1).
• Action output. Predict action chunks rather than single steps. Use flow matching or
diffusion for dexterous, high-rate or multimodal behaviour; continuous regression for the
fastest inference on narrow tasks; compressed tokens (FAST) to keep a purely autoregressive
model; and latent actions to learn from action-free video. When adding a new action expert
to a pretrained VLM, protect the VLM with knowledge insulation.
• Intermediate predictions. Add reasoning or subtask prediction for long-horizon tasks and
open-ended instructions; ECoT gained 28 points over OpenVLA without additional robot
data. Predicting future observations is promising but adds cost; consider it when you have
plenty of action-free video (VLA-JEPA) or need subgoals for planning (π0.7).
A.3
Training
• Pretraining data. Favour diversity over volume, as RT-1’s ablations first showed. Data from
other robots and environments mattered more than web data for π0.5 in unseen homes, whereas
web data mainly helped with unseen objects. When mixing robots, tell the model which robot
each example comes from (soft prompts, embodiment prompts or identifier tokens). Keep
every episode, including failures: advantage and metadata conditioning can now turn them
into useful training data.
• Post-training. Several works adapt to a new task with about 50 demonstrations (ACT,
TinyVLA, MemER). Use the OpenVLA-OFT recipe for supervised fine-tuning. Move to
reinforcement learning from deployment (RECAP, online RL) only when you can run many
autonomous trials safely.
A.4
Deployment
• Real-time execution. Measure the latency per action chunk on your own hardware first and
compare it with your control period; a 7B autoregressive VLA runs at only a few hertz on
one RTX 4090 (Table 2). If inference is slower than control, execute chunks asynchronously
(SmolVLA), add Real-Time Chunking, which needs no retraining, or split a remote VLA from
an on-robot adapter when network delays are large (AsyncVLA). Run the levels of a hierarchy
at different rates.
A.5
Evaluation and reporting
• Do not rely on LIBERO alone: it is near saturation and hides brittleness. Add a perturbation
suite (LIBERO-PRO or LIBERO-Plus) and, where possible, SimplerEnv, whose scores were
validated against real robots. For navigation, report R2R-CE and RxR-CE together with
real-robot trials.
• Compare against a controlled baseline trained with the same data and recipe, as SimVLA
advocates, so that a gain can be attributed to the change being studied.
• Report the robot episodes and hours in each training stage, the share of non-robot data, the
number of embodiments, the parameter count at inference and the latency per chunk on
named hardware (Section 10).

Stopping gradients from a new action expert flowing into the VLM, so

Telling the policy how much better than average each action or

3.5
Deployment
Inference vs. control
frequency
How often the model is queried, often a few hertz, versus how often
the robot receives commands, often tens to hundreds of hertz. Action
chunks bridge the two.
3.7
Latency vs.
throughput
The delay between an observation and the resulting action, versus the
number of actions produced per second. Action chunking can raise
throughput without reducing latency.
3.7
Asynchronous
inference
Computing the next chunk while the current one executes, so the
robot does not pause.
3.7
Real-time chunking
Asynchronous inference that keeps fixed the actions about to execute
and plans the rest of the next chunk to join them smoothly.
