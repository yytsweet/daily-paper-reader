ABSTRACT
Latent Action Models (LAMs) learn control representations from action-free video,
but existing pipelines typically train latent policies with behavioral cloning, without
an explicit policy improvement objective. Although offline RL offers a natural
approach to policy improvement, standard methods do not transfer directly to
learned latent action spaces. We find that the structure of learned latent actions
makes naive offline RL unreliable: directly maximizing value over latent actions
or fitting a unimodal policy can produce latent actions that cannot be realized by
physical execution, while the latent representation can merge physical controls
that receive different rewards. To understand when latent-space optimization
remains valid, we derive conditions under which policy improvement transfers to
decoded execution and under which latent and physical optima align. Building on
these insights, we propose LAPIS for goal-conditioned latent policy improvement,
grounding value-based policy improvement in observed state–latent transitions
and using a conditional flow policy to capture their multimodal structure. LAPIS
outperforms baselines on most tasks, with the clearest gains in visual manipulation,
and remains effective even with only 1% action labels, where competing methods
largely collapse.

INTRODUCTION
Latent Action Models (LAMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for learning control represen-
tations from action-free video by inferring latent actions that explain observed state transitions (Ye
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; 2024). Because latent-action learning captures transition and outcome
structure rather than task optimality, it can naturally exploit large collections of diverse and suboptimal
video, including failed trajectories that would be poor demonstrations but remain informative for
learning how the environment changes. Yet existing LAM pipelines typically train the resulting latent
policies with behavioral cloning (BC) (Schmidt & Jiang, 2024; Ye et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024;
Bruce et al., 2024), without explicitly optimizing latent policies for improved task return. This creates
a gap between the broad data that LAMs can learn from and the policies that current pipelines can
extract from it.
Offline reinforcement learning (RL) offers a natural way to improve beyond the observed behav-
ior (Levine et al., 2020; Kostrikov et al., 2022; Fujimoto et al., 2019; Ki et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025),
but applying standard offline RL directly in a learned latent action space is not straightforward. We
identify three properties that distinguish learned latent actions from physical actions. The first two
concern realizability—whether the transition outcome represented by a latent action can be physically
produced from the current state: (i) state-conditioned realizability, whereby a latent action realizable
at one state can be unrealizable at another; and (ii) non-convex realizable set, whereby interpolating
between realizable latent actions can yield unrealizable ones. The third, (iii) outcome-based abstrac-
tion, arises because distinct physical controls that reach the same outcome can map to the same latent
action.
These properties expose two distinct failure modes of naive offline RL in latent action spaces. The
first is an optimization–execution mismatch. Because the realizable latent set is state-dependent and

transfer to decoded physical execution? The second is an objective mismatch. Because outcome-

not correspond to the optimal physical policy. This raises a separate question: when do the latent and
physical policy optima align?
To answer these questions, we analyze paired physical- and latent-action batch MDPs constructed
from the same observed transitions. For the first question, we show that batch-constrained latent
policy improvement transfers to physical execution under deterministic dynamics and supported
outcome identifiability. For the second, we show that latent and physical optima align under an
outcome-determined objective within the decoder-covered, batch-constrained physical policy class.
These results characterize when latent-space policy improvement remains valid and provide the
principles behind our method.
Building on these insights, we introduce LAPIS (Latent Action Policy Improvement on Support),
a latent policy improvement framework motivated by these failure modes. Rather than directly
optimizing a latent actor toward arbitrary high-value actions, LAPIS grounds value-based policy
improvement in observed state–latent transitions and uses a conditional flow policy to capture their
potentially multimodal structure. LAPIS is instantiated with a goal-conditioned objective based on
future states, which is naturally compatible with the outcome semantics captured by latent actions.
At inference, classifier-free guidance provides a simple way to adjust the emphasis between behavior
and high-value policy branches. With the same LAM and 5% action labels, LAPIS achieves the
highest success among latent-space methods on all five OGBench visual manipulation tasks and
outperforms prior semi-supervised offline RL baselines on nearly all tasks with only 5% or 1% action
labels. Further experiments examine the contributions of these design choices.

Latent action models from action-free video
A growing body of work learns controllable represen-
tations from action-free video by inferring latent actions that explain observed transitions (Edwards

Recent approaches learn such representations using discrete or continuous latent codes and extend
latent action modeling to richer video and vision-language-action settings (Schmidt & Jiang, 2024;
Ye et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Their
control pipelines predominantly train latent policies by behavioral cloning and use a small labeled
set to decode latent actions into physical controls. LAPO (Schmidt & Jiang, 2024) additionally
incorporates reinforcement learning by applying online PPO to fine-tune the physical action policy
after latent-action pretraining. In contrast, we study policy improvement directly in the learned latent
action space using offline data, with scarce action labels used for physical action decoding.
Action-free and semi-supervised reinforcement learning.
A related line of work studies learning
control from trajectories with missing or limited action annotations, using inferred actions, latent
representations, or other forms of auxiliary supervision to exploit action-free experience (Torabi et al.,
2018; Baker et al., 2022; Ghosh et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). Most closely
related is LAQ (Chang et al., 2022), which performs Q-learning over latent actions from state-only
data but relies on a hand-specified controller for execution. In contrast, we learn a latent action
space from action-free data together with a physical-action decoder from scarce action labels, and
study offline policy improvement directly in this learned latent action space. This setting makes the
relationship between latent-space optimization and decoded physical execution central, motivating
our analysis of when latent policy improvement transfers to physical control. Further related work is
discussed in Appendix A.

LATENT ACTION STRUCTURE AND FAILURES
A latent policy selects a latent action representing a desired transition outcome, which must ultimately
be realized through physical control at deployment. Section 4.1 characterizes the realizability and
abstraction structure of learned latent actions. Section 4.2 then shows how realizability can break the
connection between latent-space optimization and physical execution, while outcome abstraction can
misalign latent and physical objectives.
4.1

The encoder z = I(s, s′) labels the transition outcome rather than

Optimization–execution mismatch
State-conditioned realizability and a non-convex realizable set
imply that physically realizable latent actions depend on the current state, while interpolations between
realizable latents can be unrealizable. As a result, directly optimizing the actor to maximize learned
value can move it toward unrealizable latents despite behavior regularization, while a unimodal
actor can place probability between separated realizable modes and generate unrealizable latents.

physical execution can reach a different successor than the outcome represented by z. Consequently,

5.1
WHEN DOES LATENT POLICY IMPROVEMENT TRANSFER?
We first ask when an improvement measured in the latent batch MDP is preserved by decoded
physical execution. To avoid selecting unrealizable latent actions, we restrict the latent policy to
state–latent transitions observed in the batch.
Definition 2 (Batch-constrained policy). A policy is batch-constrained if its trajectory uses only
observed state–action pairs in Ba. A latent batch-constrained policy is defined analogously over Bz.
Every observed state–latent pair (s, z) ∈Bz has a physical realization by construction (δ(s, z) = 0 in
Definition 1), and the batch-exact decoder Ψ∗selects physical actions observed at s that realize this
outcome. A batch-constrained latent policy selects only latent actions that are physically realizable at
the current state.
Support alone, however, does not ensure exact transfer. Under stochastic dynamics, the same decoded
action can reach a different successor from the one used to evaluate z in the latent batch MDP,
breaking the correspondence between latent evaluation and physical execution. We therefore assume
deterministic ground dynamics, which leads to Theorem 1.
Assumption 1 (Deterministic ground dynamics). The successor is a deterministic function of the
current state and physical action, s′ = T(s, a).

WHEN DO LATENT AND PHYSICAL OPTIMA ALIGN?
Even when latent policy improvement transfers to decoded execution as in Theorem 1, this alone does
not guarantee alignment between the optimal latent and physical-action policies. Under a generic
action-dependent reward, outcome-based abstraction can collapse reward-distinct physical controls
into the same latent action, removing potentially necessary distinctions for physical optimality.
Assumption 2 (Outcome-determined reward). The reward depends on a transition only through the
current and next states, r(s, a, s′) = r(s, s′).
Under Assumption 2, physical actions reaching the same successor from the same state receive the

from the observed state–latent pairs and a conditional flow policy to capture their potentially mul-

obtain a reward-label-free objective compatible with outcome-based latent actions. Appendix D
relates the practical algorithm to the supported-improvement analysis and explains how the learned
components instantiate its principles.

7.3
EFFECT OF SUPPORTED POLICY IMPROVEMENT (Q3)
We examine whether successful latent policy improvement remains close to the behavior-supported
latent region. Starting from behavior-like policies, we strengthen critic-driven improvement by
decreasing CRL’s BC coefficient α and increasing LAPIS guidance w. Figure 3 measures dBC, the
distance from a policy latent to the nearest latent sampled from the BC policy at the same state–goal
pair, and dround, the round-trip latent error obtained by decoding a selected latent into physical

whereas LAPIS remains closer to the behavior-supported region.

CONCLUSION
We study offline policy improvement in latent action spaces learned from action-free data. We show
that state-conditioned realizability, non-convex realizable sets, and outcome-based abstraction can
make standard offline RL unreliable, and characterize when latent improvement transfers to physical

execution. In goal-conditioned RL, LAPIS performs support-aware policy improvement through
critic-guided filtering over observed state–latent transitions and a multimodal flow policy. With only

outperforms baselines on most tasks, especially in manipulation.

A
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
A.1
OFFLINE RL IN LEARNED LATENT OR SKILL SPACES
Prior work has used learned latent or skill representations to constrain policy optimization to behavior-
like controls. PLAS (Zhou et al., 2020) learns a continuous latent action space with a conditional VAE
and performs offline policy optimization through a behavior-trained decoder. Skill-based methods
such as OPAL (Ajay et al., 2021), SPiRL (Pertsch et al., 2020), and latent planning approaches (Lynch
et al., 2020) instead learn temporally extended action representations and optimize high-level policies
over them. These approaches typically rely on action-labeled experience to learn the latent control
representation or its low-level execution model. In contrast, we study offline policy improvement in a
latent action space learned primarily from action-free trajectories, using scarce action labels only to
ground latent decisions in physical control.
A.2
ACTION-FREE AND SEMI-SUPERVISED REINFORCEMENT LEARNING
Learning control from trajectories with missing actions has been studied through imitation from
observation (Torabi et al., 2018), action inference from video (Baker et al., 2022), latent-intention
learning (Ghosh et al., 2023), hierarchical goal-conditioned learning (Park et al., 2023), and semi-
supervised offline RL (Zheng et al., 2023). These approaches exploit action-free experience through
inferred actions, state or goal abstractions, or value learning without complete action supervision.
Our setting differs in that policy improvement is performed directly in a learned latent action space
and physical actions are needed only for decoding. This makes the relationship between latent-space
optimization and physical execution a central part of the problem.
A.3
OFFLINE GOAL-CONDITIONED AND CONTRASTIVE RL
Offline goal-conditioned RL learns multi-goal policies from fixed datasets without additional envi-
ronment interaction. Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) formulates goal-conditioned control
through density-ratio estimation of future-state occupancy, while subsequent work develops related
contrastive objectives for offline control (Zheng et al., 2024). LAPIS adopts this goal-conditioned
formulation in the learned latent-action space, using future states as goals and a contrastive critic to
estimate which observed state–latent transitions are useful for reaching them.
Recent work has also explored expressive generative policies and guidance-based policy extraction
(Lipman et al., 2023; Ho & Salimans, 2022; Frans et al., 2025). Most closely related, CFGRL (Frans
et al., 2025) derives classifier-free guidance as a controllable policy-improvement operator; in its value-
free goal-conditioned formulation, guidance combines unconditional and goal-conditioned generative
policy branches without an explicit value function. LAPIS shares guided flow-based extraction,
but differs in where policy improvement is introduced. LAPIS first estimates goal-conditioned
future-state occupancy with a TD-InfoNCE critic and filters only observed state–latent transitions
to construct a high-value distribution whose support remains within the observed conditional latent
support. The flow policy then models this support-aware target, and classifier-free guidance is applied
only at inference.
This distinction is particularly important in learned latent-action spaces. Unlike physical actions, a
latent action is a learned transition label whose usefulness depends on state-conditioned realizability
and on whether decoding faithfully produces the represented outcome. Thus, improving a generative
policy over latents does not by itself imply improved physical execution. Our transfer analysis
characterizes when latent-space improvement survives decoding, while the direct comparison in
Appendix E.5 shows that increasing goal guidance can improve physical-action CFGRL but does not
provide consistent improvement when the same construction is applied directly in the learned latent
space.
A.4
RELATION TO BATCH-CONSTRAINED RL AND MDP ABSTRACTION
Our theoretical analysis is related to classical results in batch-constrained reinforcement learning
and MDP abstraction, but addresses a different cross-representation question. Batch-constrained RL
studies policy evaluation and improvement when the policy is restricted to actions represented in a
14


Preprint
fixed offline dataset, thereby avoiding extrapolation to unsupported physical actions (Fujimoto et al.,
2019). In our setting, however, the policy acts in a learned action space: observed physical transitions
are relabeled by an inverse model as latent actions, and each latent decision must subsequently be
decoded back to physical control. Consequently, restricting the policy to observed latent actions is
not sufficient by itself to establish correspondence with physical execution. The latent support is
state-dependent, a supported pair (s, z) must identify the transition outcome represented by z, and
the decoder must realize that outcome through physical actions.
Theorem 1 characterizes this additional structure. We construct physical- and latent-action batch
MDPs from the same observed transitions and introduce supported outcome identifiability together
with a batch-exact decoder that links a supported latent decision to its physical realizations. Under de-
terministic dynamics, this construction yields exact equality between the return of a batch-constrained
latent policy and that of its decoded physical policy. Thus, the result extends the batch-constrained
perspective from policy optimization within a fixed action space to policy improvement through a
learned action representation whose semantics and physical execution must themselves be related.
Our optimality analysis is also connected to MDP homomorphism and abstraction theory, which
studies when an abstract decision process preserves the value structure of the underlying MDP
(Ravindran & Barto, 2003; Li et al., 2006). The latent actions considered here induce an outcome-
based action abstraction learned from observed transitions: distinct physical controls can map to
the same latent action whenever they produce the same represented outcome. This abstraction can
preserve execution while discarding distinctions that matter for reward. Theorem 2 identifies the
corresponding condition for optimality alignment: when reward is determined by the transition
outcome, an optimal latent policy decodes to an optimal policy within the decoder-covered, batch-
constrained physical policy class. The counterexample in Appendix C.3 shows that this condition is
substantive: latent-to-physical return transfer can hold exactly even when latent and physical optima
do not align.
Taken together, these results connect batch-constrained and abstraction-based reasoning to learned
latent actions, where the representation is inferred from action-free transitions rather than given a
priori. The additional questions are therefore not only whether a policy remains within dataset support
or whether an abstraction preserves an MDP, but whether a learned latent label identifies a physically
executable outcome and whether the distinctions removed by that representation are compatible with
the downstream objective.

THEORY–PRACTICE CORRESPONDENCE
The analysis in Section 5 isolates the conditions under which latent policy improvement remains
valid after physical execution. Practical LAPIS realizes these conditions through learned latent
representations, decoding, value estimation, and policy extraction. This appendix makes each
correspondence explicit and extends the exact analysis to the learned components used in practice.
Exact transfer arises as the zero-mismatch case of the transition and reward correspondence below;
supported reweighting defines the target distribution approximated by the learned policy.

D.2
STATE REPRESENTATIONS AND MARKOV CONTROL
The analysis in Section 5 uses Markov states to isolate the relationship between latent decisions
and physical execution from perceptual representation learning. The experiments use single-frame
RGB observations processed by learned visual encoders. Practical LAPIS applies the latent policy
improvement principle to this learned observation representation.
Different underlying states can produce indistinguishable observations, so the observation-level
process need not be exactly Markov. This concerns the state representation, whereas latent-action
realizability concerns whether a selected latent transition can be physically executed from the current
state. The exact results characterize the control-level correspondence under Markov state information.
Temporal or recurrent representations provide possible ways to retain additional state information;
the analysis applies when the resulting representation satisfies the stated Markov and transition
conditions.

D.6
REPRESENTATION HORIZON AND RECEDING-HORIZON CONTROL
The analysis in Section 5 is stated at the policy decision interval, where a latent action represents
the successor of a single transition. In visual latent-action learning, however, the temporal gap used
to construct the latent representation also controls how much transition information is visible to
the encoder. We distinguish three quantities: the LAM temporal gap H in zt = I(st, st+H); the
physical-action chunk size C, the number of actions predicted by a real-action policy or a latent-action
decoder; and the execution horizon E, the number of actions executed before replanning. The symbol
H refers only to the LAM temporal gap and does not apply to real-action policies without a LAM.
Our default LAM uses a representation horizon of H = 3,
zt = I(st, st+3),
but LAPIS remains a one-step receding-horizon controller: the decoder predicts a chunk of C = 3
actions, only the first action is executed (E = 1), and the policy replans from st+1. The critic
likewise propagates value at this one-step interval using st+1 and the recorded continuation latent
zt+1 = I(st+1, st+4). Thus, the longer temporal gap changes the representation used for control,
rather than requiring longer open-loop execution.
Representation and execution horizons.
We empirically separate these two roles by varying the
representation horizon and the number of decoded actions executed before replanning. Both H = 1
and our default H = 3 controller execute a single action before replanning. With this execution

across all three representative domains. Conversely, keeping the H = 3 representation but executing
the full three-action chunk does not account for the benefit of the longer horizon: one-step replanning
achieves higher mean performance in all three domains, with a particularly large difference on

applied to physical actions, most clearly on AntMaze, but provides no consistent improvement
when the same construction is applied directly to learned latent actions. In contrast, LAPIS obtains
large gains already at w = 1, before extrapolative guidance, with its high-value branch trained
toward the critic-filtered supported target qG rather than the unfiltered goal-conditioned behavior
distribution. Together with the component ablation in Table 4(a), this provides empirical evidence
that support-aware target construction, rather than guidance alone, is consequential for latent policy
improvement.

CFGRL in physical and learned latent action spaces.
Because LAPIS adopts classifier-free
guided policy extraction, we additionally compare with the value-free goal-conditioned formulation
of CFGRL (Frans et al., 2025). In this setting, standard flow-based GCBC corresponds to w = 1,
while w > 1 strengthens goal guidance at inference without learning an explicit value function. We
apply the same CFGRL construction both to physical actions and directly to learned latent actions,
and sweep w ∈{0, 1, 3}. In the latent-action setting, CFGRL uses the same latent action model and
decoder as LAPIS. Table 15 reports checkpoint-averaged success rates.
Increasing guidance improves physical-action CFGRL most clearly on AntMaze, where success
rises from 7.2 at w = 0 to 24.1 at w = 3. In contrast, directly applying the same goal-conditioned
guidance mechanism in the learned latent space produces no consistent improvement across the three
domains: performance remains nearly unchanged, or decreases, as w increases. Thus, the gains
associated with classifier-free guidance in an executable physical action space do not automatically
transfer when the guided variable is a learned latent action.
This difference is consistent with the cross-representation issue studied in Section 5. CFGRL’s
goal-conditioned construction treats the generated variable as the action whose future-goal occupancy
defines the improvement signal. For a physical action, this action is executed directly. A learned latent
action, however, is an outcome label inferred from an observed transition and must subsequently
be decoded into physical control. Improving a distribution over latent actions therefore does not by
itself guarantee improvement after decoding: the selected latent must be appropriate for the current
state and its represented transition must be faithfully realized by the decoder. These are precisely
39


Preprint
the additional correspondences captured by supported outcome identifiability and latent-to-physical
execution in Theorem 1.
LAPIS addresses this issue before guided inference. Rather than using guidance itself to construct
an improved latent policy, LAPIS first scores only observed state–latent transitions and forms the
critic-filtered distribution qG(z | s, g), whose support is contained in the observed conditional
distribution qB(z | s, g). The high-value flow branch is then trained toward qG, and classifier-free
guidance is applied only after this support-aware target has been constructed. This distinction is
already visible without extrapolative guidance: at w = 1, latent CFGRL remains weak on AntMaze-
medium, Cube-single, and Scene, whereas the LAPIS high-value branch reaches 51%, 53%, and
25%, respectively (Table 14). Thus, the main difference is not the use of classifier-free guidance itself,
but the construction of the latent policy-improvement target on observed state–latent transitions.

• DDPG+BC flow: a one-step policy is trained to maximize the critic while being distilled
from a flow behavior policy, as in FQL (Park et al., 2025b).

EXECUTION FAILURE OF GENERATED LATENTS
Protocol.
We measure whether latents generated by a policy are executed faithfully, on
cube-single and scene with four seeds, at every decision step of the evaluation episodes
(five tasks and 20 episodes per task). At each decision step we save the full simulator state, decode
the selected latent into its three-action chunk, execute the chunk open loop, re-encode the start obser-
vation and the observation three steps later, and record the round-trip error ρ = ∥I(ot, ˜ot+3) −z∥2 in
the standardized quantized latent coordinates used for dround in Figure 3. We then restore the saved
state and continue the episode; for every run we checked that restoring reproduces the simulator state
exactly and that probing does not change the episode.
Reference and failure rate.
For each task and LAM, we set the simulator to 2,000 dataset states
and execute the decoded chunk of the recorded dataset latent z = I(st, st+3), which gives a reference
distribution of ρ. A decision step fails when ρ exceeds the 95th percentile of this reference distribution,
so dataset latents fail 5% of the time by construction. The threshold is computed for the LAM and
decoder that each policy uses.

ADDITIONAL DISCUSSION OF EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
This appendix provides additional context for several experimental comparisons whose interpretation
involves differences in policy-improvement mechanisms, supervision protocols, decoder training,
or environment dynamics. These details are complementary to the implementation specifications in
Appendix E and the theory–practice correspondence in Appendix D.
F.1
ANTMAZE PERFORMANCE AND CONSERVATIVE EXTRACTION
Table 1 shows a substantial gap between fully supervised real-action CRL and real-action LAPIS on
AntMaze, particularly on the larger mazes. These results highlight a tradeoff of the support-aware
update used by LAPIS under full physical-action supervision.
LAPIS is motivated by a failure specific to learned latent-action spaces: direct value maximization
can select latent actions whose represented transitions are not physically realizable after decoding.
The latent realizability failure underlying this mismatch is absent when the policy directly outputs
physical actions, although ordinary offline-RL distribution shift remains. In this setting, restricting
policy improvement toward behavior-supported transitions can be unnecessarily conservative, while
more aggressive critic-driven optimization may be beneficial. Conversely, in the learned latent-action
setting, direct maximization can move the policy toward unrealizable latents, motivating the supported
update used by LAPIS.
The larger gap on more difficult AntMaze tasks is therefore consistent with a cost of conservative
policy extraction in long-horizon navigation. Accordingly, the fully supervised results provide
physical-action references for the limited-supervision comparisons. Our main question is whether
effective policy improvement can be retained after replacing physical actions with learned latent
actions under limited action supervision.
F.2
PROTOCOL DIFFERENCES ACROSS BASELINE TABLES
The GCIQL entries in Table 1 and the GCIVL entries in Table 3 (labeled GCIQL in the original
submission) differ in their value-learning and policy-extraction objectives. Table 1 uses an action-
conditioned critic and a state–goal value function, with policy extraction based on the Q −V
advantage. This formulation is applied to physical actions at the stated label budgets and to learned
latent actions in the LAM setting.
Table 3 uses an action-scarce adaptation that learns a state–goal value function from all observed
transitions, without requiring action labels for value learning. Its physical-action policy is trained only
on labeled transitions, weighted by the value difference between successive states. The two tables
therefore use different methods rather than repeated evaluations of the same procedure. Because the
Table 3 variant learns no action-conditioned Q, it corresponds to GCIVL in OGBench (Park et al.,
2025a), and we name it accordingly. GCIVL in Tables 1 and 21 is the standard OGBench GCIVL,
trained only on transitions with (physical or latent) actions; the Table 3 variant also learns its value
from action-free transitions.
F.3
INTERPRETING DECODER LABEL-BUDGET TRENDS
Table 5 isolates decoder supervision from latent representation learning: all label budgets use the same
frozen LAM trained with 5% action labels, while only the amount of action supervision available to
the decoder is varied. Thus, the non-monotonic performance of the state-marginal decoder across
1%, 5%, and 100% decoder labels is not caused by changes in the learned latent representation.
The state-marginal decoder Ψ(a | z) aggregates physical realizations across states without observing
the current state. If the same latent code admits different appropriate controls at different states,
broader coverage can make the empirical conditional p(a | z) more heterogeneous, partially offsetting
the benefit of additional labeled examples. By contrast, the state-conditioned decoder Ψ(a | s, z) can
use the current state to resolve such variation when sufficient supervision is available.
This provides a plausible interpretation of the pattern in Table 5: the marginal decoder performs
better under 1% and 5% supervision, where sharing action information across states can be beneficial,
while the state-conditioned decoder performs better with full supervision. The fact that the marginal

Sec. 6.2) reports the related observation that a decoder with access only to the latent action plateaus

F.5
STANDALONE F-GCBC AND THE LAPIS BEHAVIOR BRANCH
Table 1 reports F-GCBC as a standalone flow-based goal-conditioned behavioral-cloning baseline,
whereas w = 0 in Table 14 evaluates the behavior-conditioned branch of a jointly trained LAPIS
actor. Although both are trained from observed latent transitions using flow matching, they are not
the same trained policy. LAPIS uses a single conditional velocity model vθ(·; o) whose o = 0 branch
is trained toward the observed distribution qB and whose o = 1 branch is simultaneously trained
toward the critic-filtered distribution qG through Equation (5). The two losses therefore update shared
model parameters.
Consequently, setting w = 0 at inference selects the behavior-conditioned velocity field of the jointly
trained two-branch model. Table 14 is an inference-time guidance ablation within a fixed LAPIS actor,
whereas Table 1 evaluates a separately trained F-GCBC policy. Their different training objectives
and parameter sharing explain the difference between their scores.

FUTURE WORK AND LIMITATIONS
G.1
FUTURE WORK
A promising direction is to extend LAPIS from goal-conditioned to language-conditioned control.
Prior work has shown that language instructions can be grounded into visual goal representations
and executed through goal-conditioned policies (Myers et al., 2023; Cachet et al., 2024). This
suggests that LAPIS could retain its latent-space policy-improvement framework while replacing
explicit visual goals with language-derived goal representations, enabling policy improvement for
instruction-following and vision-language-action settings. Extending the framework and its transfer
analysis to partially observed settings using temporal or recurrent state representations is another
important direction.
G.2
LIMITATIONS
Our experiments are limited to simulated visual manipulation and navigation benchmarks, and we
focus on goal-conditioned control with limited physical-action supervision. Consequently, we do
not empirically study objectives with action-dependent rewards, where outcome-based abstraction
can create a mismatch between latent and physical policy objectives. Real-robot deployment and
language-conditioned control also remain outside the scope of the current evaluation.
Our exact transfer and alignment results rely on deterministic dynamics, supported outcome identifia-
bility, batch-constrained execution through the batch-exact decoder, and Markov state information.
Practical LAPIS uses learned visual state representations, a learned state-marginal decoder, an H = 3
latent representation with one-step replanning, and classifier-free guidance, so these conditions are
not instantiated exactly. We explicitly study these departures in Appendix D and through targeted
empirical analyses. The approximate-transfer analysis accounts for transition and decoder mismatch,
while decoder-conditioning, temporal-horizon, and first-step execution analyses examine learned
decoding and the H > 1 receding-horizon controller. For guided inference, the w = 1 high-value
branch provides the supported target underlying our analysis, and the guidance sweep shows that it
already captures most of the improvement on the manipulation tasks; w > 1 provides an additional
inference-time extrapolation beyond this exact support guarantee. The direct CFGRL comparison
further shows that guidance alone does not provide consistent improvement when applied directly in
the learned latent space, reinforcing the role of support-aware target construction in practical LAPIS.
