Abstract
While quantization compresses LLMs and accelerates inference, outlier activations
impede efficient low-bit representation. In particular, massive outliers cause sub-
stantial performance degradation. Although rotation-based methods that rely on
randomized Hadamard transforms for online inference aim to mitigate this, they can
produce fragmented distributions with empty bins when a massive outlier dominates
a token’s activation. This fragmentation severely undermines uniform quantization
performance. Moreover, massive outliers concentrate in the down-projection input,
where the preceding nonlinearity requires the rotation to be applied at inference
time. This constraint prevents training-based approaches such as SpinQuant from
learning the online rotation at this position, as a learned dense matrix cannot use the
Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform and would require a full matrix multiplication
during inference. To address this, we propose SignRot (Sign-adjusted Rotation),
which identifies massive outlier channels and constructs a sign adjustment vector
that unifies the signs of the corresponding Hadamard row, collapsing the bimodal
distribution into a unimodal profile amenable to quantization. Since the aligned
outlier shifts the activation mean away from zero, we pair SignRot with asymmetric
activation quantization, whose zero-point absorbs this shift. As a lightweight,
drop-in improvement compatible with any Hadamard-based quantization method,
SignRot requires only a single calibration forward pass and adds only element-wise
sign flips at inference, without an additional matrix multiplication. Applying Sign-
Rot to QuaRot achieves a WikiText2 perplexity of 5.88 and an average zero-shot
reasoning accuracy of 72.73, surpassing SpinQuant on the LLaMA3-70B model.
Code is available at https://github.com/HYU-ARK-Lab/SignRot.

Introduction
While large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable performance across diverse
domains [32, 6, 25], their escalating scale presents substantial challenges in computational
and memory requirements.
Quantization has emerged as a promising efficiency technique
due to its straightforward implementation and ability to maintain reliable performance un-
der significant compression [17, 1, 19].
In particular, post-training quantization (PTQ) [11]
has become a de facto standard, as it requires only a minimal calibration dataset with-
out extensive retraining, making it highly practical for large-scale deployment.
PTQ has
been applied to quantize weights [11, 4], activations [38, 1], and the KV cache [12].

While PTQ methods are promising, the presence
of large-magnitude outliers in activations, called
massive outliers, remains a critical bottleneck, as
they disproportionately distort the quantization
process [38, 31, 1, 17]. To mitigate this issue,
rotation-based PTQ methods, which transform
the activation space to suppress outliers before
quantization, have been explored [1, 19, 17].
These rotation matrices can generally be fused
into adjacent weights offline, incurring no additional inference cost. However, for layers following
nonlinear activations, such as FFN down-projection layers, the rotation cannot be fused into adjacent
weights and thus typically requires online application during inference. To mitigate the resulting
computational overhead, prior work has explored the use of randomized Hadamard matrices, which
admit efficient implementation through the Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform (FWHT) [1].
However, we find that when a massive outlier dominates a token’s input activation, applying random-
ized Hadamard transforms produces a fragmented distribution with distinct positive and negative
clusters, as shown in Figure 1, which directly degrades quantization performance. This is because
the empty regions between these clusters inflate the activation quantization range and thus the step
size. This is particularly critical at the FFN down-projection, where massive outliers are known to
concentrate [30], and where existing rotation-based PTQ methods are constrained to use randomized
Hadamard transforms for efficiency.
To address this, we propose a simple yet effective activation-aware sign adjustment to the Hadamard
transform, named Sign-adjusted Rotation (SignRot). By applying column-wise sign flips that unify
the signs of the Hadamard row corresponding to the identified massive outlier channel, the fragmented
distribution is restored to a bell-shaped profile amenable to quantization. Because the aligned outlier
shifts the rotated activation’s mean away from zero, SignRot pairs this adjustment with asymmetric
activation quantization, whose zero-point absorbs the shift.
SignRot is a lightweight, drop-in improvement compatible with any Hadamard-based quantization
method, including QuaRot [1], SpinQuant [19], and DartQuant [29], and is applicable to both
dense and MoE architectures. Since massive outlier channels are fixed across inputs [30], the sign
adjustment vector needs only to be computed once via a single calibration forward pass; at inference,
SignRot adds only an element-wise sign flip to the rotated activation, with no additional matrix
multiplication. Furthermore, SignRot quantizes a 7B model in approximately 3 minutes with Round-
to-Nearest (RTN), and around 15 minutes with GPTQ [11] on a single GPU, requiring no rotation
training.
Our contribution is summarized as follows:
• We analytically characterize that randomized Hadamard transforms induce bimodal frag-
mentation in the presence of massive outliers, demonstrating that this fragmentation leads to
quantization degradation at the FFN down-projection.
• We propose SignRot, a simple yet effective activation-aware sign adjustment to the Hadamard
transform that directly addresses the fragmentation without requiring learned rotations.
SignRot is a lightweight, drop-in improvement compatible with any Hadamard-based quan-
tization method, requiring only a single calibration forward pass.
• SignRot improves quantization performance across diverse settings, including both dense and
MoE architectures. On LLaMA3-70B with GPTQ, SignRot reduces the WikiText2 perplexity
of QuaRot [1] from 6.16 to 5.88 under the same asymmetric activation quantization, and
applied on top of SpinQuant [19], which relies on learned rotations, it further reduces
perplexity from 6.38 to 5.45 and improves average zero-shot accuracy from 71.15 to 73.52.

Channel outlier and scaling methods
Previous studies [8, 38, 28] have focused on handling
channel outliers that appear in specific feature channels across entire token sequences. These methods
primarily leverage scale invariance, which utilizes the fact that a linear layer Y = XW remains
mathematically equivalent when activations are scaled by a diagonal matrix S and weights are
inversely scaled: Y = (XS)(S−1W).
While LLM.int8 [8] initially managed these outliers through mixed-precision decomposition, its
implementation proved hardware-unfriendly. To address this, SmoothQuant [38] introduced a
technique to migrate the quantization difficulty of channel outliers from activations to weights
via channel-wise scaling. This direction was further refined by OmniQuant [28], which improved
quantization performance by learning optimal channel-wise scaling factors.
However, scaling methods that only adjust the per-channel magnitude cannot effectively handle a
newly discovered type of outlier [30, 18] called the massive outlier. These outliers concentrate in
specific tokens and channels, particularly in FFN layers, and are known to be significant in quantizing
activations to 4 bits or less [17].
Massive outlier and rotation methods
To mitigate the effects of massive outliers, recent studies [1,
19, 29] leverage transformation invariance. Unlike scaling, which only performs element-wise
adjustment, rotation-based methods use an orthogonal transformation R to redistribute the outlier
energy across all available channels: Y = (XR)(R−1W). The applicability and optimization of
these rotations are fundamentally governed by their position within the Transformer [35] block.
When a rotation follows a linear operation, it can be fused directly into adjacent weights during the
PTQ phase, incurring no additional inference cost. This offline mergeability allows for intensive
optimization through training to maximize accuracy. For instance, SpinQuant [19] optimizes the
rotation matrix using CayleySGD [15]. DartQuant [29] further improves the efficiency of rotation
matrix optimization by constraining the post-rotation activation distribution, which reduces both
computational cost and overfitting risk.
In contrast, rotations that follow nonlinear operations, such as the FFN down-projection input (i.e., Td
in Figure 2, where a LLaMA-style architecture is used as a representative example), cannot be
fused and must be applied during inference. To maintain inference efficiency at these positions,
3


existing methods are constrained to use the Randomized Hadamard Transform (RHT) to leverage
the Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform (FWHT) [10]. Various methodologies have been employed to
implement this framework. QuaRot [1] serves as a foundation for training-free rotation, effectively
removing outliers using RHT, while DFRot [37] refines the rotation using a small calibration dataset
to minimize quantization errors in a near-closed-form manner.
While these training-free methods offer fast setup using a minimal calibration step, they remain
agnostic to the specific polarity of massive outliers. As we will analyze in Section 3, this disregard
for outlier polarity induces a systematic bimodal fragmentation when using RHT at the FFN down-
projection. This serves as a critical source of quantization degradation that cannot be resolved without
considering the signs of the outliers.

Analysis of Bimodal Fragmentation in RHT under Massive Outliers
The randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) combines independent random signs with the uniform
structure of the Hadamard matrix. In the absence of dominant outliers, its output can have a bell-
shaped distribution favorable for quantization [34]. The Gaussian approximation used here requires
the conditions discussed below. The Gaussian approximation under RHT relies on the Central Limit
Theorem (CLT). Independent random signs yield zero-mean summands. In this setting, the Lindeberg
condition requires that no single summand contribute a non-negligible fraction of the total variance
in the limit [2]. This assumption can fail when one channel retains a non-vanishing fraction of the
activation energy; that channel then contributes an independent two-valued term, so the limit is not
Gaussian.

Asymmetric–Symmetric matrix multiplication
SignRot pairs sign adjustment with asymmetric
activation quantization to represent the resulting shifted distribution. Sign adjustment changes the
signs of the Hadamard outputs while preserving their absolute values; it therefore leaves the max-
absolute scale of symmetric quantization unchanged. Asymmetric quantization uses a zero-point to
accommodate the shift and can allocate quantization levels to the shifted range. Asymmetric activation
quantization is therefore an integral part of SignRot rather than an optional refinement. To maintain
the efficiency of activation quantization, we use Asymmetric–Symmetric matrix multiplication
(Asym–Symm matmul, where activations are quantized asymmetrically and weights symmetrically)
defined as follows:
Ydeq = ∆X(XqWq −ZXWq)∆W
(9)
where Ydeq denotes the dequantized output, ∆X and ∆W are diagonal matrices of per-token
activation scales and per-output-channel weight scales, respectively, and ZX contains the activation
zero-points. The first term in Equation 9 is an integer dot product identical to Symmetric–Symmetric
matrix multiplication (Symm–Symm matmul, where both activations and weights are quantized
symmetrically). For per-token activation quantization, ZX = zX1⊤, where zX is a column vector
of token zero-points and 1 ∈RCin is the all-ones vector. The second term therefore factorizes as
zX(1⊤Wq). The weight column sum 1⊤Wq, with Wq ∈ZCin×Cout, is input-independent and
precomputed offline. During inference, Asym–Symm matmul computes the rank-1 outer product with
zX and subtracts it from the integer matrix product. This correction costs O(T Cout), compared with
O(T CinCout) for the matrix multiplication. Asymmetric quantization also estimates a per-token
zero-point alongside the scale required by symmetric quantization.

5.1
Experimental setup
Models and datasets
We apply SignRot on the LLaMA2 (7B, 13B, 70B) [33] and LLaMA3 (8B,
70B) [9] model families, and additionally on Mistral-7B-v0.3 [13] and Qwen3-30B-A3B [39]. To
assess our method, we evaluate quantized models in language modeling and reasoning tasks. We
report perplexity on WikiText2 [20] and C4 [24] datasets to evaluate language modeling. Further-
more, we measure the accuracy on six zero-shot reasoning tasks: ARC (Easy and Challenge) [5],
HellaSwag [41], LAMBADA(OpenAI) [23], PIQA [3], and WinoGrande [26].
Baselines
We evaluate SignRot by applying it on top of three rotation-based methods that employ
online Hadamard transforms: QuaRot [1], SpinQuant [19], and DartQuant [29]. QuaRot applies
randomized Hadamard transforms without any training, while SpinQuant and DartQuant further
optimize the rotation matrices to minimize quantization error.

Implementation details
We implemented SignRot using HuggingFace Transformers [36] and
PyTorch [22]. On a single A100 GPU, SignRot only adds 5 minutes of quantization time for the
LLaMA2-70B model, which is negligible compared to the total quantization time. The peak memory
usage during quantize LLaMA2-70B is max 7 GB for the rotation process and max 40 GB for the
GPTQ stage. We use CUTLASS [21] for 4-bit matrix multiplication and FlashInfer [40] for KV cache
quantization to evaluate the inference performance of our fully quantized model. Total quantization
time is measured on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, while inference speedup and memory usage are
measured on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
Quantization configurations
We report results using both Round-to-Nearest (RTN) and GPTQ [11]
for weight quantization; our GPTQ implementation uses 128 calibration samples randomly selected
from the WikiText-2 dataset. To address the memory-intensive nature of self-attention, we apply
group-wise asymmetric quantization to the KV cache with a group size of the head dimension,
following [1]. For each baseline, +SignRot uses the same quantization configuration as the baseline,
except that +SignRot applies per-token asymmetric activation quantization and its clipping ratios
are selected by hyperparameter tuning. QuaRot uses per-token symmetric activation quantization
following its official setting [1], whereas SpinQuant and DartQuant already use asymmetric activation
quantization. Appendix E gives the detailed quantization configuration of each baseline and of
SignRot. Sign adjustment vectors are computed using the first sample of the GPTQ calibration dataset
during the GPTQ calibration phase. For RTN, we compute the sign adjustment vector using a single
sample randomly extracted from the WikiText-2 dataset. We confirm that the choice of calibration
data has minimal impact on performance in Table 5.
5.2
Main results
Results on LLaMA families.
We report the main results in Table 1, where SignRot is applied
on top of three rotation-based baselines, respectively, on LLaMA2 (7B/13B/70B) and LLaMA3
(8B/70B) under both RTN and GPTQ weight quantization. We evaluate language modeling perplexity
on WikiText2 and C4, and zero-shot reasoning task accuracy.
For language modeling tasks, QuaRot+SignRot generally reduces perplexity over the QuaRot baseline.
The improvement is especially pronounced for LLaMA3-70B, where Wiki2 perplexity drops from
6.60 to 5.88 under GPTQ (from 6.16 to 5.88 under the same asymmetric activation quantization;
Table 4), surpassing SpinQuant [19] (6.38). For LLaMA2-13B with GPTQ, QuaRot+SignRot
achieves a Wiki2 PPL of 5.32, matching DartQuant [29] and outperforming SpinQuant (5.39). On
LLaMA3-8B, QuaRot+SignRot +GPTQ reduces PPL from 8.16 to 7.85, matching SpinQuant and

6
Conclusions
We demonstrate that the Hadamard transform, widely used in existing studies to manage activation
outliers through linear transformations, can create fragmented distributions when a massive outlier
dominates a token’s activation. Although such tokens are rare, the fragmentation they cause sub-
stantially increases the quantization error of the layer in which they first appear. To address this
fragmentation, we propose a massive outlier-aware sign-adjusted rotation method, named SignRot, a
computationally efficient method optimized for online transformation. SignRot identifies massive
outlier channels and calculates sign adjustment vectors using a single forward pass. Our approach
quantizes the LLaMA2-7B model in 3 minutes with RTN and 15 minutes with GPTQ. During
inference, SignRot adds only element-wise sign flips per token per decoder layer. On LLaMA3-70B,
QuaRot+SignRot achieves a WikiText2 perplexity of 5.88 and an average zero-shot reasoning accu-
racy of 72.73, surpassing SpinQuant. Our findings suggest that a simple, massive outlier-aware sign
adjustment can mitigate this fragmentation of online Hadamard rotation without iterative optimization.
Extending SignRot to tokens with several massive outlier channels and to layers whose dominant
massive outlier channel varies across tokens (e.g., the last layers in Appendix C), for instance by
assigning each channel group its own Hadamard transform and sign adjustment vector, is left for
future work.

To measure how much each layer
contributes, we quantize a single decoder layer while keeping all other layers in rotated FP16, and
measure the KL divergence of the final output from the FP16 model on WikiText-2 (8 sequences
of 2048 tokens, GPTQ W4A4). The KL of quantizing all layers is close to the sum of these single-
layer KLs (ratio 0.96, 1.07, and 0.93 for LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA2-13B, and LLaMA3-8B), so the
single-layer KL indicates how much each layer contributes. Figure 4 and Table 10 show that the
first massive outlier layer is among the three most expensive layers with QuaRot in LLaMA2-7B,
LLaMA2-13B, and LLaMA3-8B, at 2.3–3.6× the median of the layers without massive outliers.
SignRot removes the error of the first massive outlier layer.
As shown in Table 10, SignRot
reduces the single-layer KL of the first massive outlier layer by 36–61%, while the curves of the
two methods nearly coincide at layers without massive outliers. The reduction is not limited to the
massive outlier tokens themselves: the KL of all other tokens drops by 31–64%. Since SignRot
barely changes the KL of layers without massive outliers, this suggests that the error on the massive
outlier token, which serves as an attention sink, propagates to every other token through attention in
the subsequent layers. At the last layers, SignRot leaves the final KL unchanged, as there is no later
layer through which the error of the massive outlier token can reach other tokens. The gain carries
over to quantizing all layers. Averaged over random seeds (11 for LLaMA2-7B and LLaMA3-8B, 5
for LLaMA2-13B and Mistral-7B-v0.3), SignRot reduces the KL of the fully quantized model by
2.0–4.5% with GPTQ W4A4 and by 2.9–6.5% with W16A4 in every model, and enabling SignRot
only at the first massive outlier layer already yields 1.1–4.8% and 2.3–6.4%, respectively.

Single linear layer latency
To isolate the cost of SignRot, we benchmark the down-projection layer alone, where all of its
additional operations are concentrated. Table 19 reports the latency (ms) of Int4+QuaRot with and
without SignRot. We benchmark the down-projection layer sizes of LLaMA2-7B (11008×4096),
LLaMA2-13B (13824×5120), and LLaMA2-70B (28672×8192) at a sequence length of 2048,
averaged over 100 runs. The overhead decreases with larger models, from a maximum of 10.39% on
LLaMA2-7B (batch size 1) to a minimum of 4.12% on LLaMA2-70B (batch size 4), since the sign
adjustment cost becomes negligible compared to the dominant matrix multiplication. The overhead
also diminishes with larger batch sizes as computation becomes the primary bottleneck, and SignRot
still achieves a 2.99×–4.39× speedup over FP16 on this layer.
H
Limitations
Despite the significant performance improvements of SignRot, there are still some limitations.
First, it is unknown how to efficiently adjust the online transformation when there are no massive
outliers. Second, further research is needed to determine whether SignRot is effective when using
transformation matrices other than the Hadamard matrix. Third, SignRot folds only the channel
selected during calibration in each layer. The split induced by a second massive outlier channel
remains (Appendix D), and in some later layers massive outliers do not occur at a fixed channel: the
largest channel differs from token to token (e.g., in the last layers of Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Qwen3-30B-
A3B), so tokens whose largest channel is not the selected one are not folded. Lastly, the efficiency of
the Hadamard transformation decreases when the intermediate dimension is small, such as in MoE
architectures.
I
Broader impact
This paper presents SignRot, a computationally efficient method optimized for online transformation.
SignRot can adjust online transformations to manage massive outliers, which cannot be optimized
22


by learning-based methods. By enabling efficient quantization of large language models, SignRot
can reduce the computational resources required for training and inference, making advanced AI
technologies more accessible and environmentally sustainable. However, if misused for harmful
purposes, it could have a negative social impact.
