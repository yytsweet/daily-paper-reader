Abstract
Visuomotor policies condition action prediction on vi-
sual and proprioceptive observations.
While histori-
cal proprioceptive sequences carry rich dynamic pri-
ors, current methods treat them as equal to high-
dimensional vision, causing the proprioceptive signal
to be overwhelmed. To identify what is lost in equal-
conditioning fusion, we analyze proprioceptive data
and find that strong inter-joint coupling is the key
action-determining structure. This finding aligns with
the neural sensorimotor loop, in which proprioception
first forms a body-centered prior and vision is later cal-
ibrated via parietal remapping.
Inspired by this, we
propose ACiT, Proprioceptive-Prior Action Chunking
with Inverted Transformers, which reinforces histor-
ical joint-coupling as the primary condition with vi-
sion as a later calibration. This design is instantiated
through three modules: Inverted State Encoding builds
a proprioceptive prior via per-joint tokens and inter-
joint self-attention. State-Visual Grounding lets joint
tokens query visual evidence, making vision goal- and
calibration-only.
Inverted Action Chunking decodes
per-joint trajectories independently.
ACiT improves
success rates on most LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and
real-world tasks, with the largest gains on those that re-
quire dynamics and coordination. The same condition-
ing design transferred to the VLA backbone π0-ACiT
yields significant gains with better execution efficiency.
1 Introduction
Visuomotor policies predict actions from multi-view obser-
vations and proprioception.
Early work conditioned on a
single current state, where ACT maps the current joint state
and images to an action chunk [41]. Diffusion Policy con-
ditions on a short observation history instead [8]. A single
frame proved insufficient to resolve action ambiguity, while
history carries dynamics and hidden-state information, so
proprioceptive history was explicitly incorporated. Recent
models such as A2A and Chronos explicitly model proprio-
ceptive history [12, 42]. Another line discretizes historical
states or actions into tokens to better integrate with LLM
backbones, at the cost of losing continuous coupling struc-
ture [14, 22, 28]. Reconstruction-only tokenization can also
erode control-relevant information [16]. These works vali-
date the value of using history from both continuous and dis-
crete directions, and state history is becoming a consensus
for action prediction.
These advances have not converged at the conditioning in-
terface. First, mainstream methods map historical proprio-
ceptive states as an independent modality, either discretely
or continuously, and place them alongside a pooled global
visual vector as conditions for action generation. This naive
fusion lets high-dimensional, low-SNR visual features en-
tangle and dominate the conditioning, leading to degraded
robustness or spurious shortcuts [15, 38]. Second, we ana-
lyze the state history and the action itself: the effective di-
mensionality of the action distribution is far below the num-
ber of joints, and joints are strongly coupled, as shown in
Fig. 1(a). However, existing methods model temporal depen-
dencies and pack all joints into one vector, so the inter-joint
coupling, a structure critical to action, can only be implicit
inside the token.
Therefore, how to encode historical state information, how
to fuse modalities, and how to feed the result into action de-
coding is an open problem. Inspired by neuroscience, the hu-
man chain from peripheral proprioception to body-centered
action generation is as follows (Fig. 1(b)): muscle and joint
receptors continuously encode joint angle, velocity, and ten-
sion and project through large-diameter myelinated affer-
ents [29, 36]. The somatosensory cortex and posterior pari-
etal cortex aggregate proprioceptive history into a body-state
representation, forming a body-centered prior [1, 2]. The
posterior parietal cortex then remaps visual goals from retinal
coordinates into body-centered coordinates, with propriocep-
tive context modulating this remapping [2, 10]. Finally, the
parietal-motor pathway sends the calibrated body-centered
representation to action generation [23, 37]. The key compu-
tational asymmetry is that proprioception provides a continu-
ous, low-latency body-state stream, whereas visual goals are
remapped and integrated into the body-centered representa-
tion afterward.

tion:
the fast, continuous proprioceptive stream forms a
prior over the body state, visual goals provide goal evidence
(a likelihood-like term), and calibrating the prior yields a
posterior-like body-centered representation for action gener-
ation. Based on this, we propose ACiT on top of ACT, the

view images, proprioception, and a chunked action decoder),
so that the encoder and the head stay fixed while only the
conditioning changes. It has three modules. First, Inverted
State Encoding compresses each joint’s entire history into
a token and directly models inter-joint coupling with self-
attention. Second, State-Visual Grounding treats vision as a
per-joint goal-calibration signal and does not let it directly
enter the encoder. Third, Inverted Action Chunking treats
each joint as a token on the action side, and each joint inde-
pendently generates its complete trajectory chunk. The de-
coder does not need to translate between “visual coordinates”
and “joint coordinates”.
The historical proprioceptive se-
quence and action generation share a more consistent feature
space. To separate the interface from the backbone, we port
the same conditioning to a pretrained VLA and obtain π0-
ACiT, which keeps π0’s architecture and changes only how
the state enters it. Experiments on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0,
and real-world manipulation tasks show that ACiT and π0-
ACiT improve success on most tasks, with improved robust-
ness under visual perturbations, especially on tasks that de-
pend on dynamics and joint coordination.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We formulate the conditioning interface as Bayesian
sensorimotor cue integration, with history as the prior
and vision as the calibration evidence, and realize it as
an explicit asymmetric fusion.
• We propose ACiT, which uses three inverted Trans-
former modules to explicitly build and preserve the
inter-joint coupling dynamics in the state history instead
of flattening them.
• We validate ACiT on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-
world tasks, where it improves the average success rate
by 5.7 to 10.8 points over the strongest baseline. π0-
ACiT improves the average success rate by 26.9 points
at 20.2% lower inference latency than π0.
2 Related Work
History in the conditioning interface. Early visuomotor
policies condition on a single frame: ACT maps the cur-
rent joint state and images to an action chunk [41], and Dif-
fusion Policy instead uses a short observation window [8].
Later work makes the state history explicit in the condi-
tioning input [12, 42].
A parallel line discretizes states
or actions so that autoregressive backbones can consume
them [14, 21, 22, 28], at the cost of losing continuous struc-
ture, and reconstruction-only tokenization can erode control-
relevant information [16]. Under both interfaces propriocep-
tion enters as one flat vector or a one-dimensional token se-
quence, so the coupling among joints stays implicit.
Vision and proprioception under fusion. Pooling both
modalities into one condition leaves nothing to stop the high-
dimensional visual stream from dominating and introducing
spurious shortcuts [15, 38]. One line responds by letting the
robot state steer vision, through robot-centric pooling [43]
or active visual attention [39], yet the asymmetry is applied

to perception rather than to the conditioning itself. Large
VLA backbones inherit the same symmetric interface, con-
catenating one proprioceptive token with visual and language
tokens [4, 5, 25].
Structure as an inductive bias. Topology-aware models
inject the kinematic graph as a structural bias for cross-
embodiment transfer [24, 27].
ACiT shares that motiva-
tion but acts on the conditioning interface: it carries his-
tory per joint, keeps the resulting prior primary, and lets each
joint retrieve its own visual calibration in a single generation
pipeline. Appendix D expands the comparison.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and Tasks. We evaluate on two simula-
tion benchmarks and a real-world dual-arm platform.
RoboTwin 2.0 [7] is a dual-arm manipulation benchmark
with 14-DoF joint states and actions and three cameras (a top
camera and two wrist cameras). We use six tasks: Lift Pot,
Pick Dual Bottles, Handover Block, Move Can Pot, Place
Dual Shoes and Shake Bottle, abbreviated as Lift, Pick Dual,
Handover, Move Can, Shoes and Shake. Each task has an
easy and a randomized hard split, the latter varying object
pose, color and background (Appendix A.1). LIBERO [18]
is a single-arm benchmark with 7-DoF actions, 8-DoF states,
and two cameras.
We evaluate on Spatial, Object, and
LIBERO-10. We exclude Goal, which requires language in-
structions.
Real-World Platform. We build a dual-arm platform from
two reBot Arm B601-DM arms [30]. Each arm has six DoF, a

4.4 Transfer to a Large VLA
We apply ACiT’s conditioning to the post-training of the
mainstream π0 model [4], which yields π0-ACiT. Both mod-
els are fine-tuned end-to-end per task, with the PaliGemma
VLM [3] and the flow-matching expert keeping their archi-
tecture and objective. Only π0’s single-frame proprioceptive
token is replaced by Gt from the Inverted State Encoding and
State-Visual Grounding of ACiT.

Simulation Benchmarks
Fig. 8 compares the two models on six RoboTwin tasks. The
gains land on the same group of tasks as the ACT-family re-
sults of Fig. 5: both backbones improve where one frame
cannot determine each joint’s action on its own (Pick Dual
Bottles, Handover Block, Place Dual Shoes) and stay flat

backbones that differ in size, visual prior and action head
agree on which tasks benefit, so the gain comes from the
conditioning interface rather than from a property of a back-

phase relation between joints, a relation that exists only in
the history. Randomization removes the same fraction for
both models, so the gain is not a tolerance to visual shift.

4.7 Efficiency
Table 3 reports inference cost and model size. ACiT adds
only 8.6 M parameters over ACT (92.6 M against 84.0 M)
and keeps its one-step sampler, at 0.48 ms per action, while
DP and FM pay ten integrator steps at a 3.2× larger model.
The same holds after transfer: π0-ACiT needs 4.73 ms per
action against 5.93 ms for π0, at the same ten steps and essen-
tially the same size (4.05 B against 4.03 B), so the reduction
comes from the interface, not from a cheaper sampler.
5 Conclusion
We presented ACiT, which treats historical joint coupling as
the primary condition and vision as a later calibration: each
joint carries its own history, retrieves its own visual evidence
and decodes its own trajectory. It improves most LIBERO,
RoboTwin 2.0 and real-world tasks, and transfers to a pre-
trained VLA at lower cost.
Limitations. ACiT is instantiated only with a continuous
action head, so it does not cover the discrete action tokens
that autoregressive VLAs rely on. Extending it there needs
demonstration data beyond what our dual-arm platform pro-
vides, which we leave to future work.

A Experimental Setup
A.1 Simulation Benchmarks
RoboTwin 2.0 is a bimanual platform with two 6-DoF arms
and parallel grippers, simulated for table-top manipulation.
We use six tasks, shown in Fig. 11. Lift Pot lifts a pot by
both handles and holds it; Pick Dual Bottles grasps and lifts
two bottles at once; Handover Block passes a block from one
arm to the other; Move Can Pot lays the can against the pot;
Place Dual Shoes places two shoes into a box; Shake Bottle
lifts a bottle and shakes it. Each task comes with an easy split
(demo_clean) and a randomized split (demo_randomized)
in which object poses, object colors and the scene back-
ground are randomized. All numbers are 100 episodes per
task, and the success criterion is the one shipped with the
benchmark, listed in Table 4.
LIBERO evaluates single-arm table-top manipulation with
language instructions.
We use three suites: LIBERO-10
(ten long-horizon tasks), LIBERO-Object (objects unseen in
training) and LIBERO-Spatial (spatial relations), with 100
episodes per suite.
A.2 Implementation Details
The ACT-family models share one protocol: 50,000 train-
ing steps, batch size 16, action chunk 50 with 50 executed
steps, seed 1000, and AdamW [20]. The visual backbone is a
ResNet-18 [9]. All models are implemented in PyTorch [26]
with LeRobot [6], and the simulator is MuJoCo [32]. For
run-to-run variation we follow the protocol of ACT [41],
which reports three training seeds on simulation and a sin-
gle trained policy on hardware, where every additional seed
needs a full physical re-evaluation. Table 2 of the main paper
reports the same sweep. ACiT uses a state history of H=8
frames. ACT conditions on the current state only; DP and
FM take H=2 frames and flatten them into the global condi-
tion, which is the setting their original papers use. DP and
FM execute 32 steps in the 50k_final runs and 10 in the
5task runs.
Architecturally, ACiT keeps the ACT transformer (d=512,
eight heads, FFN width 3200, four encoder and seven de-
coder layers, 32-d CVAE latent) and adds a two-layer state
encoder followed by a two-layer cross-attention block. The
π0 backbone is left intact; π0-ACiT adds a two-layer state en-
coder and a single grounding layer to it and keeps the flow-
matching action expert unchanged.
For the VLA transfer (Sec. 4.4), we initialize π0-ACiT
from pi0_base. The PaliGemma [3] backbone and the flow-
matching action expert keep their original architecture, and
π0’s single-frame proprioceptive token is replaced by the two
conditioning modules of ACiT. The Inverted State Encoding
encodes each joint’s eight-frame history with a 1D convolu-
tion and a linear projection into a joint token, and forms the
proprioceptive prior by inter-joint self-attention. State-Visual
Grounding then applies a joint-level cross-attention over the
visual tokens, producing Gt as the proprioceptive condition
of the action expert. The Inverted Action Chunking is not
transferred, and the flow-matching objective is unchanged,
so the action head keeps its architecture and capacity and

B Ablations, Sensitivity and Cost
B.1 Module Ablation
Tables 5 and 6 give the per-task rates behind Table 1. The
three removals do not fail in the same places. Equal fusion
costs the most on Handover Block and Place Dual Shoes, the
two tasks whose success turns on coupling between joints,
and it is almost free on Move Can Pot. Flat state costs a
nearly constant amount on every task, which is what one ex-
pects when the model loses the per-joint state representation
rather than a specific coupling. Flat decode is the opposite:
it loses on Pick Dual Bottles and Place Dual Shoes, where
each joint needs its own trajectory, and it gains on Handover
Block and Move Can Pot, where one shared trajectory keeps
the joints in phase.
B.2 Sensitivity to History Length
History length is a hyperparameter of the conditioning inter-
face rather than a module, so we report it separately. Tables 7
and 8 vary the number of proprioceptive frames and keep ev-
erything else fixed. The same pattern appears everywhere:
one frame is the weakest, four recovers most of the gap, eight
is best on RoboTwin and on the real platform and matches
four on LIBERO, and sixteen is slightly worse, which is what
one expects when extra frames add redundancy but no new
dynamics.

B.4 Seed Variance
Table 2 of the main paper repeats the training of ACiT and
ACT on RoboTwin with two further seeds. The evaluation
episodes are shared across runs, so the only source of vari-
ation is the training itself, and the spread across seeds stays
small relative to the gap between the two models.
C Analysis
C.1 Failure Cases
Figs. 12 and 13 also show how the methods fail once the
scene is shifted. The baselines fail at the grasp itself: under
a changed background, ACT and FM approach the cube with
an offset and close on empty space, and DP pushes it away
before closing; under a changed object color the same offset
appears, which is the behavior expected when a single frame
has to carry both the pose and the appearance. ACiT keeps
the grasp in the same frames and fails later, when placing
the object. Its remaining failures are therefore concentrated
in placement precision and in the phase of a handover, not
in grasp stability, which is consistent with Fig. 7: the gap to
the baselines is smallest on the two tasks whose success is
dominated by the grasp and largest under the dark shift.
Figs. 14–19 replay one episode of each task under all four
policies, with the checker outcome and the number of steps
used marked on the last frame; the success conditions are
listed in Table 4; every row of a figure shares the same ini-
tial state. On Place Dual Shoes success requires both shoes
inside the box with the target orientation and both grippers
open, which only ACiT satisfies. On Pick Dual Bottles ACiT
lifts the bottle within a fraction of the step budget while the
baselines never leave the table. On Lift Pot ACiT and DP
both succeed, with ACiT finishing in fewer steps, while ACT
and FM fail. On Move Can Pot only ACiT brings the can to
the pot. On Shake Bottle DP never lifts the bottle, and on
LIBERO-Spatial all four policies succeed with ACiT taking
the fewest steps.

D Extended Related Work
D.1 Proprioceptive History: Representation and Condi-
tioning
Action policies are moving from single-frame proprioceptive
conditions to explicit history modeling. In the continuous
route, ACT [41] maps the current joint state and visual ob-
servations to an action chunk with a CVAE. Diffusion Pol-
icy [8] conditions on the last H observations, proprioception
included, concatenated into one global vector before condi-
tional denoising; its own ablation reports that a vision-based
policy degrades as H grows, which suggests that a flattened
history does not scale. A2A [12] embeds historical proprio-
ceptive sequences into the latent space of action generation,
and Chronos [42] treats observation history as the latent state
of the policy. Meanwhile, another route discretizes historical

D.2 Topology-Aware Morphological Bias
Standard VLA policies are usually embodiment-agnostic and
must infer kinematic structure from data.
GET-Zero [27]
proposes a Graph Embodiment Transformer that uses em-
bodiment graph connectivity as a learned structural bias in
attention, enabling zero-shot adaptation to new joint config-
urations and link lengths. Human2LocoMan [24] further pro-
poses a Modularized Cross-embodiment Transformer (MXT)
that uses embodiment-specific tokenizers and detokenizers
on a shared Transformer trunk to handle heterogeneous bod-
ies. These works establish that joints are not independent
dimensions and that the kinematic graph should be an ex-
plicit inductive bias. However, existing topology methods
mainly target cross-embodiment transfer and action-interface
design. They do not treat continuous per-joint proprioceptive
history as a generative prior, nor let each joint retrieve its own
visual calibration signal. ACiT unifies continuous per-joint
history, kinematic topology, and per-joint visual grounding
in a single inverted generation pipeline.
