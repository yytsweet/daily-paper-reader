On the Utility of Pre-Trained Visual Representations for Policy
Learning of Contact-Rich Insertion Tasks
Leon Kim1, Dinesh Jayaraman1,†, Michael Posa1,†
Abstract— Visuomotor imitation learning has become a pre-
dominant paradigm for real-world robotic manipulation, yet
it remains data-inefficient and brittle to task-irrelevant visual
shifts. Pre-trained visual representations promise to ease this
burden by distilling priors from internet-scale data, but the
“right” representation for robotics remains unresolved: existing
studies span broad but sparse task distributions and reach
conclusions that vary and even conflict, pointing to strong task
and setting dependence. We examine this question by bringing
a magnifying glass to a common contact-rich manipulation
task: insertion. We compare representations from widely used
vision foundation models against an explicit contact-centric
representation on two closely related insertion tasks, perturb
each along controlled axes of occlusion and physical tolerance,
and test whether conclusions transfer to the real world. Task
dependence proves to be more subtle than previous, broadly
scoped evaluations revealed: the best representation differs even
between our near-identical tasks, and small changes shift trends
in unpredictable ways. Despite this variability, results across
simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate the distinct
value of spatially-grounded, and in particular, contact-centric
information for contact-rich policy learning, while underscoring
how difficult it is to estimate such cues reliably under realistic
visual complexity and sensor noise. We hope these findings
motivate further study of representations in contact-rich ma-
nipulation, along with advances in contact perception and its
integration into visuomotor policy learning.
I. INTRODUCTION
Imitation learning has emerged as a predominant paradigm
for robotic manipulation, enabling hobbyists and researchers
alike to train visuomotor policies for diverse real-world
tasks [1], [2], [3], [4]. Yet state-of-the-art imitation learning
remains brittle to task-irrelevant visual shifts unless sup-
ported by laborious amounts of demonstration data. Pre-
trained visual representations promise to ease this burden
by leveraging priors distilled from internet-scale datasets [5],
[6], [7]. However, which representation is “best” for robotics
remains hotly debated: studies to date have evaluated broad
but sparse task distributions and reached conclusions that
vary and even contradict one another, with most suggesting
the answer is highly task- and setting-dependent [8], [9],
[10], [11]. Consistent with this, modern robotics foundation
models (RFMs) have not converged on a single recipe,
instead adopting disparate pre-trained vision encoders [12],
[13], [14].
This uncertainty is especially salient in fine-grained,
contact-rich
manipulation.

datasets and state-of-the-art visual representations, RFMs
still struggle with contact-rich behaviors, with performance
often lagging behind that on gross motor skills and soft-
object manipulation. Perhaps because of this difficulty, fine-
grained, contact-rich manipulation remains underrepresented
in robot learning: datasets and benchmarks largely emphasize
reaching, pick-and-place, or articulated object manipulation
where successful behaviors demand neither millimeter-level
precision in positioning nor frequent contact mode transitions
across different surfaces [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20].
When contact-rich tasks do appear, they are often posed with
generous tolerances that avoid tight-fit failure modes.
We argue this gap persists because contact-rich tasks
both amplify common challenges and introduce unique ones.
Rigid-body contact yields stiff, highly sensitive dynamics
where small errors can cause discontinuous outcomes. Tight
tolerances and friction induce hard-to-recover failures (e.g.,
jamming and wedging) that are pervasive in real assem-
blies [21], [22]. Finally, during close proximity and contact,
task-relevant surfaces and their gaps are frequently occluded,
limiting visual feedback precisely when and where it is most
needed. These challenges have motivated efforts to incor-
porate spatially-dense contact and proximity information of
critical object surfaces from tactile (and sometimes visual)
sensors into visuomotor policy learning [23], [24], [25], [26],
[27], but its value relative to standard visual priors remains
unclear.
In this work, we restrict the question of the “right”
visual representation to contact-rich manipulation, specif-
ically insertion, and ask: (1) Do general-purpose vision
foundation model (VFM) features (e.g., DINOv2 [5], SAM-
2 [28], Theia [29]) improve policy performance over training
from scratch? (2) Can explicit contact-centric information
outperform VFMs, and under what conditions? Prior studies
have pursued such questions over broad sets of disparate
tasks, drawing preliminary conclusions that contact-rich tasks
will benefit more from contact representations [23], [27].
These studies are beneficial for surveying the landscape
of manipulation tasks, but we argue here that this breadth
obscures the severity of the very task-dependence they report.
Here, we specifically study a narrow domain, two similar
insertion tasks, where previous studies would have reason-
ably indicated that contact features would bring value to both
tasks. By evaluating policies in a narrow task domain, we are
able to identify and highlight the surprising sensitivity in our
findings to subtle changes in these tasks. To expose setting-
dependent sensitivity, we perturb tasks along controlled axes:
camera viewpoint shifts that modulate occlusion, and the

tightness of physical tolerances. Finally, we extend our
evaluation to the real world, where visual complexity and
sensor noise provide further nuance to simulation results.
As this manuscript will detail, our results identify a
surprising level of sensitivity, often defying traditional ex-
pectations. (1) In keeping with published results, we fully
expected VFMs, particularly the robotics-tailored Theia, to
substantially improve policy performance. However, while
at least one VFM managed to provide substantial gains in
most settings, the best-performing representation differed
across tasks: Theia performed best in our real-world task
but consistently matched baselines across simulated ones.
Meanwhile, segmentation masks performed best in one sim-
ulated task but degraded once we introduced occlusions.
(2) Regarding contact-specific features, we hypothesized that
explicit contact information would be more useful than com-
mon VFMs. Ground-truth (GT) contact information indeed
consistently outperformed other representations across all
settings, pointing to the critical importance of directly collo-
cated tactile-like spatial features. However, when occlusions
were minimal, segmentation masks performed just as well.
Furthermore, estimated contact features struggled to match
the performance of their ground-truth counterparts when
faced with heavy occlusions or real-world sensor noise.
In sum, our contributions are as follows:
1) We take a hotly contested question in robot learning,
restrict it to fine-grained, contact-rich insertion tasks,
and demonstrate that the benefit of pre-trained visual
representations is even less predictable than previously
suggested.
2) Our results highlight not only that the best representa-
tion is highly task-dependent, but that seemingly small
variations in a task can affect trends in unpredictable
ways.
3) Despite this variability, our study provides evidence
that contact information can offer distinct value, while
also underscoring the practical challenge of inferring
it reliably under occlusion and sensor noise.
Ultimately, our findings show that within the contact-rich
domain, it is difficult to anticipate the right representa-
tion among common VFMs. While explicitly incorporating
contact information into visuomotor policy learning holds
promise, realizing that promise remains challenging. We
offer this study less as a prescription than as a controlled
demonstration that no reliable prescription currently exists.
This motivates further research into the role of visual repre-
sentations in contact-rich settings, the advancement of robust
contact perception capabilities, and their effective integration
into policy learning algorithms.

Leveraging pre-trained visual representations for manipu-
lation is an active topic in robot learning. Chen et al. [8]
studied interpretable mid-level visual representations and
found surface normals and “3D edges” provide consistent
gains across simulated reaching, pushing, and pick-and-place
tasks, though the best representation remains highly task-
dependent.

(e.g., R3M, MVP, VC-1).

Most similar to our work, Yang et al. [11] investigate
several “spatially-grounded” representations. Notably, they
include a simulated peg-insertion and puzzle-board assembly
task. Consistent with the literature, they find different tasks
benefit from different representations: object position traces
are best for peg-insertion while object poses are preferable
for assembly. However, no details of object dimensions,

it difficult to contextualize the results. In contrast, our work

3 mm tolerances for peg-insertion and negative clearance
for the book-insertion task, pushing the demand for precise
control and contact-rich interactions.
Our work builds on insights from the literature while
maintaining a tightly-scoped task regime. We adopt DINOv2,
Theia, segmentation masks, and training-from-scratch as
baselines in our study, but we explicitly focus on fine-
grained, contact-rich manipulation, where we hypothesize
success hinges on representing the precise spatial relation-
ships between task-relevant objects. To this end, we introduce
a novel contact-centric visual representation specific to our
study which is designed to expose gaps and local geometry
salient to contact-rich behaviors.
III. METHODOLOGY
We use a diffusion policy architecture [1], employing a
ResNet-18 encoder [35] to condition a U-Net denoiser [36].
The policy is trained to predict SE(3) end-effector trajec-
tories over a 1.4 s horizon subsampled at 25 Hz from
the demonstration data. The diffusion policy operates at
5 Hz, with planned trajectories executed by a low-level
impedance controller operating at 1 kHz. To achieve real-
time performance on an NVIDIA RTX 2070 SUPER, we
tune the denoising diffusion implicit model’s scheduler [37]
to 50 training steps and 10 inference steps. Other training
and inference hyperparameters match the original implemen-
tation.
Demonstrations are collected via a 3Dconnexion Space-
Mouse. The 6D inputs are scaled to a delta SE(3) transfor-
mation integrated over time to produce desired end-effector
poses at 100 Hz and tracked by the same impedance con-
troller used during policy execution. Recorded data includes
30 Hz RGB-D frames, 1 kHz joint states, 100 Hz desired
end-effector poses, and 1 kHz impedance controller states.
To address real-world execution latencies, we linearly
interpolate desired end-effector trajectories from the policy
by measuring the delay with respect to the timestamp of the
policy’s image input. In contrast to Chi et al. [2] where a
fixed latency was assumed, by continually measuring delays
we can adapt to variability induced by inference times and
networking.
The baseline policy, which we refer to as “training from
scratch,” receives the common standard of current RGB
image and end-effector pose as inputs. Extending this: (1)
We concatenate the corresponding depth image with the
RGB image and (2) include a short history of previous
actions, which are encoded using a small 1D convolutional
network. Anecdotally, we found these additions improve the
data efficiency and performance of the baseline while being
straightforward to implement.
We compare the baseline policy against variations aug-
mented with the following pre-trained visual features:
1) DINOv2 (+DINOv2): A representative task-agnostic
latent feature trained via self-supervised learning on
real images. Following the recommendations from
Theia’s authors, we use the model variant incorporating
registers [38], and provide policies with only the dense
patch tokens.
2) Theia (+Theia): A SoTA latent feature tailored for
robotics distilling CLIP, DINOv2, and ViT [29]. The
authors claim this combination was best among addi-
tional choices of Depth-Anything [39] and SAM. Theia
was also found to outperform robotic-specific VFMs
like R3M and MVP.
3) Segmentation Masks (+Msk): A representative inter-
pretable, object-centric feature. We obtain segmenta-
tion masks capturing the grasped object’s projected
visible geometry. To disambiguate the utility of the
representation itself from the quality of estimates, we
include in our policy evaluations both estimated masks
from SAM-2 [28] and ground-truth for simulated ex-
periments.
4) Contact-Centric Features (+Cntct): Another inter-
pretable, object-centric feature inspired by the work
of [40], [41]. Tailored to this study as a proof-of-
concept, we attempt to capture spatial information
between the grasped object and environment localized
to surfaces near and in-contact. Our model ingests a
depth image and estimates a probabilistic heatmap of
contact regions and for close-to-contact surfaces of the
grasped-object and environment: single-channel maps
of distances-to-contact and three-channel maps of the
normals (Fig. 3). We trained this model via super-
vised learning using a small-scale synthetic dataset. As
with segmentation masks, we compared estimated and
ground-truth versions. Additionaly, we find providing
a mask of the grasped-object alongside depth improves
estimation and include the bolstered model in our eval-
uations (referred to as “+Cntct(Msk)”) when possible.
We downsample the raw 480 × 640 RGB-D images to
180×240 for each policy. To augment the baseline with pre-
trained visual features, we concatenate pre-trained feature
maps and RGB-D inputs along the channel dimension when
possible. To achieve this for SAM-2 and our contact-centric
features, we provide images at 240 × 320 and downsample
the outputs to 180×240. However, for DINOv2 and Theia’s
patch features, this yields 15 × 20 patch features that are
downsampled to 12 × 15 and instead concatenated between
the third and fourth block of the ResNet encoder. While more
sophisticated strategies for imparting visual priors may exist,
we leave their exploration to future work. In the real world,


due to VRAM limits, we evaluate VFMs on a secondary
workstation streaming features over UDP.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments evaluate visual representations for vi-
suomotor policy learning in fine-grained, contact-rich tasks,
addressing the questions from the introduction. We measure
a representation’s utility as whether its inclusion improves
task success rate over the from-scratch baseline across data
budgets. We focus on insertion tasks, which encapsulates
the core challenges of this domain: occlusion of critical
spatial information, precise positioning demands, and fric-
tional failure modes. We propose two instantiations, each
requiring distinct behaviors, and qualify simulation findings
with a real-world analogue. Simulated tasks are implemented
in ManiSkill [42].
A. Common Setup and Evaluation
For all tasks, environments feature a single external static
RGB-D camera placed across from, and looking at the robot.
Honing in on the difficult insertion stage of each task, we
initialize the robot with a pre-grasped object. The initial robot
pose remains fixed across episodes while object geometries,
color, and target locations are randomized, demanding gen-
eralization and robustness to irrelevant visuals. We observe
that policy performance variance is often dominated by
demonstration dataset quality rather than training seeds,
especially in lower data regimes. Therefore, for simulated
tasks, we train policies at each data budget using five random
subsets of demonstrations and report the mean and standard
error. Across data budgets, we nest demonstration subsets.
i.e. for the same seed, the 30-demonstration subset is fully
contained within the 50-demonstration subset. Policies are
trained for 300k iterations with checkpoints evaluated every
20k steps on 100 unseen environments to select the model
with the highest success rate. Final evaluations are conducted
on a unique set of 300 unseen environments. Success is
determined by achieving insertion within a specified time
budget of 1.2× the duration of the slowest demonstration.

V. RESULTS
Our results suggest that the impact of visual represen-
tations are highly task-dependent in our setting and often
challenged our initial expectations. We organize our analysis
around the motivating questions, which we find most salient
for understanding the results and guiding future research
directions. While we conducted extensive evaluations in
simulation, practical considerations make this difficult in the
real world. Nevertheless, we observe several trends that raise
interesting questions.
A. Common General-purpose VFMs
Despite the state-of-the-art performance reported by Theia
across simulated benchmarks, both it and DINOv2 failed
to outperform the from-scratch baseline in the simulated
book-insertion task (Fig. 10). This suggests that neither
DINOv2 on its own or the added distillations of CLIP and
ViT provided by Theia’s features confers significant benefits
in this setting. We note these results do not necessarily
contradict the findings from Theia’s authors where out of
12 simulated tasks, Theia performed significantly better than
DINOv2 on only 5. Due to its state-of-the-art status and
robotics-tailored design, we maintain Theia as a representa-
tive general-purpose VFM for the remaining tasks. Results
in the simulated peg-in-hole setting echo those from book-
insertion, with Theia performing on par with the baseline
across tolerances and degrading similarly as tolerances are
tightened (Fig. 9).
Though Theia failed to outperform the baseline in sim-
ulation, we find it provides significant boosts in the real-
world book-insertion task at both low and high data budgets

B. Contact-Centric Features
Ground-truth contact-centric information is consistently
among the best representations across all simulated tasks
and is the only representation to significantly outperform the
baseline across all peg-insertion tolerances. These margins
are most prominent in the low and mid data regimes (30
and 50 demonstrations) and at tighter tolerances (3 mm and
9 mm). However, it is closely matched by segmentation
masks in the simulated book-insertion setting (Fig. 10). This
suggests that explicitly encoding the geometric relationship
between the grasped object and environment, especially of
surfaces near and in-contact, is especially useful in tightly-
toleranced insertion tasks. With the caveat that when relevant
interfaces are clearly visible, other spatially-grounded repre-
sentations such as segmentation masks may provide sufficient
structure for inferring task-critical information.
Similarly, we found that the estimated contact-centric fea-
tures are as good as their ground-truth counterparts for book-
insertion (Fig. 10), with both largely maintaining their edge
over the baseline across changing viewpoints (Fig. 11). This
seems to be anecdotally reflected in the estimation quality of
our contact features which, while noticeably degraded, still
manage reasonable contact-region estimates despite greater
occlusions. However, these gains failed to transfer to the
peg-in-hole task (Fig. 9). This likely reflects the sustained
occlusion of critical faces during insertion attempts of the
peg, making inference of relative configurations and contact
difficult. Moreover, in the real-world book-insertion task,
estimated contact-centric features do not provide significant
gains over training from scratch at either data budget, likely
due to the challenges of accurate estimation in the presence
of real-world sensor noise. However, the combination of
contact-centric features and masks yields a significant boost
across data budgets, outperforming either alone and the
baseline, suggesting these two representations may provide
synergistic benefits in real-world settings.
VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
We present a controlled study of visual representations for
visuomotor policy learning in fine-grained, contact-rich ma-
nipulation. Rather than surveying a broad task distribution,
we focus explicitly on two closely-related insertion tasks, a
rigidly constrained peg-in-hole setup and a dynamic book-
insertion scenario, and perturb each with targeted variations
in physical tolerance and task-relevant occlusion. Across
these settings, we compare canonical representations from
vision foundation models against a hypothesis-driven repre-
sentation that explicitly encodes contact-centric information.
Our results underscore the highly task-dependent and
often unpredictable nature of which visual priors are most
useful for policy learning. Relative trends differ drastically
across settings and even fluctuate unexpectedly over subtle
variations within the same task. Task dynamics, viewpoints,
sensor noise, and visual clutter all seem to modulate which
priors provide value. These results echo community-wide
findings, but we believe that the contact-rich domain, with
stiff, complex dynamics and occlusions, is especially re-
sistant to one-size-fits-all strategies. Instead, representation
choice in this domain currently demands per-task empirical
validation, reinforcing our claim that no reliable prescription
yet exists.
In spite of this variability, one result held across every
setting: ground-truth contact information was the only repre-
sentation to deliver consistent gains, underscoring the impor-
tance of directly capturing geometric relationships between
the manipuland and its environment. Our proof-of-concept
contact-centric representation shows that such features can
improve learning outcomes when estimates are accurate.
By contrast, widely-utilized VFM features like DINOv2
and Theia failed to outperform from-scratch training in
simulation experiments. When task-relevant occlusions are
minimal, segmentation masks offer an off-the-shelf solution
that confers comparable gains to contact-centric features.
However, under realistic visual complexity and sensor noise,
accurate spatial relationships and contact cues are difficult to
infer. In these settings, combining segmentation masks with
contact-centric features seems to yield synergistic benefits,
outperforming either alone. These findings motivate advanc-
ing contact perception capabilities and their integration into
visuomotor policy learning. While dense proximity or tactile
sensing at key interfaces directly captures critical informa-
tion, such instrumentation is often impractical. A pragmatic
path may be to fuse strong shape priors and pose estimation
from vision with distal haptic or tactile sensing to infer the
inter-object states that govern contact mode transitions.
Several factors qualify our conclusions. First, variance
across randomized demonstration subsets suggests demon-
stration quality is a major driver of policy performance.
Furthermore, we note that this intra-seed variance may also
be attributable to checkpoint evaluation frequency. Future
work should disentangle these effects to clarify their roles
and interactions. We also note some limitations. Confidence
in real-world results is bottlenecked by practical constraints,
such as early stopping, reproducibility of environment con-
ditions across policy rollouts, and limited evaluation scale.
Higher-fidelity simulation and sophisticated statistical meth-
ods may offer a tractable alternative to scaling real-world
trials [12]. Finally, extending this controlled, magnifying-
glass methodology to other contact-rich settings, especially


those demanding delicate yet forceful interaction, would
test whether the instability we document is endemic to the
domain, and remains an important avenue for future work
