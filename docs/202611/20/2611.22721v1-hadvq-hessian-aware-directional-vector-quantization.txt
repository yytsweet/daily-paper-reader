HADVQ: Hessian Aware Directional Vector
Quantization

Valery Sandul2, Daniil Ryazanov2, Ekaterina Muravleva1
1Applied AI Institute, Moscow, Russia

Abstract
Ultra-low-bit quantization of large language models is a challenging task because
aggressive compression severely distorts model parameters and disproportionately
harms a small number of highly sensitive channels. We present a vector quantization
method tailored for this regime. Our approach begins with random preconditioning,
which regularizes the weight space and makes it more amenable to quantization.
We then represent weights in polar coordinates and decouple the quantization
of directions and magnitudes. To quantize directions, we leverage the structure
induced by random preconditioning and design a structured codebook based on
the E8 lattice, yielding a compact yet expressive representation. To further reduce
degradation, we detect extremely important channels using a proxy Hessian and
preserve them with an additional cost of only 0.16 bits. Together, these components
provide an effective quantization pipeline for LLMs in the extremely low-bit
regime. Empirical results demonstrate improved accuracy, highlighting the benefit
of combining geometric structure, lattice-based codebooks, and selective channel
preservation.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT Brown et al. [5], LLaMA Touvron et al. [41, 42],
Grattafiori et al. [19], DeepSeek, and Qwen Yang et al. [47] have become a central component of
modern natural language processing. Their strong performance across a wide range of language tasks
has led to rapid adoption, but their large memory footprint remains a major obstacle for practical
deployment. For example, storing very large models in BF16 may require hundreds of gigabytes or
even more than a terabyte of memory, which makes deployment expensive and hardware-demanding.
Several approaches have been proposed to reduce model size, including distillation Hinton et al.
[25], Kim and Rush [29], pruning [22], low-rank approximation Denton et al. [10], and quantiza-
tion Han et al. [21]. In this work, we focus on post-training quantization (PTQ), a practical approach
for reducing the memory cost of pretrained models without retraining them from scratch. Scalar
quantization (SQ) Frantar et al. [15], Lin et al. [31], Xiao et al. [46], which maps each weight
independently to a low-bit representation, is widely used and performs well in the 3–4-bit regime.
However, in the more aggressive low-bit setting, vector quantization (VQ) Chee et al. [6], Tseng et al.
[43], Yue et al. [49] is often more robust because it can better capture local structure in the weight
space.
In this paper, we study ultra-low-bit vector quantization without fine-tuning. Our method is
motivated by the observation that quantization error is not distributed uniformly across channels:
a relatively small subset of channels contributes disproportionately to performance degradation.
Building on this observation, we propose a structured vector quantization method that combines
random preconditioning, magnitude–direction decoupling, and Hessian-aware channel preservation.

Contributions.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• We propose a training-free vector quantization method that combines magnitude–direction
decoupling with Hessian-aware preservation of sensitive channels.
• We introduce and study new strategies for directional codebook construction, and show in
ablation experiments that the final design performs better than alternative initializations.
• We evaluate the method across multiple model families and benchmarks, including both
question answering and perplexity tasks, and study how quantization sensitivity differs
across models.
• We provide code and supporting materials to facilitate reproducibility.

2.1
Post-training Quantization of LLMs
Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces the cost of a pretrained model by encoding its weights
in a low-bit representation without retraining the model from scratch. For large language models,
this setting is important because full-precision checkpoints use a lot of memory, and lower weight
precision makes deployment easier.

Random Preconditioning.
A direct application of vector quantization to raw weight blocks is
often difficult because their distribution is highly unbalanced across coordinates. In particular, a
small number of coordinates may dominate the quantization error, which makes a fixed codebook

3
HADVQ: Hessian Aware Directional Vector Quantization
HADVQ combines several ideas within a single post-training quantization pipeline. Figure 1 gives
an overview of the method. Starting from the weights of linear layers in a pretrained model, we
first apply random preconditioning to make weight blocks more suitable for vector quantization.
Each preconditioned block is then represented by its magnitude and direction. The directional
component is quantized with a structured codebook, while the magnitude is quantized separately.
The quantized direction and magnitude are then combined to reconstruct the weight block. Finally,
to reduce the degradation caused by aggressive compression, we preserve a small subset of highly
sensitive channels selected using proxy Hessian scores. Together, these steps define our method for
post-training quantization in the extremely low-bit regime.

Models and Datasets.
We evaluate a diverse collection of large language models from the
Qwen3 [47], Llama2 [42], and Llama3 [19] families. Our experiments cover sizes ranging from
0.6B to 13B parameters, including Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B/4B/8B, Llama2-7B/13B, Llama3-8B, and
Llama3.2-1B/3B. We report perplexity on the WikiText-2 [34] test set and zero-shot accuracy on
BoolQ [7], WinoGrande [38], ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge [8], HellaSwag [50], PIQA [4], and
MMLU [24, 23], and additionally report five-shot results on MMLU. For downstream evaluation, we
use the LM Eval Harness [17] versions 0.3.0, 0.4.4, and 0.4.11, depending on model and benchmark
compatibility.
Baselines.
We compare against representative PTQ baselines. Scalar quantization baselines include
GPTQ [15], AWQ [31], QuaRot [1], OSTQuant [26], and ReSpinQuant [28]. Vector quantization
baselines include QuIP# [43], AQLM [13], ICQuant [30], VPTQ [32], PCDVQ [49], and QTIP [44].
Some vector quantization methods, such as QuIP#, AQLM, PCDVQ, and QTIP, can optionally be
combined with fine-tuning to further improve performance. In contrast, our method is evaluated in a
pure post-training setting without fine-tuning. We leave this direction for future work.
Calibration setup.
For proxy Hessian estimation, we use mit-han-lab/pile-val-backup [16]
with 256 calibration samples of sequence length 1024. The resulting scores are used to identify highly
sensitive channels for selective preservation.
Implementation details.
We use a block size of 8 and evaluate effective bit budgets of 2.41,
2.535, and 3.41 bits per parameter. We quantize directional and magnitude components separately.
Magnitudes are quantized to 2 bits using the Lloyd–Max algorithm [33], while directions are quan-
tized to 2, 2.125, and 3 bits for the 2.41, 2.535, and 3.41 bit settings, respectively. For efficient
nearest-neighbor lookup in the directional codebook, we use torch_kdtree [18]. Randomized
Hadamard preconditioning is implemented with fast-hadamard-transform [9]. Proxy Hessian
scores are aggregated at the channel level and used to rank channels by sensitivity, and the top-ranked
channels are preserved separately. Our implementation is based on PyTorch [36] and Hugging Face
Transformers [45]. All experiments were conducted on a single V100 32GB GPU.

sensitive channels is important in the extremely low-bit regime: removing these channels or replacing
them with random ones leads to a clear drop in both downstream accuracy and WikiText-2 perplexity.
Figure 2 shows that preserving 1% of sensitive channels reduces quantization loss on a number of
layers, although it does not remove the overall growth of the loss across depth and does not fully stop
error accumulation. Figure 3 shows that 99% of channels have relatively small sensitivity compared
to the top 1% sensitive channels. Second, the proposed codebook construction is also important:
both the greedy min-max initialization, similar to the procedure used in Yue et al. [49], and the
evolutionary alternative perform worse than the final HADVQ design. For the evolutionary approach,
we use the method of Khrulkov et al. [27]. The best results are obtained when both components are
used together. We also observe that reducing the preserved fraction from 1% to 0.5% only slightly
affects performance on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, but causes a more visible drop on Qwen3-0.6B,
suggesting that smaller models are more sensitive to this budget.

5
Related Work
Post-Training Quantization.
PTQ is an approach for compressing LLMs without retraining, and
it has become a crucial technique for LLMs deployment. Lin et al. [31] introduced a technique
that protects a small subset of salient channels determined from the activation distribution. Frantar
et al. [15] showed a method that is based on approximate second-order information. Xiao et al.
[46] explored the fact that weights are easy to quantize while activations are not; they smoothed the
activation outliers from activations to weights. These works show that the quality of the PTQ method
depends on salient channels, second-order information, and outlier handling.
Sensitivity-aware and outlier-aware quantization.
An important observation in quantization
is that the sensitivity of weight channels and outliers of weight values can dramatically influence
quantization performance. There are works [12, 11, 48] that are dedicated to measuring layer
sensitivity using Hessian trace information. As mentioned above Lin et al. [31] used activation
statistics to identify salient channels. Li et al. [30] leveraged outlier statistics to design an efficient
index coding scheme for low-bit quantization. Our method belongs to this family of methods, we
identify an extremely small subset of highly sensitive channels using a proxy Hessian criterion.
Per-channel transformations.
A single global quantization scale does not consider sensitive
channels and different value ranges. Therefore, per-channel transformations improve quantization
quality. Xiao et al. [46] uses per-channel scaling to migrate outlier channels from activation to weight.
Shao et al. [39] uses a differentiable approach to learn optimal scaling and shifting parameters. In our
work, we use another approach with random preconditioning to change the geometry of the weight
space.
8


Preconditioning-based quantization.
Another line of work improves quantization by transforming
the geometry of weights and activations before the quantization procedure. Sun et al. [40] developed
a learnable affine transformation that flattens the distributions of weights and activations. Kim
et al. [28] proposed a rotation-based transformation using offline activation rotation fusion and
matching bases with efficient residual subspace rotation. Tseng et al. [43] uses randomized Hadamard
preconditioning to make weight matrices more incoherent. These methods explain that the geometry
of weights/activations is a very important factor for low-bit quantization.
Vector quantization.
In ultra-low-bit scenario, scalar quantization becomes inadequate because it is
difficult to consider the distribution and geometry of weights. Egiazarian et al. [13] demonstrated from
the point of view of additive quantization and multi-codebook quantization through learned additive
codebooks. Tseng et al. [43] combines incoherence processing through random preconditioning with
lattice codebooks based on the E8 lattice. Liu et al. [32] formulates vector post-training quantization
as a second-order optimization problem and introduces channel-independent refinement and improved
codebook initialization. Yue et al. [49] found out that the directional component of a weight vector
exhibits significantly greater quantization sensitivity compared to magnitude. Our method is closely
related to this line of work; it combines random preconditioning, direction-magnitude decomposition,
and structured vector quantization.
6
Conclusion
We presented HADVQ, a post-training quantization method for large language models in the extremely
low-bit regime. The method combines random preconditioning, magnitude–direction decoupling,
structured directional vector quantization, and Hessian aware preservation of sensitive input channels.
Together, these components provide a practical way to reduce model size while maintaining strong
downstream performance.
Across multiple Llama and Qwen models, HADVQ achieves a favorable accuracy–compression
trade-off and remains competitive with recent scalar and vector quantization baselines, despite not
using fine-tuning. The ablation study further shows that both the structured directional codebook and
sensitive-channel preservation contribute to the final performance, while the selected channels remain
relatively stable across calibration datasets.
A direction for future work is to extend HADVQ beyond the current post-training setting, for
example by combining it with fine-tuning or by studying larger block sizes and alternative structured
codebooks.
7
Limitations
This work has several limitations. First, we consider only the weight-only post-training quantization
setting and do not study activation quantization or joint weight–activation compression. Second,
the current method is evaluated purely without fine-tuning, so its behavior in combination with
post-quantization fine-tuning remains an open question. Third, the directional codebook is based on
the E8 lattice and is therefore naturally tied to 8-dimensional blocks; extending the same construction
to other block sizes may require different structured codebooks. Finally, the method relies on a
random Hadamard transform (RHT), which must also be applied during de-quantization and therefore
introduces additional runtime overhead. In practice, this overhead is moderate since the transform can
be computed in O(n log n) time. The additional memory used by the codebooks and the sensitive-
channel mask is shared across the whole model and is therefore negligible when spread over billions
of parameters.

C
Broader Impacts
This work studies post-training quantization for large language models. A potential positive impact
is that improved ultra-low-bit quantization can reduce memory usage and deployment costs, which
may make language models more accessible in resource-constrained settings and lower the hardware
requirements needed for inference.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The main claims in the abstract and introduction are consistent with the actual
scope of the paper and are supported by both the experimental results and the ablation study.
In particular, the paper claims that HADVQ combines random preconditioning, magnitude–
direction decoupling, structured directional vector quantization, and Hessian-aware channel
preservation, and these components are described in Section 3 and evaluated in Sections 4.2
and 4.3. The abstract and introduction also focus on the extremely low-bit post-training
setting, which matches the experiments reported in the paper.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The paper includes a dedicated Limitations section discussing the main
constraints of the current study, including the focus on weight-only PTQ, the fact that
the method is not evaluated together with fine-tuning, the dependence of the directional
codebook on 8-dimensional E8 structure, and the additional runtime overhead introduced
by the random Hadamard transform. It also clarifies that the extra memory used by the
codebooks and the sensitive-channel mask is negligible when amortized over a model with
billions of parameters.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
