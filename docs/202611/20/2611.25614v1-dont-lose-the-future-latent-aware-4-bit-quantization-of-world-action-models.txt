Abstract
World action models (WAMs) predict how a scene will
evolve and condition their actions on this predicted future.
Deploying them on a robot’s onboard computer calls for 4-
bit weights, activations and key–value caches (W4A4KV4).
Existing 4-bit quantizers for WAMs preserve the generated
action, and estimate sensitivity one denoising step at a time.
However, they fail to preserve the fidelity of the predicted
future: once the action pathway is protected, the remain-
ing action error enters mostly through a degraded future
that none of them deliberately protects. We propose LAQ,
a latent-aware quantization framework that preserves the
predicted future where it is written, stored and read. Specif-
ically, we introduce a reader metric, which measures future
error by its effect on the action, and a read count, which
weights each rounding error by the number of denoising
steps that read it. Together they give the allocation of preci-
sion over activations and caches a closed-form solution. We
further develop gaze-guided refinement, which gives the to-
kens the action attends to a second 4-bit pass in the denois-
ing loop and in the cache. All weights stay at 4 bits, with no
high-precision branch and no per-task recalibration. On the
LIBERO-Plus benchmark, LAQ reaches 76.4% and 83.3%
average success on Fast-WAM and Cosmos-Policy, within
1.2 percentage points of the 16-bit models and all above the
baselines with 16-bit KV caches. It compresses the model
by 3.8×, reduces the peak GPU memory of the whole policy
by 2.3×, and runs 1.66× faster than the 16-bit model. On a
real AgileX PiPER arm, it reaches 47.0% average success
over three tasks, against 52.0% for the 16-bit model and
30.0% higher than QuaRot.

2. Related Work
World action models. World action models train a video
generator jointly with an action model and condition actions
on a predicted future [18, 19, 35, 36], which extends vision-
language-action policies [3, 17] with the visual priors, and
the size, of video backbones.
Post-training quantization. Second-order rounding com-
pensates the error of each layer with the inverse Hes-
sian of a layer-wise reconstruction objective [9, 10], and
activation-aware scaling [22, 33] and rotations [2, 5, 24]
remove activation outliers, which brings language models
to 4-bit weights, activations and key–value caches. Mixed-
precision methods allocate bit widths across layers [25, 30],
and GuidedQuant weights the layer objective with gradi-
ents of the end loss [16]. For diffusion and video genera-
tors, post-training methods correct errors that accumulate
over the denoising loop [11], handle timestep-dependent
activations [39] and absorb outliers into a 16-bit low-rank
branch [20]; quantization-aware training reaches 4 bits and
below for these generators [13, 21] and for vision-language
models [6].
Cache quantizers compress the key–value
caches of video-language models [27, 28] and of video gen-
erators and world models [32, 38], down to 2 bits and below.
These methods preserve layer outputs or generation qual-
ity; LAQ builds on rotation and second-order rounding, and
changes what the quantizer preserves: the predicted future
as the reader sees it.
Quantization of robot policies and WAMs. QuantVLA
and BitVLA compress vision-language-action policies with
calibrated scales and 1-bit weights [29, 37]. For WAMs,
QuantWAMs [40], Q-WAM [1] and SteerQuant [31] decide
which channels, activation directions, token streams and de-
noising steps to protect by their impact on the action, and
PreDE predicts the task degradation of a quantization con-
figuration from offline action deviations [34]. They measure
sensitivity to the final action or the training loss, one denois-
ing step at a time. LAQ instead targets the predicted future
that the action is read from, counts how often each rounding
error is read, and quantizes the caches.
Token-level precision.
Squat keeps the tokens with the
highest attention at 8 bits [26], STaMP concentrates the
activation energy in a few tokens that receive higher pre-
cision [7], SemanticDialect re-quantizes the residual of
salient tokens in video diffusion transformers [15], and Zip-
Cache assigns cache precision by token saliency [12]. In all
of these token-level quantizers, a model’s own attention or
activation energy decides which tokens matter. In a WAM,
the predicted future is consumed by a different part of the
model, and LAQ lets this reader decide: tokens are selected
by their effect on the action and tracked at run time through
the attention of the action tokens.

3.2. Observations
We quantize Fast-WAM [36] to W4A4KV4 with rotation
and second-order rounding [2, 10], protect the action path-
way by keeping the activations of the action tokens at higher
precision, and measure A, Z and ρ on LIBERO contexts.
We also inject errors of equal energy that are either frozen
across the denoising steps or redrawn at every step.
Observation 1: Protecting the action is not enough. Sen-
sitivities measured on the final action concentrate on the ac-
tion pathway [1], which suggests protecting this pathway
first. Doing so removes only 16% of the action error, and
the future error does not change at all (Tab. 1). Restoring
the predicted future to full precision removes 71.2% of the
remaining action error (Fig. 1). This implies that the quanti-
zation target must include the predicted future itself, rather
than only the generated action.
Observation 2: The future is damaged where it is writ-
ten, and most by errors that are read many times. Rais-
ing the precision of the reader leaves the future error exactly
unchanged, so the future can only be preserved in the com-
putation that writes it. Within this computation, rounding
errors differ in how often they are read: the tokens of the
observed frame keep the same rounding error at every de-
noising step, cached keys and values are written once and
read at every step, and a weight is read by every token at
every step. An injected error of fixed energy damages the
future 3.1×, 2.2× and 2.1× more in the observation tokens,
the observation cache and the text cache when it is frozen
across the denoising steps than when it is redrawn at each
step (Tab. 2), because its effects on successive steps add up.
A sensitivity estimated one step at a time misses this accu-
mulation: quantizing the ten video denoising steps together
damages the part of the future that the reader depends on
2.50× more than the sum of the ten steps quantized one at
a time (Tab. 2, bottom). This implies that an error should
be weighted by how many times it is read, not only by the
layer and step where it occurs.

Observation 3: The reader depends on a narrow part of
the future, stable in location but not in direction. We
linearize the reader at the full-precision future and com-

dimensional latent, M has an effective rank of 41.5, and the
first predicted frame carries 98% of its trace. Which loca-
tions of the latent the reader depends on is stable: the di-
agonal of M correlates at 0.86 across disjoint halves of the
calibration contexts, and it concentrates on a small region of
the scene, the arm and the gripper (Fig. 3). The directions
are not: the leading 32 eigenvectors estimated on the two
halves overlap by only 0.19. This implies that protection
should follow the tokens the reader depends on, rather than
a fixed set of directions.
4. Methodology
As illustrated in Fig. 2, LAQ preserves the predicted future
where it is written, stored and read. A reader metric and a
read count, both estimated offline, place the errors of activa-
tions and caches in one objective with a closed-form mini-
mizer, and gaze-guided refinement gives the selected tokens
a second 4-bit pass at run time. LAQ involves no training.

do so without leaving 4-bit arithmetic.

The gaze gj of token j is its share of the reader’s sensi-

control step of an episode uses the offline gaze. Observa-

jointly, the gaze of one denoising step selects the tokens of
the next. The selection therefore follows the reader in the
current scene, and the quantities fixed offline (M, nu, ϕs
and µ) do not depend on the task, so LAQ needs no recali-
bration for a new task.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental setup
Models and benchmarks. We quantize Fast-WAM [36]
(6B parameters, of which 1B form the action expert) and
Cosmos-Policy [18] (2B parameters). Fast-WAM first de-
noises the future latent and then denoises an action chunk
that attends to it through a key–value cache; the tokens of
its observed frame do not depend on the denoising step and
are computed once. Cosmos-Policy denoises the future and
the action jointly, so its reader starts from the future state at
an intermediate denoising step and runs the remaining steps
to the action, and its keys and values are quantized at ev-
ery step. We evaluate in simulation on LIBERO-Plus [8],
which perturbs the four LIBERO suites [23] along seven
dimensions, on the original LIBERO suites, and on a real
robot arm (Sec. 5.3). LIBERO-Plus is our main benchmark,
since both BF16 models already succeed in about 98% of
the episodes of the original LIBERO suites.
Baselines and quantization settings. We compare with
the general-purpose quantizers QuaRot [2] and Spin-
Quant [24], and the diffusion quantizers ViDiT-Q [39] and
SVDQuant [20]; the WAM quantizers [1, 31, 40] have no
public implementation. All baselines quantize weights and
activations to 4 bits and keep the cached keys and val-
ues in BF16, whereas LAQ also quantizes the caches to
4 bits. Since adaptive layer normalization prevents a ro-
6


tation of the residual stream, QuaRot and SpinQuant ro-
tate the input of every linear layer by block rotations of
size 128; ViDiT-Q and SVDQuant follow their published
recipes, the latter with a 16-bit low-rank branch of rank 32.
LAQ uses 4-bit weights rounded by GPTQ [10] in random-
ized Hadamard coordinates, 4-bit activations with per-token
scales, and 4-bit keys and values with one scale per token
and head. As is common, all methods keep the input and
output interface layers (0.01% of the weights) in BF16 and
store the timestep-conditioning layers as exact per-step ta-
bles. ViDiT-Q, SVDQuant and LAQ calibrate on the same
80 LIBERO contexts, and the budget of LAQ refines 15.4%
of the activation values of the denoising loops and 6.0% of
the cached keys and values.

The baselines lose 4.6–5.6 points relative to BF16. The
gains of LAQ concentrate on the camera-viewpoint and
sensor-noise perturbations, where the baselines lose the

lines stay within 0.2 points of it, and LAQ reaches 98.2%,
above every baseline and level with BF16.

5.5. Efficiency
Tab. 6 reports the cost of LAQ on Fast-WAM on an NVIDIA
A100, with its weights stored and multiplied as 4-bit codes.
With 4.01 bits per weight and no high-precision branch,
LAQ compresses the denoiser from 12.0 GB to 3.2 GB
(3.8×) and reduces the peak GPU memory of the whole pol-
icy from 14.0 GB to 6.0 GB (2.3×). The cached keys and
values of one call shrink from 204 MB to 56 MB (3.6×).
One policy call takes 888 ms from observation to action
chunk, 1.66× faster than the BF16 model (1475 ms): the
runtime multiplies the packed 4-bit codes directly on the in-
teger tensor cores of the GPU, and the second pass of a re-
fined token is the same integer product on additional rows.
Calibration takes 1.2 GPU-hours with a peak memory of
27 GB, since it only runs forward passes of the quantized
model on calibration contexts.
6. Conclusion
We presented LAQ, a latent-aware quantization framework
for 4-bit world action models. Once the action pathway of
a 4-bit WAM is protected, its remaining action error enters
through a degraded predicted future. LAQ measures future
error as the reader sees it, weights each rounding error
by how many times it is read, allocates precision jointly
over activations and caches, and gives the tokens the reader
attends to a second 4-bit pass.
With every weight at 4
bits and no recalibration per task, it recovers closed-loop
success across two WAMs in simulation and on a real
robot arm.
When compressing a WAM, the predicted
future is not an intermediate quantity that can be sacrificed.
