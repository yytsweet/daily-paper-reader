SoScale: Second-Order Scale Search for FP4 Quantization
Pengxiang Zhao, Xing Li, Hui-Ling Zhen, Weizhe Lin, Xianzhi Yu, Zhenhua Dong
Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd.
Abstract
Low-bit quantization reduces the memory footprint and infer-
ence cost of large language models (LLMs), yet accurate FP4
quantization remains challenging. Practical formats such as
MXFP4, NVFP4, and HiF4 pair a 4-bit floating-point payload
with block-wise or hierarchical scale codes, making accu-
racy highly sensitive to how each local distribution is scaled.
Default maximum-absolute-value scaling preserves dynamic
range, but can spend scarce FP4 levels on rarely occupied out-
lier regions and leave dense tensor values poorly resolved. Ex-
isting scale-search methods minimize local element-wise re-
construction error, optimizing tensor fidelity rather than layer-
output fidelity. We propose SoScale, a second-order weight
scale-code search method for hierarchical FP4 quantization.
SoScale searches hardware-valid scale codes under a Hessian-
aware layer reconstruction objective and couples this search
with GPTQ-style error recovery, selecting scales by their im-
pact on layer outputs. Across Qwen3 models, SoScale con-
sistently reduces downstream quantization degradation under
MXFP4, NVFP4, and HiF4.
Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong
capabilities across a wide range of natural language process-
ing tasks (OpenAI 2026; DeepSeek-AI 2026; Qwen Team
2026), but their deployment remains constrained by sub-
stantial memory footprint and inference cost. Post-training
quantization has therefore become an important technique
for reducing model size and improving serving efficiency
without expensive retraining (Frantar et al. 2023; Xiao et al.
2023; Lin et al. 2024; Shao et al. 2024; Zhao and Yuan
2025). Among recent low-bit formats, FP4 is appealing for
LLM inference because it compresses each value to four bits
while retaining exponent-based dynamic range and enabling
hardware acceleration for low-bit floating-point computation.
Practical FP4 formats, such as MXFP4 (Rouhani et al. 2023),
NVFP4 (Alvarez et al. 2025), and HiF4 (Luo et al. 2026),
further introduce hierarchical scaling mechanisms to better
accommodate the non-uniform distributions of LLM weights
and activations.
Despite this promise, accurate quantization with hierarchi-
cal FP4 formats remains highly sensitive to scale-code selec-
tion. Since the FP4 payload provides only 16 representable

levels near zero, whereas the searched scale contracts the
grid toward the occupied region and improves resolution
for dense values. Figure 1b provides a complementary error
view: after sorting values within the block, the absolute er-
rors at the two tails remain nearly unchanged, while errors in
the middle region, corresponding to values near zero, drop
substantially under the searched scale.
The example above suggests that adapting the scale can be
an effective way to reduce FP4 quantization error, and prior
work has explored several related directions. Some methods
reshape tensor distributions before quantization (Xiao et al.
2023; Ashkboos et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2025);
others compensate residual quantization errors after or dur-
ing quantization (Frantar et al. 2023; Meng et al. 2026); and
FP4-specific methods adapt block scales with format-specific
heuristics or local distortion objectives (Cook et al. 2026;
Gupta et al. 2026). These approaches show that distribution-
aware FP4 quantization is important, but most scale-selection
objectives remain local: they choose a scale code by how
well it reconstructs a block, even though the code ultimately
matters through its effect on the layer output. As shown in
Figure 1c, reducing local element-wise error does not neces-
sarily reduce model-level perplexity. This motivates treating
scale selection as a layer reconstruction problem rather than
a local tensor fitting problem.

training quantization (PTQ) method for weight and acti-
vation scale-code selection in hierarchical FP4 quantiza-
tion. SoScale operates on a fixed pretrained model, with-
out retraining or modifying the target FP4 format; instead,
it searches within the format’s legal scale-code space and
chooses codes by minimizing a Hessian-weighted layer re-
construction loss. At quantization time, SoScale formulates

remains complementary to preprocessing methods such as
block Hadamard rotations (Shao et al. 2025).
We evaluate SoScale on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B (Yang
et al. 2025) across three hierarchical FP4 formats: MXFP4,
NVFP4, and HiF4. Across downstream benchmarks, the best
SoScale variants achieve the highest average score in ev-
ery model-format group, outperforming RTN, GPTQ (Fran-
tar et al. 2023), local ScaleSearch (Gupta et al. 2026), and
BlockRotation (Ashkboos et al. 2024) baselines. On Qwen3-
14B, these variants retain 97.17% of BF16 average accuracy
under MXFP4, 99.20% under NVFP4, and 99.40% under
HiF4. These results indicate that optimizing scale codes with
second-order layer information recovers accuracy that local
tensor-level objectives miss.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify an outlier-induced scale mismatch in hierar-
chical FP4 quantization: default range-preserving scales
can reserve scarce FP4 levels for regions that receive no
assignments, reducing resolution for dense weight values.
• We introduce SoScale, an offline scale-code optimizer
that preserves the target FP4 format, combines Hessian-
weighted layer reconstruction with quantization error
compensation, and retains compatibility with existing
FP4 hardware inference paths.

Related Work
Hierarchical FP4 formats.
Low-bit floating-point formats
reduce LLM inference cost while preserving exponent-based
dynamic range and enabling hardware acceleration. Practi-
cal FP4 formats, including MXFP4 (Rouhani et al. 2023),
NVFP4 (Alvarez et al. 2025), and HiF4 (Luo et al. 2026),
pair a 4-bit payload with block-wise or hierarchical scales;
their main structural differences are summarized in Table 1.
Despite differences in scale hierarchy, granularity, and code-
books, these formats share the same abstraction: the scale
code places the FP4 payload levels on the real-valued axis
for each block. Consequently, scale selection becomes a pri-
mary source of accuracy loss when block distributions are
heavy-tailed or contain outliers.
Post-training quantization methods.
PTQ reduces the
memory and compute cost of pretrained LLMs without ex-
pensive retraining. Simple round-to-nearest (RTN) quanti-
zation is widely used as an efficient baseline, but often de-
grades accuracy at very low bit-widths. Calibration-based
PTQ methods improve low-bit accuracy by using activation
statistics, saliency estimates, or second-order approximations
to preserve layer behavior (Frantar et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2024;
Shao et al. 2024; Zhao and Yuan 2025). A complementary
line smooths, rotates, or rebalances weights and activations
before quantization to reduce outlier-induced difficulty (Xiao


et al. 2023; Ashkboos et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025). A common
lesson is that quantization errors should be evaluated by how
they affect layer outputs, not only by element-wise tensor dis-
tortion. SoScale adopts this layer-centric view, but applies it
to the discrete scale codes of hierarchical FP4 formats.
FP4 methods.
Recent FP4 methods typically handle out-
lier sensitivity through preprocessing, compensation, or scale
adaptation. Block Hadamard rotations shrink each MXFP4
block before quantization, but rely on a downstream scale
rule (Shao et al. 2025). ARCQuant repairs NVFP4 errors
with reordering and residual channels, adding compensa-
tion instead of optimizing scales (Meng et al. 2026). The
closest work adapts scales directly: Four Over Six switches
between two NVFP4 rules (Cook et al. 2026), and Scale-
Search minimizes local mean squared error (MSE) over block
scales (Gupta et al. 2026). These methods establish scale
selection as important, but remain tied to format-specific
rules or local tensor loss. SoScale uses a Hessian-weighted
layer reconstruction loss to search legal scale codes across
MXFP4, NVFP4, and HiF4, with GPTQ recovery.
Methodology
This section presents SoScale, a weight scale-code search
method for hierarchical FP4 quantization. For each linear
layer, SoScale selects legal scale codes with a Hessian-
weighted layer reconstruction objective and formulates
blockwise quantization as a sequential second-order recon-
struction process with error recovery. We first formulate
scale-code search, then describe error recovery, and finally
summarize the full pipeline.

Second-Order Error Recovery
The previous subsection defines how to choose scale codes
for a fixed block of weights. In sequential quantization, how-
ever, committing one block introduces an error that can be
partially absorbed by the remaining unquantized blocks. If
later blocks were searched on the original weights, their scale
choices would ignore this compensation. This coupling is
natural for hierarchical FP4 formats because both scale se-
lection and error recovery operate at the block level. SoScale
therefore follows the GPTQ principle (Frantar et al. 2023):
after each block is quantized, it propagates a second-order
correction to future blocks, and the next scale-code search is
performed on the compensated weights.

Experiments
We evaluate SoScale on Qwen3-family LLMs under W4A4
quantization with three hierarchical FP4 formats: MXFP4,
NVFP4, and HiF4. We report downstream benchmark accu-
racy against representative baselines.
Experimental Setup
Models.
We evaluate SoScale on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-
14B (Yang et al. 2025).
Quantization formats.
We evaluate MXFP4, NVFP4, and
HiF4, the three hierarchical FP4 formats summarized in Ta-
ble 1. Our main setting is W4A4: weights are quantized by
the compared weight quantizer, and activations use the on-
line per-token FP4 rule described in the methodology. We
quantize linear layers in transformer blocks, while keeping
token embeddings, the LM head, and normalization layers
in BF16. We also report a direct-activation setting for scale-
search methods, where activations use direct RTN quantiza-
tion; this isolates the weight-side contribution while keeping
the activation path closer to hardware-native FP4 execution.
Baselines.
We use BF16 as the full-precision reference.
RTN denotes the default format-specific round-to-nearest
FP4 quantizer. GPTQ denotes GPTQ-style second-order
weight quantization (Frantar et al. 2023). Since GPTQ is
originally a weight-only PTQ method, in the main W4A4
comparison we pair its quantized weights with the online
per-token activation rule described above. ScaleSearch de-
notes local block-scale search that minimizes element-wise
MSE (Gupta et al. 2026). BlockRotation denotes the stan-
dalone block Hadamard preprocessing method proposed for
MXFP4 (Shao et al. 2025); in our experiments, we apply the
same preprocessing to MXFP4, NVFP4, and HiF4. In tables,
the suffix w/ Had. denotes applying block Hadamard pre-
processing before the corresponding quantization method,
following QuaRot (Ashkboos et al. 2024) and block rota-
tion (Shao et al. 2025). We use these names consistently in
the result tables.
Calibration and implementation.
For methods that re-
quire calibration, we sample 128 sequences of 2,048 tokens
from the first WikiText shard (Merity et al. 2016), following
a widely used PTQ setup (Frantar et al. 2023). For SoScale,
these calibration activations are used to estimate the empir-
ical Hessian for the Hessian-aware scale-code search. We
load Qwen3 checkpoints and tokenizers from their public
Hugging Face repositories, use datasets (Lhoest et al.
2021) and transformers (Wolf et al. 2020) for data pre-
processing and model execution, and implement all quanti-
zation routines in PyTorch (Paszke et al. 2019).
Evaluation benchmarks.
We evaluate quantized models
using the Language Model Evaluation Harness (Gao et al.
2024) with batch size 64 on GSM8K (Cobbe et al. 2021),
HellaSwag (Zellers et al. 2019), MMLU (Hendrycks et al.
2021), ARC-Challenge and ARC-Easy (Clark et al. 2018),
BoolQ (Clark et al. 2019), and PIQA (Bisk et al. 2020).
We report exact-match accuracy for GSM8K and accuracy
for the remaining tasks. Unless otherwise noted, GSM8K is
evaluated in a 5-shot setting and the other benchmarks are
evaluated in a zero-shot setting. We report the average score
across tasks and its relative ratio to the BF16 reference.

Ablating search and recovery.
Table 4 compares MSE
scale search, MSE search with blockwise recovery, and full
SoScale on Qwen3-8B under fixed FP4 formats. Adding re-
covery to the MSE objective gives mixed gains over Scale-
Search: it is marginal under MXFP4, decreases NVFP4, and
improves HiF4. In contrast, full SoScale is best for all three
formats, indicating that recovery is most effective when cou-
pled with Hessian-aware scale-code selection rather than a
local MSE objective.
Conclusion
We presented SoScale, a second-order scale-code search
method for hierarchical FP4 quantization. SoScale addresses
a practical failure mode of low-bit floating-point formats:
range-preserving or locally MSE-optimal scales can place
the FP4 grid poorly for layer outputs, especially when outliers
interact with discrete scale codes. By optimizing hardware-
valid weight scale codes under a Hessian-aware layer recon-
struction objective and interleaving this search with GPTQ-
style error recovery, SoScale improves W4A4 quantization
without changing the target FP4 representation. Experiments
on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B across MXFP4, NVFP4, and
HiF4 show consistent gains over RTN, GPTQ, local Scale-
Search, and BlockRotation baselines, with the largest head-
room under MXFP4 and persistent gains for stronger formats.
The direct-activation setting further shows that SoScale can
improve weight quantization while keeping the activation
path simple and hardware aligned. These results suggest that
scale-code selection should be treated as a layer-aware op-
timization problem, rather than merely a local tensor-fitting
rule, for accurate low-bit floating-point LLM inference.
