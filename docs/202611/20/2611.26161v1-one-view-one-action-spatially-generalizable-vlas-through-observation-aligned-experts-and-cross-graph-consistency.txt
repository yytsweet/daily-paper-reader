Abstract
Multi-view vision-language-action (VLA) models still face
challenges in spatial generalization, particularly under
changes in camera viewpoints and robot initial states.
These models typically receive action supervision only in
the robot base frame, requiring them to implicitly learn
the spatial correspondence between observations from each
view and base-frame actions. We propose One View, One
Action (OVOA-VLA), which learns to predict actions in
each view’s camera frame, allowing the same physical mo-
tion to provide diverse, observation-aligned supervision
across views. OVOA-VLA jointly trains a base-frame ex-
pert and view-specific action experts, whose robot-state
inputs and action targets are expressed in their respec-
tive camera frames.
Two complementary directed atten-
tion graphs model both directions of interaction between
the base and view-specific experts, jointly leveraging view-
specific action supervision to improve base-frame predic-
tion. Cross Graph Consistency further encourages agree-
ment between the two graphs’ base-frame predictions for
the same sample.
With the same multi-view inputs, the
model supports both full and base-only inference.
Ex-
periments across multiple simulation benchmarks demon-
strate improved manipulation performance and robustness
to camera-viewpoint and robot-initialization perturbations.
Full and base-only inference achieve average success rates
of 88.1% and 85.7% on LIBERO-Plus, respectively. In real-
world evaluations, base-only inference raises the average
success rates from π0.5’s 28.3% to 58.3% across three tasks
under spatial distribution shifts.
1. Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models transfer visual and
semantic knowledge from large-scale pretrained vision-
language models to robotic control, achieving substantial

†Corresponding author
progress in language-conditioned manipulation [3–5, 19,
31, 34, 46]. However, reliable manipulation also requires
spatial generalization: the ability to generate appropriate
actions across changes in camera viewpoints and robot ini-
tial states. Viewpoint shifts alter the visual appearance of
scenes and robot motion [23], whereas variations in the
robot’s state may require different motion trajectories to ac-
complish the same task. Yet existing benchmarks reveal
substantial performance degradation under both types of
shifts, highlighting the persistent challenge of spatial gen-
eralization [9, 11, 29, 32, 45].
Action representation provides a useful perspective on
this spatial generalization challenge.
The same physical
robot motion can have different numerical representations
across coordinate frames [22, 42, 43]. Figure 1(a) illustrates
this for a single end-effector movement: its displacement
components differ when expressed in the robot base coor-
dinates and in each camera’s coordinates. Yet multi-view
VLAs typically combine observations from multiple cam-
eras while receiving action supervision only in the robot
base frame [18], requiring them to implicitly learn the spa-
tial correspondence between observations from each view
and base-frame actions [16, 42]. Consequently, the learned
correspondence may become tied to spatial configurations
encountered during training, limiting generalization to un-
seen camera viewpoints and robot initial states [12, 36,
39].
Prior work has explored camera-frame action pre-
diction [22, 42] and explicit camera-geometry condition-
ing [17] to improve this spatial correspondence. These stud-
ies highlight the importance of reference-frame choice and
camera geometry in action learning. This motivates jointly
leveraging action supervision from all available views to
improve the spatial generalization of the base-frame policy.
As shown in Figure 1(b), we introduce camera-frame ac-
tion supervision for each view while retaining the original
base-frame supervision.
We introduce One View, One Action (OVOA-VLA), a
framework for learning observation-aligned actions. Its or-
ganizing principle is to predict actions expressed in the
corresponding camera frame for each view.

We instantiate OVOA-VLA with a Mixture of Trans-
formers [25], comprising a view-specific action expert for
each input view and a base-frame expert.
Each view-
specific expert directly attends to visual tokens from its cor-
responding view, receives robot state expressed in that cam-
era frame, and predicts actions in the same frame. The base
expert uses all visual inputs and the base-frame robot state,
while retaining the original base-frame action supervision.
We jointly train these experts under two complementary di-
rected attention graphs. In the base-to-reference graph, the
view-specific experts attend to action features from the base
expert; in the reference-to-base graph, the base expert at-
tends to action features from the view-specific experts. Dur-
ing training, we perform a forward pass under each graph
for the same sample, with both passes sharing model param-
eters and receiving demonstrated-action supervision. Since
both base-frame predictions correspond to the same action
target, we introduce Cross Graph Consistency (CGC) to en-
courage agreement between them. Our model supports two
inference modes: full inference uses the reference-to-base
graph, while base-only inference uses the base-to-reference
graph, where the base expert does not attend to the view-
specific experts and thus permits their removal.
OVOA-VLA demonstrates strong performance across
multiple simulation benchmarks [11, 26, 28, 41]. Full infer-
ence achieves average success rates of 98.7% and 88.1% on
LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. On LIBERO-Plus, it improves
success rates from 66.2% to 75.4% under camera view-
point perturbations and from 67.2% to 88.4% under robot
initialization perturbations, compared with π0.5. Base-only
inference achieves 85.7% on LIBERO-Plus, demonstrating
that the gains persist without view-specific experts. In real-
world evaluations on a Franka Research 3 robot, base-only
inference outperforms π0.5 by 5.0 percentage points in av-
erage success rate across six in-distribution tasks and raises
the average success rate from 28.3% to 58.3% across three
tasks under spatial distribution shifts. Our main contribu-
tions are as follows:
• Observation-aligned action learning.
We introduce
OVOA-VLA, which jointly learns camera-frame actions
from diverse, observation-aligned supervision derived
from the same demonstration, improving base-frame pol-
icy generalization.
• Complementary expert training. We design two com-
plementary directed attention graphs to model both inter-
action directions between the base and view-specific ex-
perts, with CGC encouraging consistent base-frame pre-
dictions across graphs for the same sample.
• Two inference modes. Our model supports full inference
with the reference-to-base graph and base-only inference
with the base-to-reference graph, where the base expert is
independent of the view-specific experts.
• Experimental validation. Experiments across four sim-
ulation benchmarks and six real-world tasks demonstrate
improved manipulation performance and robustness to
camera-viewpoint and robot-initialization shifts.

These models have demonstrated

A major design choice in
VLAs lies in how actions are encoded and generated,
ranging from autoregressive prediction of discretized action
tokens [19, 31, 46] to continuous action generation with
diffusion [21] or flow-matching objectives [5].
Another
important consideration is the reference frame in which
actions are expressed. Many VLAs predict joint-space ac-
tions or Cartesian actions expressed in robot-base or world
frames rather than the input camera frames [42], leaving the
geometric correspondence between camera-centered visual
observations and action targets to be learned implicitly.
Spatial Generalization in Vision-Language-Action Mod-
els
Existing approaches to spatial generalization can be
broadly grouped by their focus on observations, spatial per-
ception, or action prediction. VISTA augments demonstra-
tions with synthesized camera views, while Cai et al. study
multi-view demonstrations and generate additional views
using RoboNVS [7, 37]. AnyCamVLA instead adapts test-
time observations toward the training camera configuration
without policy retraining [13]. For spatial perception, Spa-
tial Forcing aligns visual features with pretrained 3D rep-
resentations, QDepth-VLA introduces quantized depth pre-
diction as auxiliary supervision, and ActiveVLA actively
selects viewpoints and zooms into critical regions [20, 24,
27]. For action prediction, OC-VLA predicts camera-frame
actions using camera extrinsics, whereas CamVLA jointly
predicts camera-frame actions and hand-eye transforma-
tions for calibration-free control [22, 42]. OC-VLA++ in-
troduces geometry-guided cross-view action equivariance,
while Huang et al. enforce consistency between flow veloc-
ity predictions from action-equivalent views [14]. Qwen-
RobotManip further adopts camera-frame EEF deltas with
camera positional encoding [40]. Our work is most closely
related to action-level approaches, introducing observation-
aligned action supervision as auxiliary prediction tasks for
individual views while retaining the original base-frame ac-
tion supervision.

3.3. Action Expert Interaction and Consistency
Complementary Attention Graphs.
To enable the view-
specific action prediction tasks to contribute to learning the
base-frame policy, we introduce two complementary di-
rected attention graphs that control cross-expert token visi-
bility within the MoT self-attention layers. The two graphs
share model parameters but use different attention masks
to separately model the two directions of interaction be-
tween the base-frame and view-specific experts, rather than
coupling them within a single bidirectional attention graph.
Here, B denotes the base-frame action expert, while R col-
lectively denotes the view-specific experts associated with
the camera reference frames, with arrows indicating the di-
rection of information flow. In the base-to-reference graph
(B →R), the view-specific action tokens attend to the base-
frame action tokens, while reverse attention is blocked. This
allows the view-specific action losses to update the base ex-
pert through the attention connections, while keeping base-
frame predictions independent of the view-specific action
features. In the reference-to-base graph (R →B), the
attention direction is reversed, allowing the base-frame ac-
tion tokens to attend to the view-specific action features and
leverage them for base-frame action prediction.

Inference Modes.
Our model supports two inference
modes: Full Multi-Expert Inference and Base-only Infer-
ence, both using the base-frame action for robot execution.
In Full Multi-Expert Inference, we adopt the reference-to-
base graph (R →B), allowing base-frame action tokens
to attend to view-specific action tokens and thus requiring
all action experts for prediction. In contrast, the base-to-
reference graph (B →R) keeps base-frame predictions in-
dependent of view-specific action-token features, enabling
Base-only Inference with only the base-frame action expert
while omitting the auxiliary view-specific experts. The se-
lected inference mode remains fixed throughout the entire
rollout. Base-only Inference therefore maintains inference
cost comparable to the baseline with a single action ex-
pert, while Full Multi-Expert Inference leverages additional
action features from the view-specific experts, providing a
flexible trade-off between performance and computation.
4. Simulation Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Simulation Benchmarks.
We evaluate our method on
four simulation benchmarks to assess both spatial gen-
eralization and broader manipulation capabilities:
(i)
LIBERO [26] comprises four multi-task manipulation
suites; (ii) LIBERO-Plus [11] extends LIBERO with con-
trolled perturbations for evaluating out-of-distribution ro-
bustness, and we evaluate the LIBERO-trained model in
a zero-shot manner; (iii) VLABench [41] is a large-scale
benchmark for language-conditioned robotic manipulation,
where we use Track 1 to evaluate downstream task learn-
ing and skill acquisition under in-distribution settings; and
(iv) MimicGen [28] provides diverse manipulation tasks
spanning long-horizon and high-precision behaviors, from
which we select ten Panda D0 tasks to evaluate multi-task
manipulation performance.
Baselines.
On LIBERO [26] and LIBERO-Plus [11], we
compare against representative VLA baselines listed in Ta-
ble 1. On VLABench [41], we use our reproduced π0.5 [4]
as the primary baseline and include results reported in
the original benchmark paper for reference.
On Mimic-
Gen [28], we compare against our reproduced π0.5 [4] under
the same experimental protocol.
Implementation Details.
We initialize our model from a
pretrained π0.5 [4] checkpoint and initialize all action ex-
perts with the weights of the original π0.5 action expert. We
train a separate OVOA-VLA model for each benchmark for
30,000 steps, using a batch size of 32 on two NVIDIA RTX
PRO 6000D GPUs. At inference time, the policy predicts
10-step action chunks and replans after executing the first
five actions. Additional preprocessing and augmentation
details are provided in Appendix C.2.

5. Real-World Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup.
We conduct real-world experiments on a 7-DoF Franka Re-
search 3 (FR3) robot. The policy takes RGB images from
a fixed third-person camera and a wrist-mounted camera,
together with a task instruction, and predicts 6-DoF end-
effector (EEF) target poses relative to the observation-time
EEF pose, along with continuous gripper-closedness val-
ues. The evaluation includes three short-horizon tasks and
three long-horizon tasks, covering basic manipulation skills
and multi-stage manipulation, respectively. For each task,
we collect 50 demonstration trajectories using GELLO tele-
operation. During data collection, object initial positions
are randomized within predefined ranges, while the third-
person camera remains fixed. Demonstrations from all tasks
are pooled to jointly post-train a single multi-task policy.
For all real-world evaluations, we use Base-only Inference,
which activates only the base-frame action expert, result-
ing in a single-expert inference setting comparable to that
of π0.5. Each method is evaluated over 20 rollouts per task,
and we report task success rates. Detailed task definitions,
hardware configurations, camera calibration, and training

5.3. Robustness under Spatial Distribution Shifts
To further evaluate real-world spatial robustness, we select
three tasks and consider two types of spatial distribution
shifts. For Camera Viewpoint Shift, we evaluate two un-
seen fixed-camera views while retaining the same wrist-
mounted camera.
For Robot Initialization Shift, we de-
fine two perturbed initial EEF poses while keeping all other
conditions unchanged. For each task and each configura-
tion, we conduct five real-world rollouts. All OOD experi-
ments are evaluated zero-shot without retraining or adapta-
tion, and detailed task selection, perturbation settings, and
shift-specific results are provided in Appendix D.3. Figure 4
shows that OVOA-VLA outperforms π0.5 on all three tasks,
increasing the overall success rate from 28.3% to 58.3%.
This consistent improvement demonstrates stronger zero-
shot robustness to spatial perturbations.
6. Conclusion
We introduce OVOA-VLA, a framework that leverages
observation-aligned action supervision across multiple ref-
erence frames to improve spatial generalization in VLA
policies. By combining view-specific action experts with
Complementary Attention Graphs and Cross Graph Con-
sistency, our method enables action prediction tasks in
different reference frames to jointly contribute to learn-
ing the executable base-frame policy. Experiments across
four simulation benchmarks and six real-world manipula-
tion tasks demonstrate improved manipulation performance
8


and stronger robustness to camera-viewpoint and robot-
initialization shifts. Notably, Base-only Inference retains
performance gains without the view-specific action experts,
while maintaining inference cost comparable to the baseline
on LIBERO. These results highlight the value of leveraging
multiple views not only as visual observations but also as
sources of observation-aligned action supervision.

A. Additional Related Work
Mixture-of-Transformers for Multimodal Modeling
Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) employs modality-specific Trans-
former parameters while maintaining global self-attention over a joint token sequence, enabling specialized computation
alongside cross-modal interaction [25]. This architecture has been applied across diverse multimodal tasks. BAGEL em-
ploys separate experts for multimodal understanding and generation [10], while Mural connects a frozen LLM with an image
diffusion model for text-to-image generation [15]. Video-As-Prompt introduces a trainable MoT expert to guide a frozen
video diffusion model for controllable video generation [2]. In robotics, π0 [5] and π0.5 [4] couple a VLM with a sepa-
rately parameterized action expert, while Motus integrates visual-understanding, video-generation, and action experts within
a three-expert MoT architecture [1]. In our work, we adopt MoT to accommodate multiple reference-frame action experts
and their attention-based interactions.
B. Action Representation Details
Table 6 summarizes the proprioceptive states and action representations used across platforms. LIBERO and MimicGen retain
the native seven-dimensional incremental control commands, comprising three translational components, three rotational
components in rotation-vector form, and one gripper command, with the original controller scales preserved. For VLABench
and real-world Franka, absolute target poses are encoded relative to the end-effector pose observed at the start of the action
chunk. VLABench uses position differences and component-wise XYZ Euler-angle differences, whereas Franka uses position
differences and a rotation-vector representation of the relative orientation. All targets within a chunk share this initial pose as
their reference. Gripper components retain their platform-specific definitions; for Franka, gripper closedness ranges from 0
(fully open) to 1 (fully closed). Actions are executed in the robot base frame on all platforms. For LIBERO and MimicGen,
denormalized predictions are passed directly to the environment; for VLABench and Franka, predictions are first decoded
into absolute target poses and then passed to the respective execution interfaces.
When constructing wrist-frame action supervision, we adopt temporal reference conventions consistent with each plat-
form’s action definition. For LIBERO and MimicGen, each action in a chunk is transformed using the wrist-camera extrinsics
at its corresponding timestep. For VLABench and real-world Franka, the entire chunk uses the wrist-camera extrinsics at the
initial observation timestep, which are updated upon replanning.

C. Additional Details of Simulation Experiments
C.1. Benchmark and Dataset Details
LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus.
The four task suites in LIBERO [26] emphasize spatial relationships, manipulated objects,
task goals, and long-horizon manipulation, respectively, providing a common environment for evaluating policies across
different task requirements. We use all 40 tasks from the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites and jointly train a single
multi-task policy on the publicly released LIBERO demonstrations, with 50 trajectories per task and 2,000 trajectories in
total. The original images have a resolution of 128 × 128, and we do not filter out failed trajectories before training. Each
12


task is evaluated over 50 rollouts. LIBERO-Plus [11] organizes perturbations into dimensions, enabling separate assessments
of policy sensitivity to different environmental changes. We evaluate all seven official perturbation dimensions, following the
official evaluation configurations and rollout counts. All LIBERO-Plus evaluations are conducted zero-shot using the same
LIBERO-trained model, without training or adaptation on any perturbed data.
VLABench.
VLABench [41] is a benchmark for language-conditioned robotic manipulation, with multiple evaluation
tracks assessing task learning and generalization. We adopt the in-distribution setting of Track 1, which evaluates the ability
of policies to learn downstream tasks and acquire manipulation skills from demonstrations. We use the 10 primitive tasks in
the fine-tuning setup, with 500 demonstration trajectories per task and 5,000 trajectories in total, and jointly train a single
multi-task policy across all tasks. Visual observations comprise two fixed camera views and one wrist-mounted camera view,
each with a resolution of 480×480. The robot state includes the end-effector position and orientation, along with the gripper
state. Evaluation follows the official Track 1 task configurations, with 50 rollouts per task. We report success rate (SR),
progress score (PS), and intention score (IS), averaged over the 10 tasks. SR measures overall task completion, PS measures
execution progress as the proportion of completed subtasks, and IS reflects the tendency to select the correct target object
based on whether the end effector approaches it. We treat SR as the primary evaluation metric, while PS and IS provide
complementary measures for understanding task progress and target selection.
MimicGen.
MimicGen [28] is a framework for generating robotic manipulation demonstrations by transforming and adapt-
ing existing trajectories to different tasks and scenes. Its tasks include both long-horizon, multi-stage manipulation and
precision-demanding assembly. For example, Coffee Preparation involves multiple stages, whereas Threading requires pre-
cise alignment and insertion. We select 10 D0 tasks using the Franka Emika Panda robot from the publicly released core
dataset: Stack, Square, Coffee, Threading, Stack Three, Three Piece Assembly, Hammer Cleanup, Mug Cleanup, Coffee
Preparation, and Kitchen. D0 denotes the default object initialization distribution, which we use for both training and evalu-
ation. We jointly train a single multi-task policy using 1,000 demonstration trajectories per task, totaling 10,000 trajectories.
Observations include third-person and wrist-camera images rendered at 224 × 224 resolution, together with the robot state.
We establish a unified evaluation protocol for these tasks, with 100 rollouts per task and the same pregenerated, fixed sim-
ulator initial states across all methods. We use the environments’ native success conditions and terminate each rollout upon
success or when the maximum episode length is reached. The maximum episode length is 400 steps for Stack, Square, Cof-
fee, Threading, and Stack Three; 500 steps for Three Piece Assembly, Hammer Cleanup, and Mug Cleanup; and 800 steps
for Coffee Preparation and Kitchen.
C.2. Training and Implementation Details
Training Configuration.
We use the AdamW optimizer with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, a weight decay of 10−10, and a global
gradient norm clipping threshold of 1.0. For LIBERO and MimicGen, the learning rate is linearly warmed up to 5×10−5 over
the first 10,000 updates and remains constant thereafter. For VLABench, the learning rate is linearly warmed up to 2.5×10−5
over the first 1,000 updates, followed by cosine decay to 2.5 × 10−6. All images are resized to 224 × 224 before being fed
into the model. During training, we apply random color jitter to images from all camera views, varying brightness, contrast,
and saturation to diversify visual appearance and reduce reliance on fixed lighting and color conditions. The corresponding
augmentation strengths are set to 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5, respectively. For the third-person views in LIBERO and MimicGen and
the primary fixed-camera view in VLABench, we additionally apply random cropping and in-plane rotation. The crop spans
95% of the input width and height and is resized back to the original input dimensions, while the rotation angle is sampled
from [−5◦, 5◦]. These random augmentations are enabled only during training. We train the π0.5 baseline on all three datasets
using the same training data, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and image preprocessing and augmentation settings as our
method.
Software and Simulation Environments.
We use JAX/Flax for model training and inference, with separate software
environments for each simulation benchmark. LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus both use MuJoCo 3.2.3, paired with robosuite
1.4.1 and 1.4.0, respectively. VLABench uses MuJoCo 3.2.2. The MimicGen environment uses MimicGen 1.0.1, robosuite
1.4.1, robomimic 0.3.1, and MuJoCo 2.3.2.

C.4. Analysis of Full and Base-only Inference under Camera and Robot Perturbations
As shown in Table 7, Base-only Inference outperforms Full Multi-Expert Inference under Camera perturbations (78.4%
vs. 75.4%), whereas Full Multi-Expert Inference performs better under Robot perturbations (88.4% vs. 81.2%). To further
analyze this difference, we execute camera-frame and wrist-frame actions under the B →R and R →B attention graphs.
As shown in Table 8, wrist-frame actions consistently outperform camera-frame actions under Camera perturbations for both
attention graphs, while the opposite trend is observed under Robot perturbations. Camera perturbations alter the observation–
action correspondence associated with the fixed camera, whereas the wrist frame maintains a fixed relationship with the robot
end effector, making wrist-frame action predictions relatively stable. Robot perturbations change the robot’s initial state
and the action trajectory required to complete the task, which also changes the wrist-camera observation. In contrast, the
fixed camera continues to provide stable global visual information, making camera-frame action predictions relatively stable.
Consequently, the two perturbation types affect the action reference frames differently, reversing the relative advantage of
camera-frame and wrist-frame actions.
These results further explain the performance difference between the two inference modes. In the R →B graph used by
Full Multi-Expert Inference, the Base expert attends to action features from the camera and wrist experts and uses them for
base-frame action prediction. Under Camera perturbations, the success rate of camera-frame actions decreases substantially,
and the useful information provided by the wrist expert is insufficient to fully compensate for the degradation of the camera
expert, limiting the overall benefit of multi-expert interaction. Under Robot perturbations, camera-frame actions maintain a
high success rate, indicating that the camera expert provides reliable motion information. In contrast, Base-only Inference
14


uses the B →R graph, in which the Base expert retains all visual inputs while its action prediction remains independent
of view-specific action features. This allows Base-only Inference to avoid the influence of the camera expert under Camera
perturbations, but it also does not directly use the additional motion information provided by the camera expert under Robot
perturbations, leading to the different behavior of the two inference modes across the two settings. The same reversal between
camera-frame and wrist-frame actions appears under both attention graphs, further indicating that this difference arises from
how the action reference frames adapt to Camera and Robot perturbations.

Training and Execution.
Our method and the reproduced π0.5 baseline are trained on the same pooled multi-task demon-
stration dataset using the corresponding language task instructions, and share the same main training hyperparameters. Both
models are trained for 30K steps with a global batch size of 64 using AdamW. The learning rate is warmed up to 2.5 × 10−5
over the first 1,000 steps, followed by cosine decay toward 2.5 × 10−6. Demonstration trajectories are sampled at 30 Hz for
training. Both policies predict 7-D Cartesian actions consisting of a 3-D translational offset, a 3-D world-relative rotation
vector, and a continuous target gripper-closedness value in [0, 1]. At each inference step, the policy predicts a 10-step ac-
tion chunk, with all actions decoded relative to the end-effector pose observed at the beginning of the chunk. The first five
actions are executed before acquiring new observations and replanning. The continuous gripper prediction is converted into
open/close commands using hysteresis: values at or below 0.3 trigger opening, values at or above 0.7 trigger closing, and
intermediate values retain the previous gripper state.
