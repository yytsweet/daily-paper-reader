MARS: Dynamic Routing of
Reinforcement-Learned Atomic Skills for
Multi-Stage Robotic Manipulation
Zequan Zeng1, Junqin Yang1, Kai Wang1,*
Abstract—Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demon-
strated strong capabilities in multi-task robotic manipulation.
However, they still face limitations in control precision and
execution stability during critical manipulation stages, such as
object grasping, alignment and insertion. To combine general-
purpose manipulation capabilities with task-specific fine-grained
control, while enabling effective coordination between generalist
and specialized policies throughout multi-stage manipulation
tasks, this paper proposes MARS (Modular Atomic-skill Rout-
ing System), a dynamic routing framework for reinforcement
learning-based atomic skills. MARS integrates a frozen generalist
VLA policy with multiple independently trained atomic skill
policies. Using a shared RL Token state representation and
the reference action of VLA, specialized control policies are
optimized through reinforcement learning for different cr‘itical
manipulation stages. For high-level policy selection, we develop
a dynamic router that integrates temporal context and skill
semantics. The router employs Mamba-based recurrent encoding
and a historical feature window to capture state evolution during
manipulation. Skill descriptions of candidate policies are encoded
into semantic queries, and attention mechanisms are used to
establish correspondences between skill semantics and temporal
state features. Experimental results show that MARS achieves
an average Macro-F1 score of 90.47% across four LIBERO
task suites under fixed-trajectory evaluation, outperforming an
MLP-based router by 7.08 percentage points. In the simulated
Tube Transport task, the full-task success rate increases from
20.04% to 85.67%. These results demonstrate that stage-specific
reinforcement learning and temporal state-aware dynamic policy
routing can improve multi-policy coordination and overall task
execution performance in multi-stage robotic manipulation.

I. INTRODUCTION
Robotic manipulation tasks, such as multi-object pick-and-
place, object transfer, and laboratory sample handling, typ-
ically involve a sequence of operations, including grasping,
transportation, alignment, placement, and insertion. These
stages impose different requirements on the underlying con-
trol policy. General-purpose policies are often sufficient for
relatively simple operations, such as object transportation and
robot resetting, whereas alignment and insertion require higher
control precision and execution stability. Consequently, relying
on a single control policy throughout an entire manipulation
task may make it difficult to simultaneously achieve general-
purpose manipulation capabilities and precise control dur-

ing critical stages. Combining pretrained generalist policies
with skill-specific control policies optimized for particular
manipulation requirements provides a promising approach to
improving multi-stage robotic manipulation. However, differ-
ent policies have distinct operating conditions and applicable
ranges. Selecting an appropriate policy according to the robot
state and task progress, while ensuring smooth transitions
between policies, remains an important challenge in multi-
policy coordination [1], [2].
In recent years, generalist robotic policies, including RT-
1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, π0, π0.5, and SmolVLA, have
demonstrated promising multi-task manipulation capabilities
through large-scale robot data pretraining and joint modeling
of visual, language and action information [3]–[10]. Neverthe-
less, the performance of these generalist policies varies across
manipulation stages, particularly in tasks involving precise
alignment and contact-constrained operations, where action
accuracy and execution stability remain challenging [11], [12].
To further improve robotic manipulation performance, recent
studies have investigated reinforcement learning (RL)-based
post-training methods for VLA policies. One line of research
improves manipulation performance by optimizing the action
generation process or updating selected model parameters, as
demonstrated by VLA-RL, SimpleVLA-RL, iRe-VLA, and
π0.6 [13]–[16]. Another line of research aims to preserve the
core capabilities of pretrained models while enhancing task-
specific control through lightweight control modules, residual
policies, or independently trained RL policies. Representative
methods include RL Token (RLT), PARTS, Object-Centric
Residual RL, and VLaRL [12], [17]–[19]. These studies have
demonstrated the effectiveness of reinforcement learning in
improving robotic manipulation from different perspectives
and have provided a foundation for integrating pretrained
generalist policies with specialized control strategies.
Although reinforcement learning can improve the control
performance of VLA models in specific manipulation tasks,
optimizing a single policy for an entire multi-stage task
remains challenging. When only a binary terminal reward
indicating full-task success or failure is available, the policy
receives no reward feedback for intermediate actions that
contribute positively to task progress. This makes it difficult
to accurately estimate the contribution of actions at different
stages to the final task outcome, thereby complicating state-
action value estimation and policy optimization. One possible
solution is to decompose critical stages with clearly defined
manipulation objectives into independent atomic skills and

By assigning local objectives and reward signals to individual
atomic skills, specialized policies can be trained to address
specific control requirements while reducing their dependence
on full-task rewards. However, independently trained atomic
skills only improve control performance within their respective
stages. Determining when to activate each skill and how to co-
ordinate transitions between specialized policies and the gen-
eralist VLA remains a key challenge for multi-policy coordina-
tion. Several studies have explored skill organization and dy-
namic routing mechanisms from different perspectives. Atom-
icVLA organizes manipulation capabilities through atomic
skill modeling and an expert-based architecture, SMoDP
introduces semantically structured policy specialization for
compositional robotic manipulation, and SWAP enables dy-
namic selection among multiple VLA policies through policy
value estimation [20]–[22]. These approaches offer different
solutions for atomic skill modeling, policy specialization, and
dynamic selection. Nevertheless, for a system consisting of
a frozen generalist VLA and multiple independently trained
RL-based atomic skill policies, determining which policy is
most appropriate at different stages of task execution remains a
key challenge. Since individual policies have different control
objectives and operating ranges, atomic skill selection should
not rely solely on predefined manipulation stages but should
also account for state changes throughout the sequential exe-
cution process. Meanwhile, descriptions of candidate policies
provide policy-specific semantic information that can help
the router learn correspondences between candidate skills and
temporal state contexts. Motivated by these considerations, we
formulate dynamic policy routing from two complementary
perspectives: temporal state modeling and candidate policy
matching. Specifically, temporal encoding is used to extract
contextual information from sequential robot states, while skill
descriptions are transformed into conditioned semantic queries
that interact with temporal state features. This enables the
router to construct policy-specific matching representations
and provides a basis for dynamically selecting between the
generalist VLA and reinforcement-learned atomic skill ex-
perts.
To address these challenges, we propose MARS (Modular
Atomic-skill Routing System), a dynamic routing framework
for reinforcement learning-based atomic skills in multi-stage
robotic manipulation. MARS decouples local skill optimiza-

tion from high-level policy selection, enabling coordinated
control between a generalist VLA and multiple specialized
atomic skill policies. For atomic skill optimization, we first
construct a shared RL Token adapter based on intermediate
features extracted from the frozen VLA backbone. The adapter
produces compact state representations that can be reused
across different skill policies. Each atomic skill policy then
takes the shared state representation and reference action
chunks of the frozen VLA as inputs and is independently
optimized through reinforcement learning for a specific local
manipulation objective. For high-level policy selection, we
develop a dynamic router that integrates temporal context and
skill semantics. The router employs a Mamba-base recurrent
encoder [23] to capture temporal information from robot states
and maintains a fixed-length historical feature window to pre-
serve recent state changes. Meanwhile, the skill descriptions
of candidate policies are encoded into semantic queries, which
are further refined through self-attention to capture inter-skill
relationships. Cross-attention is then applied to extract skill-
relevant information from historical state features, providing
contextual representations for subsequent policy selection. The
skill-conditioned features are then used to predict routing
probabilities for the generalist VLA and each atomic skill
policy.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows:
1) We propose MARS, a multi-policy coordination frame-
work that integrates a frozen generalist VLA with
independently optimized reinforcement learning-based
atomic skill policies using shared RL Token representa-
tions and VLA reference actions.
2) We develop a dynamic policy router that combines
Mamba-based temporal modeling and skill-conditioned
attention to select appropriate control policies based on
temporal state information and candidate skill semantics.
3) We evaluate MARS through simulation and real-world
robotic experiments, demonstrating its effectiveness in
atomic skill execution, dynamic policy routing, and full-
task manipulation performance.
II. METHOD
Multi-stage robotic manipulation involves operations with
different control requirements, requiring both general-purpose
manipulation capabilities and specialized control during crit-
ical stages. To leverage the complementary strengths of gen-
eralist and specialized policies, we propose MARS, a multi-
policy coordination framework that decouples local atomic
skill optimization from high-level policy selection. In this
framework, a frozen generalist VLA handles basic manipu-
lation and provides reference actions for atomic skill learning,
while multiple atomic skill policies undergo independent rein-
forcement learning optimization for specific manipulation ob-
jectives. A high-level dynamic router then selects appropriate
control policies based on robot state information and candidate
skill semantics, as illustrated in Figure 1.

C. Atomic Skill Policy Learning with Shared RL Token Rep-
resentations and Reference Action Constraints
To improve control performance during critical manipu-
lation stages, we train specialized atomic skill policies for
different local manipulation objectives using reinforcement
learning. Each policy is independently optimized using the
Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)
algorithm [24], with actions predicted by the VLA backbone
serving as references. This design allows the skill policies
to refine their control behaviors for specific manipulation
requirements while leveraging the action prior provided by
the pretrained VLA.

the shared RL Token encoder remain frozen, and only the

D. Dynamic Policy Routing with Temporal Context and Skill
Semantics
Through independent reinforcement learning optimization,
individual atomic skill policies acquire specialized control
capabilities for different manipulation objectives. However,
completing multi-stage manipulation tasks requires effective
coordination between the VLA backbone and these specialized
policies. To achieve this, the system must select appropriate
control policies based on the robot’s current state and historical
context throughout task execution. Accordingly, we develop a
dynamic router that integrates temporal state information and
candidate skill semantics to support adaptive policy selection,
as illustrated in Figure 2.
1) Temporal Context Modeling: During sequential robotic
manipulation, the suitability of a candidate policy depends not
only on the current observation but also on information from
preceding states. Therefore, we introduce a causal temporal
modeling module into the routing network to incorporate
historical context into policy selection. This module consists of
a Mamba-based recurrent encoder and a fixed-length historical
feature window. The former updates the temporal state repre-
sentation, while the latter provides recent contextual features
for subsequent skill-conditioned queries.

specialized action chunk. This mechanism coordinates policy
switching with action execution, enabling continuous closed-
loop control during multi-stage manipulation.

III. EXPERIMENTS
To evaluate the effectiveness of atomic skill optimization
and dynamic policy routing in MARS, we conduct experiments
on the LIBERO benchmark, a custom Tube Transport simu-
lation environment, and a real robotic platform. These exper-
iments assess local skill execution, skill routing performance,
and full-task closed-loop execution, while further examining
the applicability of MARS to real-world robotic manipulation.
We organize our evaluation around four research questions:
Q1: Does reinforcement learning improve the execution
performance of individual atomic skills? We compare the
success rates of the frozen VLA and corresponding atomic
skill policies under identical stage-specific initial conditions to
assess the benefits of optimizing local manipulation objectives.
Q2: How do temporal context modeling and skill-semantic
queries affect routing performance? We compare different
routing architectures on identical fixed test trajectories and
conduct component ablations to examine their effects on
policy-label prediction.
Q3: Can atomic skill policies and dynamic routing improve
closed-loop full-task execution? We compare the frozen
VLA, a monolithic full-task RL policy, and multi-policy
systems with different routing configurations to evaluate their
combined contributions.
Q4: Can MARS achieve reliable closed-loop execution of
the Tube Transport task on a real robot? We evaluate
multi-tube sequential transfer on a physical robot using full-
task success rate and sample completion rate, to assess the
deployability of the proposed framework.
A. Experimental Setup
1) Platforms and Task Configurations: We conduct exper-
iments on the LIBERO benchmark, a custom Tube Transport
simulation environment, and a physical robot platform to
evaluate MARS under different manipulation conditions. We
use a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory
for model training and evaluation.
Simulation tasks. LIBERO [26], built on robosuite [27],
consists of four task suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long,
covering diverse manipulation scenarios involving spatial re-
lationships, objects, task goals, and sequential operations. We
further develop a Tube Transport simulation environment in
which the robot sequentially grasps three test tubes, transports
them to designated slots in a target rack, and completes
insertion before returning to its initial pose, as shown in Fig. 3.
Real-robot platform. Our real-world experimental platform
consists of an AgileX piper robotic arm equipped with a
parallel gripper and an Intel RealSense D435i camera (RGB,
640 × 480 at 30 Hz). The real-robot task follows the same
manipulation sequence as Tube Transport in simulation. With
fixed source and target racks and camera placement, the robot
sequentially grasps, transfers, aligns, inserts, and releases three
test tubes in the specified order before returning to its initial
pose.
2) Baselines and Comparison Methods: To evaluate the
effectiveness of MARS, we consider two groups of comparison
methods: base policies and routing methods.
Base policies. Frozen VLA directly executes the entire task
using the pretrained generalist policy without atomic skill
policies or dynamic routing. Monolithic RLT adopts the shared
RL Token representation and VLA reference action constraints
of RLT [12], but trains a single RL Actor to optimize the entire
manipulation task.
Routing methods. We compare MARS with MLP, LSTM, and
Transformer routers to evaluate the effects of different routing
architectures. The MLP predicts policy labels from the current
state, while the LSTM and Transformer incorporate temporal
context through recurrent hidden states and temporal attention,
respectively. We further introduce two ablation variants, MARS
w/o Temporal Memory and MARS w/o Semantic Queries, to
examine the contributions of temporal context modeling and
skill-conditioned queries.
3) Evaluation Metrics: Success rate (SR). SR measures
the percentage of trials that successfully complete the target
operation within the specified control budget. We follow the
official success criteria for LIBERO. For Tube Transport, a
trial succeeds only when the robot transfers and inserts all
three tubes in the specified order and returns to its initial
pose. We also use SR to evaluate individual atomic skills under
matched initial conditions.
Sample completion rate (SCR). To measure partial task
progress in Tube Transport, we calculate the proportion of
successfully inserted and released tubes across evaluation
episodes:

B. Main Result
1) Effectiveness of Atomic Skill Policies (Q1): MARS pre-
serves the generalist VLA’s capabilities in routine manipu-
lation while employing specialized atomic skill policies for
precision-sensitive operations. To evaluate the effectiveness of
these policies before examining their contributions to full-
task performance, we compare their local execution success
rates against those of the frozen VLA. Specifically, we select
representative manipulation stages from LIBERO-Spatial and
Tube Transport and evaluate both policies under identical
stage-specific initial conditions. Table IV presents the results.
As shown in Table IV, the reinforcement learning-based
atomic skill policies consistently outperform the frozen VLA
across all four local operations. The improvement is particu-
larly evident in precision tasks, with insertion success on Tube
Transport increasing from 69.57% to 96.85%. These results
demonstrate the benefits of targeted reinforcement learning
optimization for local manipulation skills. However, these
evaluations focus on individual skills under predefined initial
conditions, without accounting for policy selection, switching,
or error accumulation during full-task execution. Therefore,
improved local skill performance does not necessarily guaran-
tee higher full-task success rates.
2) Routing Performance and Ablation Studies (Q2): While
reinforcement learning-based atomic skill policies improve lo-
cal manipulation performance, effective multi-policy execution
also requires accurate policy selection. To evaluate the rout-
ing mechanism independently of low-level policy execution,
we compare different routing architectures and component
ablations on fixed LIBERO test trajectories with identical
ground-truth policy labels. We use Macro-F1 to assess routing
performance across the four LIBERO task suites.
As shown in Table V, MARS achieves the highest Macro-
F1 across all four LIBERO task suites, with an average score
of 90.47%, exceeding the MLP baseline by 7.08 percentage
points. The improvement is particularly notable on LIBERO-
Long, where MARS achieves 89.85% Macro-F1. Ablation
results further show that removing temporal memory and skill-
semantic queries reduces the average Macro-F1 to 84.97% and
86.38%, respectively. These results suggest that Mamba-based
temporal encoding and the historical feature window help
capture temporal context, while skill-semantic queries extract
candidate-specific information to support more accurate policy
routing. Together, these components contribute to improved
routing performance, particularly in long-horizon manipulation
tasks.
Figure 4 presents the training loss curves for different
routing configurations. As shown in Fig. 4(a), all methods
exhibit decreasing losses during training, while MARS main-
tains a comparatively low loss in later epochs. Similarly,
Fig. 4(b) shows higher training losses when either temporal
memory or semantic queries are removed. These observations
are consistent with the Macro-F1 results and provide addi-
tional evidence that both components improve the fit to the
supervised routing objective. Overall, the results demonstrate
that combining temporal context modeling with skill-semantic
queries improves policy label prediction across diverse manip-
ulation tasks, providing a basis for the closed-loop multi-policy
evaluation in Q3.

router’s ability to predict policy labels on fixed trajectories.

formance, where policy decisions influence subsequent robot
states. To evaluate multi-policy coordination during complete
task execution, we integrate the dynamic router with the frozen
VLA backbone and atomic skill policies, and conduct closed-
loop experiments on LIBERO and Tube Transport.

and 10.52 percentage points, respectively. On Tube Transport,
MARS achieves 85.67% SR and 91.11% SCR, substantially
outperforming both base policies. These improvements sug-

including visual observation errors, robot control inaccuracies,
and uncertain physical contacts. We therefore evaluate MARS
on the AgileX PiPER platform described in Section III-A1
to examine whether the simulated improvements transfer to
physical execution.

Although the preceding experiments demonstrate the ef-

introduces additional challenges, including perception errors,
control inaccuracies, and contact uncertainties. To evaluate
its performance under these conditions, we conduct real-
robot experiments on the AgileX piper platform described
in Section III-A1, following the same Tube Transport task

As shown in Table VI, MARS achieves a full-task success

integration of specialized atomic skills and dynamic routing
improves both task completion and execution efficiency in the

Together with the simulation results in Q3, these findings
suggest that MARS can maintain its multi-policy coordination
capabilities when deployed on a real robotic platform, sup-
porting its applicability to physical multi-stage manipulation.
IV. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented MARS, a dynamic atomic-
skill routing framework that coordinates a generalist vision-
language-action (VLA) policy with task-specific reinforcement
learning-based policies for multi-stage robotic manipulation.
MARS separates atomic skill optimization from high-level
policy selection by training specialized skill policies using
shared RL Token representations and VLA reference ac-
tions, while integrating temporal context modeling and skill-
semantic queries for dynamic policy routing. Experiments on
LIBERO and Tube Transport demonstrate the effectiveness
of this approach. MARS achieves an average Macro-F1 of
90.47% across the four LIBERO task suites and improves
full-task success rates by 34.00 and 65.63 percentage points
over the frozen VLA on LIBERO-Long and Tube Transport,
respectively. Ablation studies further support the contributions
of temporal modeling and skill-semantic queries, while real-
robot experiments demonstrate the feasibility of deploying
MARS for physical multi-stage manipulation. These results
suggest that combining specialized atomic skills with dynamic
policy routing provides an effective approach to coordinating
multiple policies in complex robotic manipulation tasks.
Nevertheless, MARS relies on supervised policy labels from
existing trajectories to train the router, which may limit its
adaptability to new tasks and atomic skills. Future work
will explore feedback-driven routing optimization to reduce
dependence on manually annotated task stages and further
investigate the reliability and generalization of MARS across
diverse real-world manipulation scenarios.
