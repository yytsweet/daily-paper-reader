Abstract
Retrieval-augmented
vision-language-action
(VLA)
models enable efficient test-time adaptation by leveraging
expert demonstrations as additional context without up-
dating model parameters. However, even when retrieved
demonstrations are relevant to the current task, the VLA
may fail to effectively utilize the retrieved information,
ultimately leading to task failure. To address this challenge,
we propose R2-VLA, a risk-controlled retrieval-augmented
VLA framework that enables reliable test-time adaptation
by satisfying a user-specified task success rate requirement
with minimal expert assistance. Specifically, R2-VLA learns
an uncertainty predictor that estimates the uncertainty of
the VLA’s predicted actions given the retrieved demonstra-
tions, guiding when to act autonomously or request expert
assistance. We further introduce a task-level risk-control
procedure that calibrates the uncertainty threshold to
satisfy a user-specified task-failure risk requirement. More-
over, R2-VLA continually incorporates expert corrections
into its retrieval memory to improve future adaptation
while preserving the prescribed task-level risk guarantee.
Experiments across three VLA backbones and four LIBERO
benchmark suites demonstrate that R2-VLA improves aver-
age task success by 9.2–11.2 percentage points, achieving
the desired target with fewer expert requests. These results
highlight the effectiveness of connecting the usefulness of
retrieved demonstrations with task-level risk control to
enable successful robotic manipulation.
1. Introduction
Recent advances in vision-language-action (VLA) mod-
els [7–9, 22, 32, 37, 38, 40] have enabled a single policy to
perform a broad range of robotic manipulation tasks, high-
lighting their potential as general-purpose robotic control
policies. However, even strong pre-trained VLAs often re-
quire substantial fine-tuning or retraining to maintain reli-
able performance under distribution shifts, such as new en-
vironments, unseen objects, or novel task instructions [45],
limiting their ability to rapidly adapt at deployment time. In
contrast, Large Language Models (LLMs) can adapt to new
tasks at inference time through in-context learning (ICL):
given only a small number of input-output demonstrations
as context, an LLM can adjust its behavior without up-
dating its model parameters [10, 15, 28]. Inspired by this
paradigm, recent work such as RICL [36] introduces in-
context adaptation to pre-trained VLAs. Specifically, RICL
maintains a compact retrieval buffer of expert demonstra-
tions and, at each decision step, retrieves demonstrations
relevant to the current observation as additional context for
the VLA policy, enabling effective adaptation to previously
unseen deployment conditions without parameter updates.
The effectiveness of retrieval-based in-context adapta-
tion depends critically on which demonstrations are re-
trieved, since these examples determine what task-specific
information is provided to the VLA at deployment. Prior
work [36, 45], including RICL, relies on visual similar-
ity between the current observation and observations in
the retrieval buffer to select relevant demonstrations. Re-
cent approaches have explored improved retrieval mecha-
nisms [21, 25, 44], such as incorporating task instructions
and learning embeddings that better capture behavioral rele-
vance. However, improving retrieval quality does not neces-
sarily guarantee successful task execution [26]. Even when
the retrieved demonstrations are relevant to the current task,
the VLA may still fail to translate the retrieved information
into appropriate actions for its current state. As illustrated
in Fig. 1(a), RICL retrieves behaviorally relevant demon-
strations but nevertheless moves the bowl in the wrong di-
rection, ultimately leading to task failure. This suggests the
usefulness of retrieved demonstrations depends not solely
on their relevance to the current task, but ultimately on

In this work, we propose R2-VLA, a risk-controlled
retrieval-augmented VLA framework that enables reliable
test-time adaptation by satisfying a user-specified task suc-
cess rate. As discussed above, retrieving relevant demon-
strations alone is insufficient for successful task execution,
as the VLA may still fail to effectively utilize the retrieved
information. Therefore, R2-VLA enables the VLA to deter-
mine when it can act autonomously and when additional as-
sistance is necessary to successfully complete the task. To
this end, we augment the VLA with a learned uncertainty
predictor that estimates how uncertain the VLA is about its
predicted actions, given the current observation, retrieved
demonstrations, and its own prior knowledge. When the
predicted uncertainty is below a specified threshold, the
VLA executes its own action autonomously (ACT). Oth-
erwise, it requests expert assistance (ASK) to avoid poten-
tially incorrect actions, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (b). How-
ever, determining an appropriate uncertainty threshold is
nontrivial, as the predicted uncertainty does not directly
correspond to task-level success. We therefore introduce
a risk-control procedure that calibrates the threshold to sat-
isfy a user-specified task-failure risk requirement. In this
way, R2-VLA directly connects the usefulness of retrieved
demonstrations to their ability to support successful task
completion.

3. Risk-Controlled Retrieval-Augmented VLA
Even behaviorally relevant demonstrations do not necessar-
ily enable a retrieval-augmented VLA to complete a task
successfully. We therefore propose R2-VLA, which deter-
mines when the VLA can act autonomously and when it
should request expert assistance, with the goal of satisfying
a user-specified task success requirement while minimizing
the frequency of expert assistance. We first formulate the
R2-VLA policy (Section 3.1), then describe how the uncer-
tainty predictor is learned (Section 3.2) and how its thresh-
old is calibrated using task outcomes (Section 3.3). Finally,
we introduce continual deployment adaptation (Section 3.4)
and present the theoretical results (Section 3.5).
3.1. R2-VLA Framework

framework built upon a retrieval-augmented VLA policy πθ

3.2. Learning the Uncertainty Predictor
As discussed above, the uncertainty predictor provides a
per-step signal to guide ACT/ASK decisions by assessing
the VLA’s uncertainty about its predicted action from its
internal representations, rather than relying on external re-
trieval quality metrics, such as the DINO distance used by
RICL. We characterize action uncertainty through the dis-
crepancy between the VLA’s predicted action and the cor-
responding expert action, where a large discrepancy indi-
cates that the VLA’s predicted action deviates from the de-
sired expert behavior, potentially leading to task failure. We
therefore train the uncertainty predictor to estimate this dis-
crepancy, as detailed below.

corresponding training data, as detailed in Appendix 8.2.

A key challenge is ensuring that the uncertainty head
learns failure modes specifically induced by retrieval. If it
is trained only with ordinary retrieval contexts from Dret,
most retrieved demonstrations may be helpful, providing
relatively few examples in which retrieval actively degrades
the resulting action.
We therefore augment uncertainty-
head training with deliberately degraded retrieval contexts,
including wrong-task retrieval, visually similar retrieval as-
sociated with a different goal, and random or uninformative
retrieval, as detailed in Appendix 8.3 Table 3. Crucially,
these corrupted contexts are used to teach the uncertainty
head action uncertainty, rather than retrieval abnormality.
A poor or unusual retrieval should not necessarily result in
high uncertainty if the VLA can still produce the correct ac-
tion from its pretrained knowledge. In this case, ut ≈0,
and the desired uncertainty remains small. Conversely, a
retrieved context may appear behavior relevant while steer-
ing the VLA toward an incorrect behavior, in which case
ut ≫0 and the uncertainty should be correspondingly
large. We provide an ablation study on the effect of incorpo-
rating corrupted retrieval contexts during uncertainty-head
training in Appendix 9.2, Table 6.

LIBERO contains four suites designed to evaluate differ-

and maintain a disjoint set of 20 demonstrations per task

for the current observation. We report task success over
100 evaluation rollouts per task using a held-out evaluation
set that is disjoint from both the retrieval and calibration
buffers. In addition to task success rate, we report the ASK
rate, denoted by bH(λ) as defined in Section 3.3, which mea-
sures the fraction of decision points at which R2-VLA re-

4.2. Results
Table 1 reports the task success rates and ASK rate of
R2-VLA compared with RICL. R2-VLA consistently im-
proves task success over the corresponding RICL base-
line across all backbone–task suite combinations, demon-
strating the effectiveness of identifying uncertain retrieval-
guided behaviors and requesting expert assistance. More-
over, R2-VLA generally achieves the prescribed task-level
success targets, which are typically set at least 5 percent-
age points above the corresponding RICL baseline. Specif-
ically, R2-VLA reaches or exceeds the target success rate
in 11 of the 12 settings. The only exception is π0-FAST
on LIBERO-OBJECT, where the achieved success rate of
78.9% is slightly below the 80.0% target. This small divia-
tion may be due to limited number of evaluation rollouts, as
our risk-control guarantee in Theorem 2 is statistical rather
than a deterministic guarantee on every evaluation set. The
ASK rate further reflect the varying difficulty of successful
execution across task suites. In particular, LIBERO-LONG,
which exhibits the lowest success rate across all three back-
bones, also requires the highest ASK rate. In contrast, the
other suites generally require fewer expert assistance while
still achieving their prescribed success targets. These re-
sults suggest that R2-VLA can adapt its reliance on expert
assistance to the uncertain of predicted execution across dif-
ferent tasks and VLA architectures.
Improved task success alone does not establish that
the learned uncertainty identifies decision points where re-
trieved demonstrations are insufficient for successful VLA
execution, as task success may also improve simply by del-
egating more actions to an expert. We therefore compare
R2-VLA against a Random ASK baseline that requests ex-
pert assistance at randomly selected decision points while
matching the expert assistance frequency of our method.
This comparison isolates the benefit of identifying uncertain
retrieval-guided behaviors rather than merely increasing ex-
pert involvement. To further investigate whether learning
action uncertainty from the VLA is necessary, we consider
two alternative uncertainty signals: (1) DINO Distance,

the VLA’s action distribution. The DINO Distance variant
directly evaluates whether the original retrieval quality met-
ric is sufficient to identify when retrieved demonstrations
provide insufficient behavioral guidance, while the Action
Entropy variant evaluates whether the model’s predictive
uncertainty can identify uncertain actions without training
an additional uncertainty estimator. For these two baselines,
thresholds are independently calibrated using the same risk-

We next evaluate whether expert corrections collected
through ASK can improve future task success while reduc-
ing the need for external assistance. Following Algorithm 2,
we divide deployment into adaptation rounds. During each
round, the retrieval buffer and calibrated threshold ˆλ re-
main fixed, and expert corrections are collected whenever
the robot requests assistance. At the end of each round,
these corrections are incorporated into the retrieval mem-
ory, followed by recalibration using Algorithm 1. Specif-
ically, we execute fresh calibration using the updated re-
trieval memory as detailed in Appendix 8.1, ensuring that
calibration satisfies the requirement of Theorem 2. For con-
sistent comparison across rounds, we evaluate each updated
policy on the same held-out evaluation sets as before. We
maintain the same fixed target success rate as in Table 1.
Figure 2 illustrates the evolution of R2-VLA using the
π0-FAST backbone across memory adaptation rounds. As
expert corrections are progressively incorporated into the
retrieval memory, task success consistently improves while
the frequency of expert assistance decreases. Importantly,
after the initial memory update, the observed success rates
meet or exceed the prescribed targets across adaptation
rounds, providing empirical evidence consistent with The-
orem 2. Meanwhile, the decreasing ASK rate suggests that
accumulated expert corrections provide increasingly useful
retrieval experiences, enabling the VLA to achieve higher
task success with less external assistance. We further exam-
ine how the calibrated threshold ˆλ evolves as the retrieval
memory grows. As shown in Figure 2, the threshold varies
across adaptation rounds, with distinct patterns across task
suites. These changes reflect the evolving distribution of
uncertainty scores induced by updates to the retrieval mem-
ory, which can alter both the retrieved demonstrations and
the resulting action predictions. Therefore, a threshold cal-
ibrated for an earlier retrieval buffer may no longer pro-
vide the desired risk control after memory updates.
By
recalibrating ˆλ after each adaptation round, R2-VLA ad-
justs its ACT/ASK decisions to the updated policy. The re-
sults also show that this recalibration can be performed with
relatively modest computational overhead, with calibration
wall time remaining approximately stable across later adap-
tation rounds. Together, these findings demonstrate that R2-
VLA can progressively improve autonomous task execution
and reduce expert dependence while empirically maintain-
ing the prescribed task-level success rate requirement.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we proposed R2-VLA, a risk-controlled
retrieval-augmented VLA framework that enables reliable
test-time adaptation by satisfying a user-specified task suc-
cess rate requirement with minimal expert assistance. R2-
VLA learns an uncertainty predictor to determine when to
act autonomously and when to request expert assistance.
We further introduced a risk-control procedure that cali-
brates the uncertainty threshold to satisfy the prescribed
task-level failure risk requirement. Moreover, R2-VLA sup-
ports continual adaptation by incorporating expert correc-
tions into retrieval memory and preserving task-level risk
control. Experiments across various settings demonstrate
consistent improvements in task success over RICL while
requiring less frequent expert assistance. Overall, our work
represents a step toward building more reliable and adaptive
robotic systems that can recognize their own limitations,
seek assistance when necessary, and continually improve
through interaction with their environments.

6. Related Work
Retrieval-Augmented
VLA.
While
recent
vision-
language-action (VLA) models, such as OpenVLA [22]
and π0 [7],
have demonstrated strong generalization
through pretraining on large and diverse robot datasets,
adapting them to new tasks often still requires task-
specific fine-tuning or retraining, limiting their ability to
behave as truly generalist agents.
In contrast, humans
can rapidly adapt to novel tasks by drawing on prior
experience [3, 34, 39]. Motivated by this capability, recent
work has explored in-context imitation learning, in which
demonstrations of a new task are provided directly to
the VLA at inference time, enabling adaptation without
updating the model parameters.
ICRT [17] is an early
representative approach, formulating robot control as next-
token prediction over sequences of visual observations,
proprioceptive states, and actions.
By placing one or
more human teleoperation trajectories in the transformer’s
context, ICRT can infer and execute previously unseen
tasks without test-time parameter updates, demonstrating
that the in-context learning behavior of autoregressive
models can extend from language to continuous robotic
control.
More recently, RICL [36] showed that such
capability does not naturally emerge in pretrained VLAs
and proposed explicitly post-training a pretrained π0-FAST
policy to exploit retrieved demonstrations. At deployment,
RICL retrieves relevant states and actions from a small
demonstration buffer and incorporates them into the model
context, enabling adaptation to new manipulation tasks
using roughly 10–20 demonstrations without updating
model parameters.
A parallel direction investigates whether existing frozen
VLAs can benefit from demonstrations without additional
in-context learning (ICL) training. Retrieval-VLA [45] en-
ables training-free adaptation by identifying relevant past
experiences and directly incorporating the retrieved infor-
mation into the predictions of a frozen VLA. Since the VLA
does not need to learn how to interpret retrieved demon-
strations as in-context examples, this approach avoids ICL-
specific post-training. Similarly, ReCAP [31] extends VLA
policies to new tasks by conditioning on demonstrations
from an external retrieval pool without task-specific retrain-
ing. Beyond directly leveraging retrieved demonstrations,
recent methods further explore how to improve their rel-
evance and utilization. StellaVLA [43] converts retrieved
trajectories into structured task plans, subgoals, and mo-
tion descriptions to provide richer task-specific guidance.
RA-VLA [21] introduces behavior-aligned retrieval and
grounded execution to improve the relevance of retrieved
demonstrations, while ICI-VLA [44] employs spatiotempo-
rally aligned demonstrations to better match the current task
phase and geometry. Collectively, these studies demonstrate
the potential of retrieval-based adaptation for extending
VLA capabilities without task-specific parameter updates.
Building on these advances, our work addresses the com-
plementary challenge of ensuring successful task execution
given retrieved demonstrations by introducing uncertainty-
guided expert assistance and task-level risk control.
Uncertainty in Robotics. Uncertainty has long been used
in robot learning to determine when autonomous execution
of the robot should be deferred to an expert [13, 20, 29, 46].
Early confidence-based learning-from-demonstration meth-
ods allow a robot to request demonstrations in states where
its learned policy is insufficiently confident [13]. This prin-
ciple was later incorporated into interactive imitation learn-
ing.
EnsembleDAgger [29] uses ensemble disagreement
to determine when a learned policy can safely act [29].
More recently, ConformalDAgger [46] replaces heuristic
uncertainty thresholds with online conformal calibration,
enabling uncertainty estimates to adapt under deployment-
time expert shift.
Recent work has extended uncertainty estimation to
VLA models. SAFE [19] estimates uncertainty from the
internal representations of a VLA to assess the reliability
of ongoing task execution and determine when intervention
may be needed. ReconVLA [11] uses conformalized action
uncertainty to compare candidate actions and select the least
uncertain action for execution. [35] estimate epistemic un-
certainty in flow-based VLAs to identify unreliable predic-
tions and prioritize informative samples for active adapta-
tion. SCALE [14] uses self-uncertainty to adapt both visual
perception and action generation, encouraging broader ex-
ploration under high uncertainty and more confident execu-
tion under low uncertainty. [18] use calibrated uncertainty
to gate control among normal VLA execution, pause-and-
reobservation, and fallback planning.
Collectively, these
methods demonstrate the value of uncertainty estimation for
identifying potentially incorrect robot actions and determin-
ing when expert assistance is necessary. Building on these
advances, our work introduces task-level risk control to cal-
ibrate the uncertainty threshold, enabling the VLA to satisfy
a user-specified task success requirement while minimizing
the frequency of expert assistance.
Risk Control for Robotics. Statistical risk-control tech-
niques [1, 2, 4] have recently been applied to LLM-based
robotic planning.
KnowNo [33] uses conformal predic-
tion to construct sets of candidate robot actions, execut-
1


ing autonomously when the prediction set is sufficiently
unambiguous and otherwise requesting human assistance.
Introspective Planning [23] improves the underlying LLM
reasoning before conformal calibration to reduce unneces-
sary clarification while retaining statistical success guaran-
tees. [41] extends conformal planning to multi-robot sys-
tems. [42] propose conformal-aware finetuning to reduce
prediction-set size and human-help rates. In contrast, we
study risk control for VLAs during closed-loop task execu-
tion. Rather than calibrating the uncertainty of an individ-
ual action, we calibrate the uncertainty threshold that de-
termines when the VLA acts autonomously and when it re-
quests expert assistance. The threshold is evaluated through
complete task rollouts, so the resulting risk criterion directly
reflects whether the induced VLA policy succeeds or fails
at the task level. This formulation also accounts for the se-
quential nature of VLA execution, where locally uncertain
decisions can affect subsequent states and ultimately deter-
mine task success.

8. Additional Implementation Details
8.1. Benchmark and Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate R2-VLA on LIBERO [24], a simulated robotic
manipulation benchmark based on the Franka Panda arm.
Each demonstration contains multi-view visual observa-
tions, including front-view and wrist-view images, together
with proprioceptive states, natural-language task instruc-
tions, and expert actions. We consider all four LIBERO
suites: SPATIAL, OBJECT, GOAL, and LONG, which eval-
uate different aspects of manipulation generalization. For
each suite, we partition the available demonstrations into
disjoint sets for retrieval and policy adaptation, risk cal-
ibration, and evaluation. Specifically, demonstration IDs
10–29 are used to construct the retrieval buffer, perform
suite-specific RICL adaptation, and train the uncertainty
head, corresponding to 20 demonstrations per task and
200 demonstrations per suite. Demonstration IDs 30–49
are reserved for risk calibration, while IDs 0–9 are held
6


out for evaluation and are not used for policy adaptation,
uncertainty-head training, or risk calibration.
During deployment, whenever R2-VLA selects ASK, a
separately trained expert policy provides the corrective ac-
tion for the current observation. The expert policy is trained
using demonstration IDs 0–49 for each suite and serves ex-
clusively as an action oracle without updating the evaluated
VLA during execution. We report task success and ASK
rates over 100 evaluation rollouts per task.
For risk calibration, we construct a predefined candidate
threshold set Λ = {λ1, . . . , λK} with K = 10. Specifi-
cally, we first collect uncertainty scores from five prelimi-
nary rollouts using demonstration IDs 30–34. Let Uref =
S5
i=1{ui,t}Ti
t=1 denote the pooled uncertainty scores across
these rollouts, where Ti is the number of decision steps in
rollout i. We compute the reference threshold as
λref = Quantile0.60 (Uref) .
We then construct Λ by selecting K = 10 candidate thresh-
olds evenly spaced over the interval [λref −0.1, λref + 0.1].
The resulting candidate set is fixed before applying the risk-
control procedure in Algorithm 1.
To evaluate continual memory adaptation, we organize
deployment into successive adaptation rounds following Al-
gorithm 2. Within each round, the retrieval buffer and cal-
ibrated threshold remain fixed, and expert corrections are
collected whenever R2-VLA requests assistance.
At the
end of each round, these corrections are incorporated into
the retrieval memory, followed by threshold recalibration
using Algorithm 1. Specifically, demonstration IDs 30–49
remain designated for calibration, while fresh initial states
are independently sampled from the task initialization dis-
tribution at each round. We then perform new calibration
rollouts using the updated retrieval memory to obtain the
calibrated threshold for the next round.
We set the overall confidence parameter to δ = 0.05, cor-
responding to a 95% confidence level. For continual mem-
ory adaptation with Q + 1 rounds, we allocate the confi-
dence budget equally across rounds, using δr = δ/(Q + 1)
for calibration at round q, consistent with Theorem 2. All
experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090
GPU with 24 GB of memory.
8.2. VLA Backbones
We follow the model architecture adopted in RICL [36],
which uses π0-FAST as the VLA backbone.
To eval-
uate whether R2-VLA generalizes across different VLA
architectures and model scales, we additionally consider
MiniVLA [5] and OpenVLA [22]. Across all three back-
bones, four retrieved demonstrations are provided as in-
context examples for each query, i.e., M = 4. Because
the publicly available backbone from RICL checkpoints are
not directly trained for LIBERO experiments, we first adapt
each backbone to support retrieval-conditioned execution.
MiniVLA.
For MiniVLA, we initialize from the 150,000-
step LIBERO checkpoint.1 We first perform 2,000 RICL-
priming optimizer updates using 68 LIBERO priming tasks
with 20 demonstrations per task. We use rank-32 LoRA,
gradient accumulation of 16, AdamW, and a peak learning
rate of 2.5 × 10−5. During priming, the vision encoder and
base language-model weights are frozen, while the LoRA
parameters, visual projector, and action-token rows remain
trainable. Starting from the primed model, we then per-
form suite-specific adaptation using demonstration IDs 10–
29. For each of the four LIBERO suites, we perform 600
additional optimizer updates with a peak learning rate of
5 × 10−6.
π0-FAST.
For π0-FAST, we initialize from Physical Intel-
ligence’s LIBERO-finetuned π0-FAST checkpoint.2 We di-
rectly perform suite-specific RICL adaptation using demon-
stration IDs 10–29. We use rank-16 LoRA, AdamW, a peak
learning rate of 1 × 10−5, and gradient clipping at 1.0. We
use 3,000 updates for OBJECT, 5,000 for SPATIAL, 13,000
for GOAL, and 10,000 for LONG.
OpenVLA.
For OpenVLA, we initialize from the official
OpenVLA-7B checkpoint.3 We first perform 1,500 RICL-
priming updates using the same 68 priming tasks and 20
demonstrations per task. We use rank-16 LoRA, gradient
accumulation of 8, and a peak learning rate of 5×10−6. We

8.3. Uncertainty-Head Implementation
Architecture.
R2-VLA augments each adapted RICL
policy with a lightweight uncertainty head.
After RICL

last conditioning-token representation after the model’s fi-
nal normalization and immediately before the vocabulary
projection. A lightweight MLP maps this representation to
a nonnegative scalar u, which estimates the action predic-

Training Data.
For all backbones and LIBERO suites,
uncertainty-head training uses demonstration IDs 10–29,
i.e., the same demonstrations used for retrieval and suite-
specific policy adaptation. Training exclusively on clean
retrieval contexts provides limited supervision for states in
which retrieval is misleading. We therefore augment the
training data with deliberately corrupted retrieval contexts.
These corruptions are designed to expose the uncertainty
head to situations in which the retrieved examples may ap-
pear plausible but provide incorrect or uninformative guid-
ance for the current action. We consider four corruption
types:
1. Wrong-task retrieval. The retrieved example is sam-
pled from a different task, preferentially from a task
whose instruction shares words with the current task but
whose scene or intended behavior differs.
2. Correct task, wrong phase. The retrieval is taken from
the correct task and demonstration, but the retrieved
timestep is shifted forward. The retrieved action there-
fore corresponds to a different stage of task execution.
3. Visually similar, wrong goal. The retrieved example is
selected from another task in the same scene, producing
a visually plausible observation while corresponding to
a different task goal and intended behavior.
4. Random retrieval. The retrieved example is sampled
randomly from a different task, providing context that is
largely unrelated to the current decision.
Among corrupted training examples, the four corruption
types are sampled according to the proportions in Table 3.
The fraction of uncertainty-head training examples that use
corrupted retrieval contexts is backbone dependent. We use
30% corrupted contexts for MiniVLA, 50% for π0-FAST,
and 30% for OpenVLA. the remaining examples use the
original clean retrieval context.

Retrieval Corruption.
We evaluate the contribution of
corrupted retrieval contexts during uncertainty-head train-
ing by comparing R2-VLA against a variant trained ex-
clusively with clean retrieval demonstrations.
As shown
in Table 6, incorporating retrieval corruption consistently
improves average task success while substantially reducing
ASK rate across all three VLA backbones. Specifically, for
MiniVLA, the average success rate increases from 59.5% to
62.8%, while the ASK rate decreases from 33.9% to 20.8%.
Similar improvements are observed for π0-FAST, where the
success rate increases from 70.5% to 72.0% and the ASK
rate decreases from 61.7% to 49.0%, and for OpenVLA,
where the success rate increases from 67.2% to 68.6% while
the ASK rate decreases from 47.4% to 33.3%. These re-
sults suggest that exposing the uncertainty head to mislead-
ing retrieval contexts improves its ability to distinguish cer-
tain from uncertain retrieval-guided actions. Importantly,
the reduction in ASK rate is accompanied by improved task
success, indicating that retrieval corruption does not merely
encourage more conservative uncertainty estimates but in-
stead enables more selective expert assistance.
Overall,
these findings support the importance of explicitly incorpo-
rating retrieval failure modes into uncertainty-head training
to improve both task success rate and expert assitance fre-
quency.
