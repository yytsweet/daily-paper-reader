Abstract
Humanoid loco-manipulation requires coordinating whole-body motion to perform manipulation
tasks in complex environments. Yet egocentric vision-language-action (VLA) policies can miss critical
body–scene relationships, and action imitation alone provides no explicit supervision for future scene
evolution. We propose BeyondEgo to extend VLA perception in space and time. Spatially, we encode a
synchronized exocentric view with frozen DINOv3 and concatenate the resulting tokens with egocentric
VLM tokens to condition the action expert. Temporally, we use future observations from both views
as privileged supervision for a residual adapter on task-conditioned VLM features. A foresight expert
regresses DINOv3 representations of both views 0.6 s ahead. At deployment, the foresight expert is
discarded and the 4.2M-parameter adapter is retained. We also introduce HELM, to our knowledge, the
first closed-loop humanoid loco-manipulation benchmark with synchronized egocentric and exocentric
views across five whole-body tasks. On HELM, BeyondEgo improves success from 48.0% to 92.0% in
distribution and from 23.5% to 65.0% on 200 held-out scenes with layout, asset, material, and lighting
shifts. Ablations show that dual-view DINOv3 foresight yields the best transfer results.

Introduction
Humanoid robots offer the potential to extend manipulation beyond fixed workspaces, enabling tasks that
require coordinating object interaction with whole-body locomotion. For example, carrying a box through
a narrow gap requires maintaining a stable grasp while aligning the torso and limbs with the opening and
avoiding surrounding obstacles. Such behaviors depend on understanding the spatial relationship between
the robot’s body and the environment, as well as anticipating how this relationship will evolve during motion.
Recent humanoid vision-language-action (VLA) models make these behaviors increasingly learnable from
demonstrations and language instructions, typically by combining a vision-language backbone (System-2), a
fast action generator (System-1), and a learned whole-body controller (System-0) [1–3].
Despite this progress, humanoid VLA policies typically rely on head-mounted egocentric cameras, which
provide limited cues for whole-body spatial awareness and future-oriented decision-making. The limitation
is both spatial and temporal. Spatially, the egocentric view moves with the robot and can be occluded
by its arms or carried objects, making it difficult to infer critical body–scene relationships, such as the
distance between the shoulders and nearby obstacles, the alignment of the body with a narrow opening, or the
robot’s position relative to a junction. Temporally, policies are typically conditioned on current observations
without explicit supervision for how these spatial relationships evolve as the robot moves. However, successful

what is visible in the current frame. These observations motivate extending the policy’s perception beyond
the egocentric view in both space and time. No existing benchmark fits this setting: ego-exo datasets are
human and offline, while robot multi-view benchmarks target tabletop manipulation.
We propose BeyondEgo, which combines exocentric spatial context with dual-view foresight. For the
spatial extension, we introduce a synchronized view from an additional exocentric camera. We encode this
view with frozen DINOv3 [4] and concatenate the resulting tokens with egocentric VLM tokens as input to
the action expert (Fig. 1). This provides additional visual context about the scene layout and the robot’s
body relative to its surroundings. For the temporal extension, we use future observations from both views as
privileged training supervision [5]. A residual adapter refines VLM latent features before they are passed
to the action expert. A foresight expert reads the adapted features together with exocentric features and
predicts frozen DINOv3 representations of both views 0.6 s ahead (Fig. 1). The foresight loss supervises the
observation interface, while the action expert is trained with flow matching. At deployment, we discard the
foresight expert and retain the 4.2M-parameter adapter.
We therefore build HELM, a benchmark of five simulated humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, with 50
paired in-distribution trials and 200 held-out scenes spanning layout, asset, material, lighting, and combined
shifts. We evaluate our designs in closed-loop simulation over this benchmark. BeyondEgo achieves 92.0%
in-distribution and 65.0% under distribution shift, improving over both the egocentric Ψ0 baseline (48.0%
and 23.5%) and the no-foresight Feature fusion baseline (84.0% and 55.0%). Target-space ablations show that
predicting DINOv3 features of both views provides the strongest transfer, whereas predicting VLM features
does not improve over the no-foresight baseline.
In summary, our contributions are:
• A spatial extension to humanoid VLAs through exocentric observations, supported by a comparison of two
fusion strategies. We show that action-expert-level fusion provides stronger generalization than VLM-level
fusion under the tested distribution shifts.
• A dual-view foresight objective that uses future observations as privileged supervision to embed future
dynamics in VLM latent features. The foresight expert is removed at deployment, while the learned feature

Related Work
Humanoid VLAs and multi-view perception.
Vision-language-action (VLA) models map visual
observations and language to robot actions [6–8], often using flow-matching action experts to generate
continuous action chunks [8,9]. Hierarchical architectures combine vision-language backbones (System-2)
with fast action generators (System-1) [1,3,10], while humanoid and legged systems further employ learned
whole-body or locomotion controllers [2,3,11–13], supported by teleoperation-based data collection [14–17].
Recent systems also leverage egocentric human videos and humanoid demonstrations for pre-training [3,18],
together with real-time action chunking [19, 20]. Beyond egocentric perception, multi-view manipulation
policies combine wrist-mounted and third-person cameras [8, 21, 22], with different viewpoints exhibiting
distinct generalization trade-offs [23]. Synchronized views enable self-supervised learning [24, 25], while
ego-exo datasets provide paired first- and third-person observations [26–28]. These capture human activity
offline, unlike our robot setting. Geometric representations, such as point clouds, have also been injected
directly into VLA action experts [29,30]. We study exocentric feature fusion in hierarchical humanoid VLAs,
exploit future observations from both views as training supervision, and contribute HELM, a benchmark
with synchronized ego-exo views and closed-loop evaluation under controlled shifts.
Predictive learning and privileged foresight. World models predict future observations under actions [31,
32], increasingly in latent spaces learned by self-supervised encoders [33,34]; self-predictive learning similarly
trains encoders to predict future representations [35]. In robot learning, foresight is commonly coupled with
action generation, either by conditioning policies on predicted frames, subgoals, or world representations [36–
40], or by jointly predicting future observations and actions [41–44]. Closest to our setting, ω-0 [45] predicts
future visual latents for humanoid loco-manipulation and accepts either an egocentric or an exocentric view. It
uses a single view per task, and its action branch still attends to the future-prediction queries at inference; we
fuse both views at every step and remove the foresight expert entirely at deployment. FLARE [46] aligns policy
features with future observation embeddings, while other auxiliary objectives align or reconstruct current
inputs using frozen visual features [47,48] or image regions [49]. Learning using privileged information [5]
provides another perspective: prior robot learning methods distill privileged simulator states into sensor-based
policies [50–52] or use them in asymmetric critics [53]. In contrast, BeyondEgo treats frozen DINOv3
features of future dual-view frames as privileged regression targets, without requiring simulator states or
a state-based teacher policy. Specifically, it supervises the observation interface consumed by the action
expert and leaves the action expert’s flow-matching objective intact, rather than supervising the policy’s
internal representations [46] or sharing a generator between prediction and action [41,43], while discarding
the foresight expert at deployment. Our ablations further show that the choice of foresight target space
strongly affects closed-loop transfer.
Visual representations and closed-loop evaluation. Pre-trained visual representations are widely used
in robot learning [54,55], with self-supervised vision transformers [4,56] providing dense features that capture
3D properties such as depth and surface orientation [57]. Prior VLMs and VLAs fuse such visual features
with language-aligned representations inside the backbone [7,58]; we instead inject DINOv3 features directly
into the action expert and use the same feature space for future supervision. For evaluation, robot policy
generalization is increasingly assessed in closed-loop settings under controlled environmental shifts [59], with
simulation providing useful proxies for real-world performance [60].

The action a ∈R36 inherits the definition from Ψ0 [3] as {qhand, qarm, torsorpy, hb, vx, vy, vyaw, pyaw}, where

We build on a triple-system humanoid VLA model [3,45] (Sec. 3.1) and extend its perception beyond a
single egocentric observation, spatially through an exocentric camera (Sec. 3.2) and temporally with dual-view
foresight (Sec. 3.3). Figure 2 shows the complete model.
3.1
Model Architecture
Our model organizes humanoid control into three systems [3, 45]. System-2 processes observation ot
and provides semantic and geometric understanding through its hidden features. We use Qwen3-VL-2B-

with information about future dynamics through a residual block trained with future-frame supervision.
The action expert, implemented as a multimodal diffusion transformer (MM-DiT), is trained with flow
matching to predict future whole-body action chunks at:t+H conditioned on these spatiotemporally augmented
features and the proprioceptive states st. System-0 is an RL-based control policy that converts the predicted
actions into robot control commands. Following Ψ0, we use AMO [16] to map the lower-body commands
{torsorpy, hb, vx, vy, vyaw, pyaw} to leg and waist joint targets.

3.4
Implementation and Training Details
Implementation.
We use Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [61] as the VLM backbone in System-2, with feature
width d = 2048. DINOv3 ViT-L/16 [4] extracts features from observation images at 224 × 384 pixels. We
retain its class token and apply 2 × 2 average pooling to the patch-token grid, yielding 85 tokens of dimension
1024. The adapter Wα is a single linear projection layer with 4.2M parameters. The foresight expert is a
Transformer with hidden dimension 512 and approximately 19.2M parameters. We set the future prediction
horizon ∆t to 0.6 s. The System-1 action expert is a 500M-parameter multimodal diffusion transformer with
hidden dimension 1536, trained with flow matching. It predicts chunks of H = 30 commands for execution at
50 Hz, covering the same 0.6 s horizon. We connect modules of different feature widths using learnable MLP
projectors.
Exocentric dropout. To reduce over-reliance on the external view and strengthen the use of egocentric
cues, we apply exocentric observation dropout during training. With probability 0.1, we mask exocentric
tokens in all attention blocks and exclude the corresponding exocentric targets from the future prediction
loss. This requires the policy to also learn action prediction without access to the external view.
Real-time action chunking. We adopt the training-time real-time chunking (RTC) procedure in [20],
where they condition each prediction on the previously committed action chunk to maintain consistency
across successive chunks. At test time, we use 10 sampling steps and execute the first 15 commands of each
chunk before replanning, using RTC [19] with an inference delay of seven control steps.
Training details.
We follow the training recipe of Ψ0 [3] and initialize the VLM and action expert
from its released pre-trained and post-trained checkpoints, respectively. The VLM is pre-trained through
autoregressive prediction of FAST-tokenized single-step actions, first on EgoDex and then on Humanoid
Everyday. The action expert is subsequently post-trained on Humanoid Everyday using flow matching in joint
space, with the VLM frozen. For our target tasks, we keep the VLM and DINOv3 frozen and jointly optimize
the action expert, residual adapter, foresight expert, and learnable projectors using the action prediction and
future-feature supervision losses
Ltrain = Lact + λLfut,
(7)
where we set λ = 1.0. We train for 40k steps per task with a global batch size of 128 on eight B200 GPUs. We
use AdamW with β1 = 0.95, β2 = 0.999, and weight decay 10−6. The learning rate reaches 10−4 after 1,000
warm-up steps and then follows a cosine decay schedule. Training uses BF16 mixed precision and gradient
clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0. We normalize states and actions using their respective minimum
and maximum values and sample flow timesteps uniformly from [0, 1]. We also apply the training-time RTC
procedure described above.

Experiments
We evaluate BeyondEgo and the underlying spatial and temporal extensions on five humanoid loco-
manipulation tasks, focusing on three questions: How much does the complete method improve task success
and generalization? Which spatial fusion strategy and training-time foresight components contribute to
these gains? How do the target feature space, supervised views, and exocentric camera placement affect task
execution?
4.1

Dex3 hands (Fig. 3), each built so the decisive cue lies outside or ahead of the head-mounted view. All tasks
require picking up a cookie box, moving to the target table, and placing the box into a designated container.

at its end; the challenge is to adapt the turning location to the corridor length and select the correct left or

We develop an automated data pipeline: CuRobo generates arm, navigation, and whole-body control
commands, trajectories are rolled out in MuJoCo and recorded at 50 Hz, and validated trajectories are
replayed in Isaac Sim to render synchronized egocentric and exocentric images at 640 × 360. In total, 500
valid demonstrations are collected across five tasks.
Evaluation protocols and metrics. We evaluate in SIMPLE [64] (Isaac Sim rendering, MuJoCo physics,
AMO motion control). All five tasks have a 35 s episode limit. A trial succeeds if the robot follows the
prescribed route and leaves the box stably inside the target container. For in-distribution evaluation, we
sample scenes from the training distribution using a fixed random seed and conduct 10 trials per task, yielding
50 trials in total. For generalization, we freeze 40 scenes per task across five shift families (layout, asset,
material, lighting, and combined), eight scenes each, 200 in total. All scenes are frozen and hash-pinned, so
every method sees identical conditions. We report per-task and mean success rates, and for T1 the grasp,
correct-branch, and placement counts for stage-wise analysis.

4.2
Overall Performance and Generalization
Overall task performance. BeyondEgo achieves the highest mean success rate among the four configurations
in both evaluation suites (Fig. 4). In distribution, it reaches 92.0%, improving over the egocentric-only Ψ0
baseline (48.0%) by 44.0 percentage points. With the exocentric view, VLM fusion and Feature fusion reach
74.0% and 84.0%, respectively. BeyondEgo further improves upon the stronger Feature fusion baseline by 8.0
percentage points, demonstrating the performance benefits of combining spatial and temporal extensions.
Generalization to scene variations. Across 200 generalization scenes, BeyondEgo achieves the highest
success rate of 65.0%, exceeding the 23.5%, 49.5%, and 55.0% achieved by Ψ0, VLM fusion, and Feature
fusion, respectively. It succeeds approximately 2.8 times as often as Ψ0 and improves upon Feature fusion
by 10.0 percentage points, demonstrating the generalization benefits of the complete method under scene
variations. These gains extend across multiple tasks: BeyondEgo outperforms Feature fusion on four of the
five tasks, with the largest improvement on T5 and further gains on T1, T2, and T3. On T4, however, Feature
fusion achieves a slightly higher success rate (87.5% versus 85.0%).

4.3
Ablation Studies
Spatial fusion. Feature fusion improves success rates over VLM fusion by 10.0 and 5.5 percentage points
in the in-distribution and generalization evaluations, respectively (Fig. 4). It achieves higher success rates
on all five tasks under generalization and matches or exceeds VLM fusion in distribution. We hypothesize
that the additional pre-training in VLM fusion may alter the original VLM feature distribution, whereas
Feature fusion supplements exocentric information through a separate DINOv3 branch, helping maintain
compatibility between the original visual features and the pre-trained action expert. Given its higher success
rates and absence of additional backbone pre-training, we adopt Feature fusion for the temporal extension.
Foresight supervision. By adding dual-view foresight to Feature fusion, BeyondEgo improves mean success
rates by 8.0 and 10.0 percentage points in the in-distribution and generalization evaluations, respectively.
Future supervision may help the policy anticipate changes in the scene and the robot’s position, leading
to better action decisions. Both policies receive the same current observations at deployment; BeyondEgo
9


removes the foresight expert and retains the trained adapter as its only additional component.
Supervised views. For the T1 task, dual-view foresight with DINOv3 targets achieves a higher success
rate than either single-view variant or the Feature fusion baseline without foresight (Figs. 4 and 5a). This
advantage may result from complementary future information: the egocentric view captures local scene
changes, while the exocentric view captures changes in the robot’s position within the environment. Predicting
both types of future change may help the policy coordinate local manipulation with whole-body motion and
improve task completion.
Target feature space. We compare future feature targets from two frozen teacher models, DINOv3 and
Qwen3-VL, on T1. For each target space, we use egocentric-only, exocentric-only, and dual-view supervision,
yielding six variants. All variants receive current dual-view observations at deployment and differ only in the
future supervision used during training. In the generalization evaluation, DINOv3 targets yield higher task
success rates than Qwen3-VL targets under all three supervision settings (Fig. 5b). We argue that DINOv3
features may better preserve spatial relationships between the robot and its environment, providing more
useful future supervision for turning and route selection. These results support using DINOv3 features as
prediction targets for our method.
Exocentric camera placement. We compare left, right, and central exocentric camera placements on
T1, training and evaluating a separate model with each placement (Fig. 5c). Neither side camera covers
the full task scene, and the left view is more heavily occluded; the central placement covers the entire task
area. All three configurations achieve 100% (10/10) in-distribution success, while generalization performance
varies: the central camera achieves the highest success rate of 77.5% (31/40), followed by the right camera at
72.5% (29/40) and the left camera at 60.0% (24/40). Broader scene coverage may provide more complete
spatial context for route selection and whole-body motion. These results support selecting exocentric camera
positions that cover the full task scene with minimal occlusion.
5
Conclusion
We introduce BeyondEgo, which extends the perception of hierarchical humanoid VLAs in both space
and time. We compare ways of integrating the exocentric view and use dual-view future supervision to
improve the observations fed to the action expert. To evaluate perception beyond the egocentric view, we
also introduce HELM, a closed-loop humanoid loco-manipulation benchmark with synchronized ego-exo
views and controlled scene shifts. Experiments show that directly integrating exocentric information into
the action expert yields better performance, while the temporal extension with an adapter and foresight
supervision further improves overall task success and generalization to scene variations. BeyondEgo uses
future information as training supervision; at deployment, the foresight expert is removed, and only the
lightweight adapter is retained. More broadly, our results suggest that privileged signals available only during
training, such as future observations from both views, can yield deployable gains through representation-level
supervision. We hope this combination of exocentric perception and dual-view future supervision can inform
loco-manipulation across robots and sensors.
Limitations. BeyondEgo requires external camera coverage and synchronized dual-view images; large-scale
movement may limit exocentric observations, and dual-view demonstrations increase data-collection cost.
Future work could explore multi-camera coordination, more flexible placement, and foresight horizons beyond
one action chunk.

Action and state.
Each of the 30 commands in a chunk has 36 dimensions: 14 hand joint targets, 14 arm
joint targets, torso roll, pitch and yaw, torso height, forward, lateral and yaw velocity, and a target heading.

targets. Actions and states are normalized with statistics of the training data.

of FAST action tokens [65]. It is then frozen; the last-layer tokens of each view are linearly projected and
concatenated as the observation sequence of the action expert, which is trained with the recipe of Tab. 3.

Closed-loop evaluation.
The policy server runs the deployed graph; the client runs SIMPLE [64] (Isaac
Sim rendering, MuJoCo physics) with the AMO controller in the loop and no privileged inputs to the policy.
Success is evaluated by a task-specific predicate on the final state: for T1, the box must rest stably supported
near the center of the tray; for T2–T5, the box must have been released into the tray. Collisions are checked

records whether the box was grasped, whether the robot took the branch of the junction that leads to the

Generalization suite.
For each task we freeze 40 scenes, 8 per shift family: layout (arrangements with
parameters outside the training ranges, such as narrower channels and tighter obstacle gaps), asset (unseen
object and furniture meshes), material (unseen textures), lighting (intensity scaled by 0.55 or 1.5 and color
temperature 3400 K or 8800 K) and combined shifts. Only visual bindings, materials, lighting and layout
parameters change; collision geometry and scoring are untouched. Every layout and combined scene passes a
static route-feasibility check, and no scene duplicates a training episode or another scene of the suite. Scene
files are frozen and identified by SHA-256 hashes, so all methods face identical scenes.

Appendix D
The foresight expert is removable
We verify the deployment claim of Sec. 3.3 with three tests, run for each foresight configuration (DINOv3
targets on both views or on the egocentric view only, and Qwen3-VL targets on both views). (i) Target
tampering: shuffling the future frames across the batch and rescaling them, or omitting them altogether,
leaves the generated action chunks bit-identical under fixed noise. (ii) Stripping: deleting Fω from the model
19


leaves the generated action chunks bit-identical. (iii) Loading: after the weights of Fω are removed (95 tensors
with two supervised views, 52 with one), the checkpoint loads into the deployment configuration, which does
not build Fω, with zero missing and zero unexpected parameters. Together these show that no part of the
action path reads Fω or the future frames, so foresight acts on the policy only through the trained weights of
the adapter and the exocentric projection.

Appendix F
Further discussion
Why not feed the predicted future to the policy?
An alternative is to inject the output of Fω into
the action expert as extra tokens. This makes Fω part of the inference graph, so it can no longer be removed,
and it lets the action expert rely on its own forecasts, whose errors may compound in closed loop. BeyondEgo
instead uses foresight only to shape the representation the policy reads.
Limitations.
Beyond the limitations in Sec. 5, the exocentric camera is calibrated and fixed in all
experiments, the deployed model retains the 4.2M-parameter adapter trained with the foresight loss, and the
benchmark covers one robot and five tasks in simulation.
Broader impact.
More reliable humanoid loco-manipulation can assist logistics and household work.
Exocentric cameras observe the environment and possibly people in it; deployments should follow privacy
regulations and inform the people being recorded.
