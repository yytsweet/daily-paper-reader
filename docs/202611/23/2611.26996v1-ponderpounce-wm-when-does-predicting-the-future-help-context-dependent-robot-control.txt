PonderPounce-WM: When Does Predicting the Future
Help Context-Dependent Robot Control?
Jaeyoon Jung1
Suhwan Choi1,2
Yunsung Lee1†
Youngjae Yu2†
1MAUM.AI
2Seoul National University
Abstract
How does future prediction affect context-dependent robot control? We intro-
duce PONDERPOUNCE-WM, extending PONDERPOUNCE with three future tokens
supervised to predict visual-feature changes. Its pretrained MLLM, PONDER, inte-
grates history and demonstrations into continuous cognition for the action model,
POUNCE. We investigate token ordering and delivery to distinguish predictive su-
pervision from direct future conditioning. On RoboMME, cognition-first ordering
with cognition-only delivery improves success from 60.83% to 67.42%. With the
same ordering, direct future-token delivery yields 64.00% success. Probes show
increased future information in cognition while the tested past-event attributes
remain largely decodable. Across four real-robot tasks, PONDERPOUNCE-WM
achieves 67.44% mean success under asynchronous execution, compared with
60.98% for PONDERPOUNCE. These results suggest that future prediction can im-
prove context-dependent control through auxiliary supervision without delivering
future tokens to the action model.

Introduction
Context-dependent robot control requires retaining past events that are no longer observable and
using demonstrations to interpret the current task. Predicting future observations may help learn such
control representations, but the information needed to anticipate visual changes need not coincide
with what an action model needs to act. Prior work combines observation history or explicit memory
with future prediction for control [1, 2, 3]. Building on PONDERPOUNCE’s use of a pretrained
MLLM’s native causal context as episode memory [4], we investigate how predictive supervision
shapes this continuous context representation, called cognition. We introduce PONDERPOUNCE-WM,
which adds future tokens supervised to predict visual-feature changes at multiple horizons, drawing
on FLARE [5] and InternW0-∆[6]. This retains the MLLM context engine rather than introducing a
video-generation backbone for pixel prediction.
PONDERPOUNCE-WM retains the dual-system architecture: PONDER integrates episode context, and
POUNCE generates actions conditioned on cognition. Our focus is how predictive supervision changes
a representation already responsible for episode memory: whether it improves control, whether direct
future conditioning adds a benefit, and whether past-event information remains decodable. We vary
the causal ordering of cognition and future tokens and whether POUNCE receives cognition alone
or also the future tokens. On RoboMME [7], future supervision with cognition-first ordering and
cognition-only delivery improves success from 60.83% to 67.42%, while direct future-token delivery
reduces the gain (Table 1). Future and past-event probes, together with inference-time token removal,
examine how predictive learning changes cognition and its utility for control. Across four real-robot
tasks under asynchronous execution, PONDERPOUNCE-WM achieves 67.44% mean success versus
60.98% for PONDERPOUNCE, supporting real-world applicability (Table 4).

PONDERPOUNCE-WM
We study world modeling as auxiliary prediction of future visual features from episode context, rather
than action-conditioned rollout for planning.
Base system.
PONDERPOUNCE [4] couples a pretrained MLLM, PONDER, which retains the task
instruction, demonstrations, and execution history in its native causal context, with an action model,
POUNCE. At query t, it appends observation Ot, optional text describing a new subgoal, and a learned
cognition token. This token’s final hidden state ct ∈RH, with MLLM width H, is the continuous
context representation passed to POUNCE. At invocation j, POUNCE combines its current observation,
proprioception, and task with the prefix [e∆(j), Wcecj] to predict an action chunk. Here Wc projects
the newest ready cognition ecj to the action model’s width, and e∆(j) encodes its age from the source
observation. POUNCE reuses this state until a newer PONDER output is ready. Both systems train end
to end: action flow-matching loss propagates through the prefix, while grounding token cross-entropy
enters through PONDER’s LM head. Action gradients into PONDER are scaled by 0.5, following the
base recipe.
Future prediction.
We add three learned future tokens Fi, indexed by slot i ∈{1, 2, 3},
giving K=4 tokens per query including cognition.
Each slot predicts at horizon hi, with
(h1, h2, h3) = (0.5, 1, 2) s in simulation and (1, 2, 3) s on the real robot (Appendix F). Let ϕ be
POUNCE’s frozen SigLIP encoder [8] and It the third-person image. The target is the feature change
∆t,h = pool4×4ϕ(It+h) −pool4×4ϕ(It), where pooling retains 16 spatial features. A shared linear
head maps the hidden state ft,i of slot Fi to b∆t,hi ∈R16×1152. The auxiliary loss Lfut is the mean
squared prediction error over valid query–horizon pairs, excluding targets beyond the episode end.
We optimize L = Lfm + 0.1Lground + 5Lfut. Future-loss gradients update PONDER’s shared param-
eters and, when future tokens attend to cognition, flow through cognition as well. Thus predictive
supervision can shape cognition without delivering future tokens to POUNCE. The prediction head is
not needed for action generation.
Token ordering and delivery.
The orders are [cog, F1, F2, F3] (cog-first) and [F1, F2, F3, cog]
(fut-first). Causal attention lets cognition read same-query future tokens only in fut-first. Both retain
previous queries’ tokens. Independently of order, POUNCE receives cognition alone or all four hidden
states through shared Wc, giving four configurations (Figure 1).

Experiments
Setup.
RoboMME [7] has 16 tabletop tasks requiring counting, event memory, or following
an in-context video demonstration, with 50 evaluation episodes each (800 per repeat). We use
Qwen3.5-9B [9] for PONDER and π0.5 [10] for POUNCE. All checkpoints follow the same base
PONDERPOUNCE training recipe [4], apart from the slot and supervision settings under study
(Appendix G). RoboMME success rates (%) average three closed-loop evaluations per checkpoint.
Main results.
Table 1 shows that future supervision with cog-first ordering and cognition-only
delivery reaches 67.42%, compared with 60.83% for PONDERPOUNCE (K=1) and 60.00% for four
slots without future supervision. The K=4 control disables future supervision and delivers all four
slots to POUNCE. All four serve as cognition tokens trained end to end through the action loss. Relative
to K=1, gains concentrate in Counting and Imitation, with a smaller improvement in Permanence
and little change in Reference. The largest gains occur on RouteStick and VideoUnmaskSwap, but
MoveCube and PickHighlight decline by 10 and 9 pt, respectively (Appendix A). Holding cog-first
ordering fixed, direct delivery of future slots lowers success to 64.00% (−3.42 pt). With fut-first
ordering, the corresponding difference is 64.17% to 63.17% (−1.00 pt). Direct delivery thus yields
a lower observed mean in both layouts, and the ordering difference is larger with cognition-only
delivery. Paired episode-bootstrap 95% intervals exclude zero for the cog-first delivery difference
and the improvement over the K=4 no-future-loss control, but include zero for the fut-first delivery
difference (Appendix B).

we fit linear probes to future-feature changes on 192 held-out episodes. Table 2 shows that 1 s
explained variance rises from 0.25 to 0.31–0.34 and near-time discrimination from 0.63 to 0.67–0.69.
Thus future information becomes more linearly decodable from cognition, even when future tokens
are not delivered to POUNCE. Removing execution history lowers explained variance at 1 s by
0.10–0.14 across variants, indicating that context contributes to these predictions. Probe scores do
not rank the variants in the same order as control success.

Real-robot evaluation.
We evaluate PONDERPOUNCE-WM (cog-first / cog) on the same four
RoboMME-inspired tasks and held-out scenes as the prior PONDERPOUNCE evaluation [7, 4], using
wall-clock asynchronous execution (Appendix F). Table 4 compares the new results with the reported
baselines: mean success increases from 60.98% to 67.44%, with gains on Put Fruits, Track Cube,
and Draw Pattern, but a decline on Repick Block (5/14 vs. 6/14). These results support real-world
applicability, but separate evaluation sessions and differing cognition ages prevent isolating predictive
supervision’s effect. With 14–16 trials per task, task-level comparisons remain imprecise.

Conclusion
PONDERPOUNCE-WM reaches 67.42% success on RoboMME and 67.44% across four real-robot
tasks, versus 60.83% and 60.98% for PONDERPOUNCE. Predictive supervision increases future-
feature decodability while largely retaining tested past-event attributes, with some location degra-
dation. Direct delivery yields lower observed success, while cog-first retains most gains without
future tokens at inference (Appendix D). These findings connect predictive learning and control
utility [12, 13, 14] in an episode-memory setting. Comparisons cover one architecture and future
targets from one encoder and camera view, without isolating future prediction from other auxiliary
visual objectives or establishing the delivery-penalty mechanism and broader memory preservation.
Within this scope, predictive supervision helps without delivering future tokens to the action model.

Paired bootstrap intervals for the main differences
Every evaluation repeat of every checkpoint runs the same 800 evaluation slots (16 tasks × 50
episode indices). For each slot we take a model’s success rate over its three repeats, form the per-slot
difference between two models, and resample the 800 slots with replacement (2,000 draws, the same
draws for every comparison) to obtain a percentile 95% interval of the mean difference (Table 6).
The intervals cover evaluation-slot sampling with repeat noise folded into the per-slot averages; they
do not cover training seeds, and are not adjusted for multiple comparisons. The published K=1
reference is available only as per-task percentages, so it is not paired here; its three repeats span
59.62–62.25% (Appendix H). The improvement over the K=4 no-future-loss control, the cog-first
delivery difference, and the ordering difference under cognition-only delivery exclude zero; the
fut-first delivery difference and the ordering difference under all-slot delivery do not. An interval
covering zero means the difference is not resolved by three repeats of 800 episodes, not that the
variants perform the same.

Past-event probe: protocol and uncertainty
Query selection.
We replay 100 held-out episodes per task through the serving path, retaining task
instructions, demonstrations sampled at 1 fps, and execution observations sampled at 1 Hz. PONDER
6


generates its own subgoal text, without ground-truth text in its context. We extract cognition at the
first query at or after each annotated subgoal transition. Recorded observations are shared across
checkpoints.
Targets and scoring.
We probe three past-event attributes:
• Hidden-container location. VideoUnmask uses the first execution query, and ButtonUn-
mask the first query at the transition from button pressing to container picking. Ridge
regression predicts the hidden container’s image coordinate from cognition, using the
recorded annotation as the target. A prediction is correct if its nearest container is the target.
Candidate positions use a calibrated world-to-image affine map. Chance averages the inverse
candidate count, and the constant reference predicts the mean target position.
• Repetition ordinal. PickXtimes and SwingXtimes queries are labelled by repetition index
(first, second, or third). SwingXtimes includes both legs of each swing. A ridge read-out
predicts one-hot labels and is scored by argmax.
• BinFill colour counts.
At each pick-subgoal transition, the label is the tuple
(nred, ngreen, nblue) of cubes already binned, identified from simulator actor identities,
colours, and disappearance after earlier visibility, including cubes introduced during exe-
cution. Each tuple is a class, and accuracy requires an exact tuple match. There are 269
queries from 100 episodes. Chance is uniform over tuples allowed by the task’s colour
targets, excluding the completed state.
Labels are used only for probe fitting and scoring. Counting tasks additionally use a majority-class
reference.
Read-out and references.
Ridge probes use three-fold cross-validation by episode, with penalties
selected on an inner training split. Inputs are cognition hidden states, excluding future slots. The
K=4 no-future-loss control averages its four cognition slots. The same procedure probes current-
frame SigLIP features (4×4 pooled) and each checkpoint’s cognition given only task instructions
and current observations, with no demonstrations or execution history. Group scores average tasks
equally.
Uncertainty.

Future-token removal at inference
Following work that separates predictive training from test-time future generation [12, 14], we
omit all three future tokens from PONDER without retraining. Both checkpoints were trained with
cognition-only delivery, so POUNCE’s input remains one cognition slot. Success changes from
67.42% to 66.17% for cog-first and from 64.17% to 8.75% for fut-first (Table 8). Cog-first retains
most of its gain, but removal changes the accumulated context and token positions in both layouts,
and additionally removes same-query future-token access in fut-first. The intervention therefore does
not isolate these sources of dependence.

Real-robot experiments
Platform and tasks.
We follow the prior PONDERPOUNCE setup [4], using the right arm of a
Trossen ALOHA Stationary AI Kit with external and wrist RGB views and proprioception. The
four RoboMME-inspired tasks [7] are PutFruits (transfer a specified number of fruits and press a
stop button), TrackCube (select the cup hiding the specified-colour cube), RepickBlock (pick blocks
in the demonstrated order), and DrawPattern (reproduce a demonstrated path). The latter three use
pre-execution demonstration videos.
Data and models.
The 710 teleoperated episodes have human-annotated subgoals but no demon-
stration reasoning. Training-episode/held-out-scene counts are 104/14, 195/16, 206/14, and 205/15
for the four tasks above. Evaluation scenes and demonstration clips are held out from training. The
first three rows of Table 4 are prior results [4]. We train PONDERPOUNCE-WM (cog-first / cog) with
the simulation recipe in Appendix G, using future horizons of 1, 2, and 3 s, subgoal-only grounding,
30-step action chunks, and a 16K training context.
Evaluation and timing.
PONDER and POUNCE target 1 Hz on separate H100 GPUs, with 30 Hz
action playback and a 131K serving context cap. POUNCE uses the latest ready cognition and its
age. A human judges success or failure under a two-minute limit. Each held-out scene contributes
one trial, and the reported average weights the four task rates equally. Table 10 pools server-side
timing samples across 59 trials per model. PONDER compute time includes transition-triggered text
decoding. Cognition age runs from its source observation to the POUNCE invocation using it, and
action observation age from the POUNCE observation to action delivery. Clock phase can affect
cognition age, so these deployment measurements do not isolate future-token overhead.

Training configuration
We follow the PONDERPOUNCE recipe [4] on RoboMME’s 1,587-episode base split with grounded
subgoals and demonstration reasoning. Training uses 4,320 steps, global batch 32 on 32 H100 GPUs,
learning rate 2×10−5 with 100 warm-up steps followed by a constant schedule, gradient clipping at
1.0, and ZeRO-2. Both backbones use public pretrained weights, with newly initialized carrier tokens
and projections. Training samples POUNCE invocations every 100 ms and PONDER queries every 1 s
with a simulated 300 ms delay. All four future-supervised configurations share the data, objectives,
schedule, and training seed. The K=4 control sets the future-loss weight to zero and delivers all four
slots to POUNCE. Real-robot differences are listed in Appendix F.
H
Compute and evaluation details
Simulation evaluation.
The K=1 reference uses the prior three-repeat result of 60.83% (range
59.62–62.25%) under the same serving protocol [4]. Both systems run at 1 Hz in simulated time with
a 300 ms cognition delay. POUNCE uses 10 flow-matching steps and plays 20-step chunks at 20 Hz,
starting after the first cognition is ready. Each checkpoint is evaluated three times on the same 50
episodes per task, capped at 1,300 environment steps.
Future-probe protocol and metrics.
Probes use 192 held-out episodes and 2,752 queries, exclud-
ing targets beyond episode end. Three-fold cross-validation splits by episode, with ridge penalties
selected on an inner training split. Explained variance is 1 −MSE(b∆, ∆)/ MSE(0, ∆), averaged
over held-out folds, with zero matching no-change prediction. The 1 s, no history condition removes
earlier execution observations, subgoals, and carrier tokens but retains the task, demonstrations,
current observations, and current-query grounding text. Near is two-way accuracy comparing the
predicted 1 s delta with the true target and a same-episode distractor offset by one or two queries. The
no-change predictor scores 0.51.
