Abstract
Visual labeling of egocentric demonstrations often leaves
contiguous gaps in action supervision.
Reliable poses
around a gap still provide its net translation and rota-
tion.
We use this information to train vision-language-
action policies while preserving ordinary flow matching on
reliable local actions. A composition objective constrains
the aggregate motion of missing-action estimates, combin-
ing multiple fixed windows when a gap exceeds the policy
horizon. A shared interval branch learns from reliable end-
points and supplies the composition target. On LIBERO
with 20% of demonstrations and 70.5% missing actions, the
main method achieves 87.9% mean success over three train-
ing runs. In one paired comparison with matched observa-
tion exposure and training cost, its advantage over mask-
ing is 10.5%. On Ego-informed RoboTwin gaps, success
is 45.4%, versus 40.3% for masking. On five real-robot
tasks, mean success is 59.6%, versus 54.8% for interval
flow matching alone and 48.8% for masking.
1. Introduction
Egocentric demonstrations can supplement robot teleoper-
ation [8], but visual motion labels can become unreliable
under occlusion or tracking failure. HOT3D benchmarks
this hand and object tracking problem [1]. Filtering unreli-
able estimates leaves contiguous gaps in action supervision.
Figure 1(a) illustrates how these gaps can affect either hand
independently while visual observations remain available,
using constructed pose offsets on real Ego frames. How can
a vision-language-action (VLA) policy [2] learn from par-
tial action labels and partly observed interval motion?
We study end-effector pose increments:
the policy
learns stepwise translation and rotation, while reliable poses
around a gap specify their net change. Mapped Ego trajec-
tories and robot demonstrations share this supervision struc-

ture. Each interval requires reliable endpoints in a common
frame and its starting observation.
Common treatments use only part of this supervision
structure. Holding the last pose creates zero motion fol-
lowed by a reacquisition jump. Interpolation assigns point-
wise targets along an assumed interior path. Masking re-
moves unreliable targets, but also leaves the known net mo-
tion unused. We instead retain local supervision wherever
labels are reliable and use endpoint motion to constrain the
missing segment as a whole.
The training objective follows the available information
at two temporal scales (Figure 1(b)). Reliable local actions
keep their original flow-matching (FM) targets. Within a
gap, we compose estimated pose increments in temporal or-
der and constrain the resulting net motion. This leaves the
individual missing actions free to depend on the observa-
tion and the pretrained policy. Long gaps share a constraint
across fixed windows, each with its own observation.
We implement interval supervision with a shared action–
interval expert. Its interval branch learns net motion from
reliable endpoints and supplies features to action genera-
tion. Our main method uses a stopped-gradient interval pre-
diction as the composition target, with a linear reference in
the missing-action inputs. The missing actions receive ag-
gregate supervision rather than pointwise interpolation tar-
gets. Observed endpoints provide comparison targets.
Our contribution is a training formulation that connects
reliable local supervision to interval motion, including in-
tervals that span multiple policy windows.
Controlled
LIBERO experiments test composition, interval prediction,
and increasing gap severity. RoboTwin transfers bimanual
missingness patterns from Ego data. A five-task real-robot
comparison evaluates learning from robot and visually la-
beled Ego demonstrations with natural labeling gaps.

3.5. Shared model and attention
Actions and intervals use separate tokens in a shared pre-
trained action expert (Figure 2). Pretrained action projec-
tions are retained, while new interval projections embed
noised motion and its duration. Tokens DH and DX pre-
dict interval velocities for their respective durations. The
main view pairs actions with DH at Ct. Auxiliary forwards
share weights and use the exact start observation Cu. Sup-
plementary Sec. B gives tensor shapes and embeddings.
Missing-action inputs.

missing-action tokens in normalized action space.
With
Gaussian noise ϵi, zero reference uses xτ,i = τϵi, and pure
noise uses xτ,i = ϵi. For linear reference, endpoint interpo-
lation with a normalized-zero gripper yields eai. Its input is
xτ,i = (1 −τ)eai + τϵi. Zero reference denotes zero in nor-
malized action space. Pointwise action FM supervises re-
liable positions, whose inputs retain the ordinary FM path.
All three keep missing action keys readable, so their context
affects reliable predictions.
Figure 3 shows the attention mask. Action queries read
the observation prefix, all action keys, and a valid main DH
with q = (0, H). Interval queries read only their own pre-
fix and themselves. Auxiliary interval calls provide no ac-
tion features. The interval-FM-only baseline instead blocks
missing action keys and zeroes their inputs.
3.6. Joint inference at a fixed horizon
At deployment, independent Gaussian action and interval
states are jointly denoised over ten Euler steps with q =
(0, H). All main tokens are active, and actions read interval
features at each step. The integration-step count matches
the LIBERO baseline (supplementary Sec. B). Deployment
requires only the current observation, with variable-length
auxiliary calls confined to training.

4.2. Compared methods and information access
Hold repeats the last reliable pose before computing labels,
including rotation. Its FM input and target both use the re-
sulting actions. Linear interpolates translation and rotation
between endpoints, then uses the completed actions as both
FM inputs and targets. Mask excludes missing keys and
losses. Interval FM only adds main and auxiliary interval
prediction to this blocked-key baseline. The composition
variants retain missing keys and cross the three inputs in
Section 3.5 with four constraint choices. Linear reference
supplies a missing-action FM input without a pointwise tar-
get. Unless otherwise stated, Ours in the main performance
comparisons denotes linear-reference inputs with predicted-
interval composition over bridges. Observed-D and D-free
variants isolate its components.
The base comparisons share demonstrations, ordinary
sampling, gap manifests, initialization, and evaluator. Aux-
iliary interval views and grouped action views change expo-
sure and computation. Section 4.5 specifies these controls.

4.5. Contribution of the interval branch
A 2 × 2 comparison separates interval prediction from ob-
served bridge composition (Table 2). All four variants use
zero-reference inputs and readable missing keys. Adding
composition to action-only supervision raises success from
78.8% to 84.5%. Adding interval prediction alone gives
80.3%, while combining both gives 87.8%. The interval
branch adds 3.3% when composition is present, compared
with 1.5% without it.
At the same training budget, the branch-removal compar-
ison also covers pure-noise and linear inputs (supplemen-
tary Table S4). Retaining D improves success by 4.1% and
0.3%, respectively. The smaller linear-reference difference
shows that the benefit depends on the missing-input con-
struction. D-free removes the interval token, projections,
loss, and dedicated interval-start observation forward.

5. Discussion and Limitations
Endpoints constrain net motion, while reliable local labels
supervise interior actions. Their quality depends on visual
estimates and the mapping to robot control. Tracking fail-
ures can bias endpoints and require interpolated Ego state
inputs. Contact and controller tracking errors can also sep-
arate commanded from measured motion.
6. Related Work
Generative policies and auxiliary prediction.
Flow
matching learns a velocity field along a probability
path [11], and π0.5 uses a generative action expert [2].
PAD jointly denoises images and actions [6]. UMA jointly
models 3D object motion and actions with available-label
masks [3]. SUAVE uses masked video/action tokens, in-
cluding action-free co-training [14]. ELAN4D supervises
an action branch with robot keypoint tracks and discards its
track decoder at inference [7]. ForesightFlow predicts suc-
cess potential for policy improvement and candidate rank-
ing [13]. Our interval branch predicts physical endpoint
motion and constrains the aggregate output of missing ac-
tions across fixed windows.
Incomplete action supervision.
Behavioral cloning from
observation infers actions through inverse dynamics [15],
while latent-action pretraining learns transitions from
video [16]. EgoMimic aligns human and robot data for co-
training [8]. We address label gaps after this alignment:
reliable actions coexist with observed motion over missing
intervals. Ambient Diffusion learns from corrupted mea-
surements [5]. Flow Matching with Missing Data averages
over posterior completions [9], with complete-data equiva-
lence requiring the true completion law. Our objective in-
stead combines local action FM, observed-interval FM, and
an action-composition constraint.

7. Conclusion
Reliable local labels and observed endpoint motion provide
complementary supervision. Our method retains action FM,
constrains missing-action composition, and combines fixed
windows for long gaps. Matched-observation LIBERO con-
trols and Ego-informed simulation support this formulation,
while five real-robot tasks show its value for learning from
visually labeled demonstrations.

Bimanual labels and model.
Actions contain two six-
dimensional pose increments and two gripper commands,
with per-coordinate, per-arm masks. A missing pose inval-
idates both adjacent differences. The bimanual suffix has
50 joint-action tokens and two six-dimensional interval to-
kens with side identifiers and a shared head. Each interval
reads its starting observation without access to action keys.
Actions can read main-interval tokens. Bridge targets and
composition are arm-specific.
At nominal 0.06 s spacing, the 50-action horizon (H50)
spans 3 s.
Of 5,295 complete bridges, 30 require two
bridge-aligned windows. The 472 boundary-open gaps have
no interval target. Valid endpoints exist for 34.7% of these
per-arm interval slots. Grouped actions share one computa-
tion graph and one optimizer update. The other arm’s reli-
able labels remain active. Each method uses 5,000 updates
and 800 evaluation episodes.
Closed-loop comparisons.
Clean restores labels on the
same training demonstrations.
Other methods share one
bridge manifest. Hold/Linear use completed poses for FM
inputs and targets, including rotation interpolation for Lin-
ear. Mask and interval-only models block missing action
keys. The composition model uses readable keys and linear-
reference missing inputs. Composition targets are stopped-
gradient interval predictions. A separate control uses ob-
served endpoint motion. Initialization, ordinary sampling,
controller, updates, and evaluation conditions are common.
Interval and grouped-action views add observations to the
ordinary action batch.
