Abstract
Imitation learning for surgical robots is limited by the
cost of collecting demonstrations. Meanwhile, many robot-
assisted procedures are recorded as stereo video, yet poli-
cies cannot learn from them directly; clinical video carries
no action labels. We present FlowScope, which pretrains a
surgical policy network on unlabeled clinical video by fore-
casting scene flow, the metric 3D motion of scene points,
with labels computed by off-the-shelf stereo depth and opti-
cal flow models. We pretrain the trunk of an Action Chunk-
ing Transformer (ACT) on a dataset of 1,149 clinical pro-
cedures (1.35M clips) and discard the flow head afterward,
so the deployed policy is a standard ACT with no added in-
ference cost. On a real surgical robotic system, FlowScope
raises needle pickup success from 54% to 90% and first-
handover success on needle regrasp from 4% to 52% (full
task: 2% to 14%) over training from scratch (n = 50 trials
each), and retains its gains with a generative flow-matching
action head. Ablations indicate that metric 3D motion is the
strongest pretraining signal among those tested; kinemat-
ics, 2D tracks, depth alone, and scale-free 3D flow, each
paired with kinematics, yield smaller or no gains. Repre-
sentation analyses suggest that scene-flow pretraining en-
codes metric needle depth more accurately, that kinematics
pretraining increases reliance on the robot state, and that
needle-depth probes follow the ordering of real-robot suc-
cess where offline validation error does not.
1. Introduction
Robot-assisted surgery is a promising but underexplored
frontier for robot learning. Automating even routine su-
turing subtasks, such as needle pickup or needle handover,
could reduce surgeon fatigue and ultimately improve patient
outcomes. Imitation learning has recently made dexterous
behaviors like these tractable in general-purpose manipu-
lation [3, 11, 48], following a simple recipe: collect more
demonstrations, train better policies. In surgical robotics,
the first half of this recipe is the bottleneck.
Surgical demonstrations are scarce and expensive. Each
one requires an expert operator, often an experienced
robotic surgeon, a carefully prepared physical setup, and
time on a costly system. Simulation offers a potential al-
ternative, but accurately modeling deformable tissues and
realistic endoscopic scenes remains challenging, limiting
reliable transfer of learned policies to physical surgical
robots [17, 28, 39].
Pretraining offers another way to reduce the demonstra-
tion burden. It has transformed language [14, 36, 37] and
vision [20, 31, 41]. Robotics has followed, from the RT
series [4, 5, 32] to vision-language-action models built on
web-scale data [3, 35]. The natural next step is to fine-tune
these generalist models on surgical tasks. Yet vision mod-
els pretrained on general-domain images transfer poorly to
surgical scenes [13, 19, 34], and the robot data behind gen-
eralist policies looks nothing like the inside of a patient.
Indeed, in our experiments, a fine-tuned π0.5 trails FlowS-
cope on both surgical tasks (Sec. 4). Surgical policies need
surgical pretraining data.
Such data already exists at scale. Many robot-assisted
procedures are recorded through an endoscope, producing a
large and growing corpus of video from the domain a policy
will be deployed in. This video, however, cannot serve as
demonstrations directly. Imitation learning needs action la-
bels, but clinical video carries none; the kinematics of clin-
ical systems are rarely available outside the manufacturer.
Even when they are available, they are offset from where
the instruments appear in the image, due to errors in the for-
ward kinematics and calibration [26, 47]. Actions recovered
from them do not match what the policy sees.
What, then, should a surgical policy learn from unlabeled
stereo video? Our answer is motion. What a surgeon does
is visible in how the scene moves: the instruments sweep
toward the needle, the needle lifts from the tissue, and the
tissue deforms under traction. Because the endoscope is
stereo, this motion can be estimated in metric 3D with off-
the-shelf stereo depth and optical flow models. The result-

ing scene flow is an action-like signal that is read directly
from the pixels, needs no robot labels, and does not inherit
the errors of the kinematics.
We present FlowScope, a pretraining recipe for surgi-
cal policies built on scene flow. Using off-the-shelf stereo
depth [43] and optical flow [42] models, we convert raw
surgical video into metric 3D trajectories of points on tis-
sue, needles, and instruments. We then pretrain the trunk of
an Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) model to take the
endoscope image as input and predict the 3D positions and
future trajectories of queried points. Pretraining adds only
a set of point queries and a lightweight flow head, both dis-
carded afterward, so the fine-tuned model is a standard ACT
policy and FlowScope requires no changes at deployment.
We pretrain FlowScope with 1,149 clinical procedures,
about 1.35M training clips, and fine-tune it on two sub-
tasks on a minimally invasive surgical robotic system: nee-
dle pickup and needle regrasp. On both tasks, FlowScope
substantially improves real-robot success over training from
scratch: from 54% to 90% on pickup, and from 4% to 52%
on the first handover of regrasp. It also outperforms Dif-
fusion Policy [11] and the finetuned vision-language-action
model π0.5 [35]. The gains are not tied to ACT’s regression
head; they persist when we replace it with a generative flow-
matching head. Pretraining also improves data efficiency:
with half of the demonstrations, FlowScope matches a pol-
icy trained from scratch on all of them on needle pickup
(52% vs. 54%) and surpasses it on needle regrasp (32% vs.
4% first-handover success).
Two findings stand out. First, scene flow alone is a better
pretraining signal than robot kinematics for surgical robot
learning. We expected the kinematics recorded alongside
the video to be useful supervision, since they describe ex-
actly what a policy must output. Yet pretraining on kine-
matics gives little to no gain over training from scratch, and
sometimes hurts; adding kinematics to scene-flow pretrain-
ing lowers success. Second, metric 3D scene flow outper-
forms its alternatives based on 2D point tracks, depth-only
supervision, and scale-free 3D flow. Metric scale, which the
stereo endoscope provides, is what makes FlowScope work.
To understand why,
we look inside the policies.
Kinematics-pretrained policies devote nearly three times as
much visual attention to the robot-state input as other poli-
cies, and keep doing so after fine-tuning. Flow-pretrained
policies instead encode the needle’s metric depth more ac-
curately than any other initialization. FlowScope teaches
the policy to locate what matters in 3D from visual input
only.
In summary, our contributions are:
• FlowScope, a recipe that pretrains surgical policy net-
works to forecast metric 3D scene flow from unlabeled
clinical stereo video, requiring no robot labels and no
changes to the deployed policy.
• A real-robot evaluation on two surgical tasks, showing
that FlowScope improves success and data efficiency over
training from scratch, kinematics pretraining, and strong
baselines, with both regression and generative heads.
• Controlled ablations showing that the benefit comes from
metric 3D motion: 2D tracks, depth alone, and scale-free
3D flow give smaller or no gains when added to kinemat-
ics, and kinematics alone are a weaker signal than scene
flow.
• Representation analyses showing that flow pretraining
improves the policy’s encoding of metric needle depth,
that kinematics pretraining shifts attention toward the
robot state, and that such probes, unlike offline validation
error, track real-robot success.
2. Related Work
Visuomotor imitation learning.
Action chunking with
transformers (ACT) [48] and Diffusion Policy [11] made
imitation learning practical for dexterous manipulation.
Vision-language-action models [3, 35] go further: they pre-
train on large robot and web datasets and adapt to a new
task with a few hours of demonstrations. Most of these
policies build on a pretrained visual backbone, and frozen
self-supervised encoders such as DINOv2 [31] are a com-
mon choice [1]. FlowScope keeps this design and changes
only what the policy is pretrained on.
Learning policies from video motion.
Point tracks and
flow are a popular interface between action-free video
and control.
ATM [45] predicts 2D tracks of arbitrary
points and conditions a policy on them; TraceGen [27],
3PoinTr [24], and PointZero [15] predict object or scene
trajectories; and ARM4R [30] and SPA [49] pretrain 3D-
and 4D-aware representations for embodied learning. In
surgery, SurgFlow [8] learns from stereo video of teleop-
erated dVRK demonstrations of each task.
It segments
the target object, predicts the object’s future 3D flow and
where the tool should make contact, and converts these into
actions with an optimization-based controller and contact-
gated grasping. FlowScope differs from these methods in
three ways. It pretrains on clinical video from many pro-
cedures, rather than on video of the target task. It predicts
metric 3D flow over the whole scene, rather than 2D tracks,
unscaled flow from monocular video, or segmented objects,
so it needs no object masks. And flow is only a pretrain-
ing target: the deployed policy is a standard learned policy
that maps images to actions, with no flow prediction or con-
troller at test time.
What makes visual representations useful for control?
Burns et al. [6] found that how well a ViT’s attention seg-
ments objects predicts out-of-distribution manipulation suc-
cess better than generic downstream accuracy.
Wen et
al. [46] trace the strengths and failures of pretrained vi-
sion models for robot learning to whether they form object-

Surgical robot learning.
Research platforms such as
the dVRK [25] enabled imitation learning for surgical sub-
tasks. The Surgical Robot Transformer [26] showed that
relative action parameterizations mitigate the kinematic er-
ror of cable-driven arms. Clinical recordings, in turn, have
been used widely for surgical perception, including phase
recognition, segmentation, and depth estimation [19]. Re-
cent work has begun to bring this video into policy learning,
by turning it into actions. SurgVIL [10] estimates tool kine-
matics from monocular clinical video and co-trains poli-
cies on these pseudo-actions together with robot demonstra-
tions, evaluating needle pickup on a real robot. Cosmos-H-
Surgical [21] fine-tunes a video world model on annotated
surgical clips, labels its generated videos with an inverse
dynamics model, and uses them as extra training data, but
reports offline trajectory error rather than real-robot roll-
outs.
Surgical WAM [2] pretrains a world-action model
on action-free video and evaluates it only in simulation.
FlowScope differs in what the policy learns from video.
Instead of actions, estimated or generated, it predicts the
metric 3D motion of the whole scene, which stereo video
measures directly and which needs neither action labels nor
video generation. Furthermore, our experiments show that
pretraining on the recorded kinematics, which are at least
as accurate as kinematics estimated from video, gives no
consistent gain in our real-robot evaluations, while leading
the policy to rely on the robot state rather than the visual
observations (Sec. 5).
3. Method
FlowScope learns a visuomotor policy in two stages, shown
in Fig. 1. First, we pretrain the policy’s transformer trunk on
clinical surgical video, using only a pretext task that needs
no robot actions: predicting where points in the scene are
in 3D and how they will move. Second, we fine-tune the
pretrained trunk on a small set of robot demonstrations, ei-
ther with the standard ACT recipe or with a generative flow-
matching action head.
3.1. Problem Setup
A policy πθ(at:t+H | ot, st) maps the left endoscope im-
age ot and the robot state st to a chunk of H future actions.
Unlike tabletop setups, which often add wrist cameras, min-
imally invasive surgery offers a single viewpoint: the endo-
scope. We use only its left image, so stereo is needed only
to construct pretraining targets and the deployed policy re-
mains single-view; feeding both views to the policy did not
improve success and doubles the cost (Sec. H.2). The stan-
dard approach trains πθ from scratch on a dataset D of tele-

operated demonstrations of the target task, such as needle

trunk of πθ so that imitation learning on D becomes more
effective. As the pretraining target we use metric 3D scene
flow: for a queried point in the current frame, predict its 3D
position and its 3D displacement over time. This target is
computed from video alone, so pretraining uses neither the
robot state nor any actions.
3.2. 3D Motion Targets from Clinical Video
Pretraining corpus.
We use stereo endoscope recordings
of clinical procedures performed with a minimally invasive
surgical robotic system: 1,175 procedures, split by video
into 1,149 training and 26 validation videos (Fig. 2). All
videos were thoroughly de-identified to ensure patient pri-
vacy, and data was collected with consent from anonymized
surgical procedures, under our IRB-equivalent institutional
review process. From each video we take candidate anchor
frames every 10 frames, each paired with the scene’s motion
over the following 100 frames (1.7 s). We keep an anchor
only if the endoscope stays still over this window (within
0.5 mm and 0.5◦), so that all labeled motion comes from
tissue and instruments rather than the camera, and if the sur-
geon drives at least one instrument for the whole window.
We read both conditions from the logged kinematics, which
serve only this selection step: they are never an input or a
target during pretraining. After filtering, the corpus yields
1.35M anchors.
Metric depth and optical flow.
We estimate dense
depth from disparity for every stereo pair with Fast-
FoundationStereo [43] and dense optical flow between con-
secutive keyframes with SEA-RAFT [42]; both generalize
well to surgical images [18, 40]. Pseudo-labels from both
models can be wrong at occlusion boundaries, on specular
highlights, and on textureless tissue, so we use a predic-
tion only if it is self-consistent: disparity estimated from
the left and from the right image must agree within 1 px,
and flow followed forward and then backward must return
within 1 px (plus 5% of its magnitude). These checks re-
move regions seen by only one eye and points that become
occluded, for example when an instrument passes over tis-
sue. Sec. A.3 lists all filters with their thresholds.
3D point trajectories.
At each anchor frame we track a
grid of pixels through the next K=10 keyframes by chain-
ing the keyframe-to-keyframe flow, and lift every tracked
pixel to a 3D point in the camera frame using the depth
at that keyframe.
This gives each point a start position
P0 ∈R3 and displacements ∆Pk = Pk−P0, k = 1, . . . , K,
in millimeters.
A point stops being tracked at the first
keyframe where it fails a check, so each (point, keyframe)
pair carries its own validity flag. Since most of a surgical
scene is static in any given second, uniform sampling would
mostly teach the model that nothing moves. We therefore
store 512 points per anchor, 256 sampled uniformly and 256
with probability proportional to how far they move, and at
training time use 256 of them as queries, up to half drawn
from points that move more than 5 mm.
3.3. Model and Pretraining
Overview.
We build on ACT [48], a DETR-style [7]
encoder-decoder transformer widely used for imitation
learning. We keep ACT’s architecture unchanged so that
the pretrained weights transfer directly, and change only
what it is trained to predict. During pretraining, the net-
work receives a single image and a set of point queries, and
predicts the metric 3D position and future 3D trajectory of
each queried point. Everything that depends on robot data
is inactive: the robot-state token and the CVAE latent token
are set to zero, and ACT’s action queries and action head
receive no loss. We keep these parts only so that the archi-
tecture matches ACT exactly at fine-tuning time.
Image tokenizer.
Surgical vision foundation models out-
perform general-purpose ones on surgical tasks [19]. We
therefore train a DINOv2 ViT-L/14 [31] on surgical video
(details and comparison studies in Sec. C). It stays frozen in
every stage of FlowScope and acts as a tokenizer, mapping
the left image at 518×518 to 37×37 = 1,369 patch tokens,
which the transformer encoder processes together with the
inactive state and latent tokens.

point. For a point at image position u, the query is

3.4. Fine-tuning and Deployment
From pretraining to a policy.
We initialize the policy
from the pretrained checkpoint with the lowest validation
flow error. For fine-tuning, we remove the point queries
and the flow head, and switch the robot-specific parts of
ACT back on: the robot state enters through the state token,
and the CVAE encoder produces the latent from the action
chunk. These parts and the action head are randomly initial-
ized, exactly as in an ACT trained from scratch; the encoder,
the decoder, and the input projections start from pretraining.
We then train the whole policy on D with ACT’s standard
L1 and KL objective, keeping the image tokenizer frozen.
Action representation.
Following prior work [26], we
express all kinematics in the camera frame and act in end-
effector Cartesian space. For each arm, an action is a trans-
lation relative to the previous step, an absolute rotation (a
3×3 rotation matrix), and an absolute gripper angle: 13 di-
mensions per arm, 26 for both. The policy predicts a chunk
of H=30 actions covering 2 s.
Generative action head.
To test whether the pretrained
trunk also helps a generative policy, we replace ACT’s
CVAE and action head with a flow-matching action head
on the same trunk, in the spirit of Diffusion Policy [11] and
the action expert of π0 [3]. The head is a 4-block trans-
former with adaptive layer normalization [33] that denoises
the whole 30-step chunk, conditioned on the decoder’s out-
put for each step, and samples a chunk with 10 Euler steps.
It is trained from scratch during fine-tuning on top of any
pretrained trunk (Sec. E.1).
Deployment.
At test time the policy is a standard ACT (or
ACT with the flow-matching head): it receives only the left
endoscope image and the robot state, and needs no depth,
flow, or other pseudo-labels.
4. Experiments
We evaluate FlowScope on a real surgical robotic sys-
tem and ask three questions. Does pretraining on clinical
video improve real-robot success, with either action head
(Sec. 4.2)? Does it reduce the number of demonstrations
needed (Sec. 4.3)? And which part of the pretraining target
is responsible for the gain (Sec. 4.4)?
4.1. Experimental setup
Tasks.
We evaluate two suturing subtasks of increasing
difficulty. In needle pickup, the dominant (right) instrument
grasps a suture needle lying on the pad and lifts it. Needle
regrasp starts the same way, but the needle is then handed
to the non-dominant instrument and back to the dominant
one. Surgeons perform this regrasp to bring the needle into
a good pose for driving it through tissue, which is the next
step of suturing. Regrasp therefore requires precise coordi-
nation of both arms over a long sequence, and we score it
in three stages: pickup, the first handover, and the second
handover, which completes the task.
Robot and data.
All experiments run on a minimally in-
vasive surgical robotic system, with a suture pad placed in-
side a mock abdominal cavity (Fig. S3). We collected 898
teleoperated demonstrations of needle pickup and 2,255 of
needle regrasp, including DAgger [38] data. Additional de-
tails are in the supplementary material (Sec. A). Every pol-
icy, baselines included, is fine-tuned on the same demon-
strations and evaluated on the same physical setup.
Evaluation protocol.
Real-robot success varies across
checkpoints and validation loss does not predict rollout per-
formance, so we evaluate each policy at five fine-tuning
checkpoints (every 20k steps up to 100k) on the same 10
predefined needle configurations (Fig. S4), with 50 trials
per policy. For pickup, the policy may make up to three at-
tempts per trial, and we report success on the first attempt
and within three attempts. For regrasp, the policy gets a
single attempt, and we report the fraction of trials that com-
plete each stage, together with the mean number of stages
completed (from 0 to 3). Additional details are in Sec. B.
Implementation.
Pretraining runs for 100k steps on 4
H200 GPUs with 128 samples per GPU. Fine-tuning runs
for 100k steps on a single 40 GB A100 with a batch size
of 128, photometric augmentation, and noise added to the
robot state (1.5 mm and 3◦) to improve robustness against
noisy measurements. Both stages use a base learning rate
of 10−5, scaled with the square root of the batch size, and

4.2. Does pretraining on clinical video help?
Table 1 summarizes the main results.
Baselines leave a large gap.
Trained from scratch, ACT
picks up the needle within three attempts in only 54% of tri-
als, and Diffusion Policy in 62%. π0.5 behaves differently:
it has the highest first-attempt success among the baselines
(42% with full fine-tuning), but it never recovers once its
first attempt fails, so its success stays at 42% after three at-
tempts. LoRA and full fine-tuning perform about the same,
even though full fine-tuning uses twice the GPUs. On re-
grasp, every baseline struggles to get past the pickup stage:
at most 20% of trials reach the first handover, and none
completes more than 2% of trials.
Scene-flow pretraining helps; kinematics pretraining
does not.
With the ACT head, FlowScope raises pickup
success from 54% to 90% and first-attempt success from
22% to 54%. The gain grows with the difficulty of the task.
On regrasp, FlowScope reaches the first handover in 52%
of trials, compared with 4% for ACT from scratch and 12%
for kinematics pretraining, and it is the best policy at ev-
ery stage, including full-task completion (14%). In paired
comparisons on regrasp, FlowScope outperforms ACT from
scratch in 31 trials and underperforms in 4 (p < 10−5).
Kinematics pretraining, in contrast, barely helps (60%
pickup success, against 54% from scratch), even though its
pretraining labels are actions in exactly the format of the
demonstrations. In our setting, pretraining therefore helps
when the target is the scene’s 3D motion, but not when it
is standard behavior cloning on the surgeon’s recorded mo-
tion.
The gain holds for a generative action head.
Replac-
ing ACT’s head with the flow-matching action head leaves
the conclusion unchanged. With this head, FlowScope suc-
ceeds at pickup in 90% of trials, against 58% from scratch
and 56% with kinematics pretraining. On regrasp, it reaches
the first handover in 40% of trials, against 20% from scratch
and 4% with kinematics pretraining. Here kinematics pre-
training is worse than no pretraining, which suggests that
predicting recorded kinematics can teach the trunk habits
that hurt downstream performance; we return to this in
Sec. 5. Full-task completion remains low for every policy
(at most 14%); even with 2,255 demonstrations, regrasp is
challenging for all the methods we evaluate.
4.3. Does pretraining reduce the number of demon-
strations needed?
A main benefit of pretraining is that it reduces how much
labeled data a downstream task needs [9, 22].
We fine-
tune each policy on 50% of the demonstrations for each
task. All policies use the same subset, and its validation
demonstrations are unchanged. We compare training from
scratch, kinematics pretraining, and FlowScope, and keep
every other setting identical, including the 100k fine-tuning
steps. The three policies are fit with the ACT head, on the
same configurations and checkpoints as in Tab. 1.
Figure 3 shows the results. With half the demonstrations,

4.4. What in the video helps?
The previous sections show that scene-flow pretraining
helps. We now ask which part of the target matters. Table 2
compares eight pretraining targets, all computed from the
same video anchors and tracked points, so that only the tar-
get changes. Each pretrained trunk is fine-tuned on needle
pickup with the ACT head. To evaluate this many models on
the robot, we roll out two checkpoints (40k and 60k steps)

success is a good proxy, since the policies that do well on
pickup also do well on regrasp.
The targets vary along three axes, each changing one fac-

To measure how much scene geom-

features to predict needle masks, instrument masks, and
stereo disparity (details in Sec. G.3). We report the relative

Depth labels come from Fast-FoundationStereo [43], the
same model used to build the scene-flow pretraining tar-
gets, so flow pretraining could in principle favor these la-
bels. However, the probe frames are robot demonstrations,
for which the trunks never saw stereo labels during pretrain-
ing. For reference, we also probe the frozen DINOv2 tokens
that every trunk takes as input; these are wider (1024-d in-
stead of 512-d), limiting direct comparison.
Flow pretraining builds metric needle geometry, fine-
tuning maintains it.
Among the pretrained trunks, the
flow-only trunk of FlowScope encodes the scene best on
every probe (Tab. 3). It is also the only trunk that encodes
the needle’s depth better than the DINOv2 features it takes
as input (0.08 vs. 0.10 abs rel), even though it has half as
many channels.
Pretraining with kinematics has the op-
posite effect: kinematics-only pretraining encodes needle
depth worse than its own input (0.13 vs. 0.10), while adding
flow restores it to the input’s level.
Fine-tuning on the pickup demonstrations removes some
of this information from every trunk, but the ranking re-
mains consistent. After fine-tuning, the FlowScope policy
still encodes the needle’s depth best (0.11), ahead of kin. +
flow (0.13) and of scratch and kinematics, which are tied
(0.14 and 0.15). The ranking on needle depth (flow only >
kin. + flow > scratch and kinematics) is also the ranking
of real-robot success in Tabs. 1 and 2, whereas offline ac-
tion error on held-out demonstrations is nearly identical for
all four policies (Sec. G.2). Consistent with prior work [6],
how well a representation captures the task-relevant object
is associated with how well the policy performs across the
four initializations we compare.
Kinematics pretraining teaches the trunk to rely on the
robot state rather than the scene.
Besides the image to-
kens, ACT’s encoder receives a token for the robot state,
the current end-effector pose of both instruments. Imitation
policies that predict actions from such proprioceptive input
are known to rely on it. Although the detriment is indis-
tinguishable offline, these models fail at rollout and closed
loop [12, 44]. We therefore measure how much of the image
tokens’ attention goes to the state token (Sec. G.1). After
kinematics pretraining, the image tokens give about a quar-
ter of their attention to it (24.5%), and the habit survives
fine-tuning (21.3%, compared with about 8% for scratch
and FlowScope). Flow pretraining cannot learn this short-
cut, since its state input is zero. We therefore attribute the
weakness of kinematics pretraining to this shortcut rather
than to the kinematic target itself; pretraining on kinematics
with the state masked is a control we leave to future work.
6. Conclusion
We present FlowScope, a method that pretrains surgical pol-
icy networks on clinical video by forecasting how points
in the scene move in metric 3D. Fine-tuned and evalu-
ated on a minimally invasive surgical robot, FlowScope im-
proves needle pickup and needle regrasp over training from
scratch, with both a deterministic and a generative action
head.
The pretraining target matters: metric 3D motion
helps, while the recorded kinematics do not. Flow pretrain-
ing builds a trunk that encodes the needle’s 3D geometry,
whereas kinematics pretraining teaches it to read the robot
state instead of the scene.
Limitations.
We evaluate on a single platform and on su-
ture pads; transfer to other robots and to ex vivo or in vivo
tissue remain as future work. Our labels are pseudo-labels
that inherit the errors of the stereo and optical-flow mod-
els, and absolute success on the full regrasp task is still low.
We compare pretraining targets within one architecture, not
against other video-pretraining systems such as SurgVIL or
Surgical WAM, whose data and platforms differ from ours.
We see FlowScope as a first step toward policies that learn
from the vast record of surgeries already performed.

A. Clinical video and scene-flow labels
A.1. Clinical video pool
In collaboration and partnerships with various hospitals, we
curate a dataset of recordings from a minimally invasive sur-
gical robotic system containing real surgeries on patients.
We sampled 1,296 procedure videos; 121 of them yield no
anchor that passes the filters (for example, the camera never
stays still while an instrument is driven), leaving 1,175 pro-
cedures, split by video into 1,149 training and 26 validation
videos so that no validation frame comes from a training
procedure. Of the 2.69M candidate anchors (one every 10
frames with a full 1.7 s window after it), 1.35M (50%) pass
the anchor filters; the median video keeps 59% of its candi-
dates.
Ethics and broader impact statement.
Our work con-
cerns pretraining methods for surgical robot autonomy, a
safety-critical domain in which policy errors could harm pa-
tients. All robot experiments in this paper are performed on
suture pads in a laboratory setting; no policy was deployed
on humans or animals. FlowScope is not a clinical system:
the success rates we report, while substantially improved,
remain far below what clinical use would require, and any
translation would require extensive validation, and regula-
tory approval.
The clinical videos were recorded during routine robot-
assisted procedures, with patient consent for the use of their
data, and were obtained under consent agreements overseen
by an institutional review board or equivalent body; the data
cannot be released. All recordings are de-identified. The
institution and review body will be named upon acceptance,
to preserve anonymity.
A.2. Label generation
Each training clip spans 100 frames: an anchor frame and
ten keyframes spaced 10 frames apart after it. Depth comes
from Fast-FoundationStereo [43] on the rectified stereo pair,
and 2D motion from SEA-RAFT [42] between consecutive
keyframes. Since the recordings are natively rectified, each
frame’s stereo calibration converts disparity to metric depth.
Points are chained through the optical flow from the anchor
to each keyframe and lifted to 3D with the depth at each
keyframe, giving each point’s metric 3D position at the an-
chor, P0, and its displacement at each keyframe. We use
H200 GPUs for pseudolabel generation and save labels on
disk at 518 × 518 in hdf5 format.

instrument is driven, and a point’s track only while its depth
and flow pass the consistency checks in Tab. S1. A track
that fails at one keyframe is dropped from that keyframe
onwards.

and only use pixels where they agree up to a pixel. We also ignore
dark pixels and only use depth values between 5-300 mm. We ap-
ply similar checks for optical flow.

B. Robot setup, tasks, and evaluation
B.1. Robot setup
We use a minimally invasive surgical robotics system
equipped with a stereo endoscope and two bipolar forceps
for gripping. For all kinematics, we operate in the end-
effector Cartesian space from the camera space. We use rel-
ative translation deltas between each sample of each clip or
episode and absolute rotations/gripper angles. This yields
13 values per arm (3 translation, 9 rotation, 1 gripper an-
gle), or 26 values for two arms. Similar to prior work, we
insert the endoscope and two arms into a mock abdominal
cavity, where we also place a suture pad and a needle. For
the sake of these experiments, we cut the needle thread short
to improve needle pickup and regrasp; future work will use
a needle with thread of proper length for the full suture. The
physical set up of the robot can be seen in Figure S3
B.2. Tasks and needle configurations
Needle pickup.
The needle pick up task is simply using
the dominant arm (right) to grab and lift the needle. We first
collect 557 demonstrations to train a baseline ACT model
with. We rollout this ACT model and further collect 341
DAgger data to correct the policy when it starts to miss the
needle. This yields 898 episodes. 3 trained operators per-
formed data collection, and the average duration of the task
is 4.9 ± 3.4 s. The policy is deemed to be successful if it
is able to pick up the needle within 3 tries and 500 model
steps.
Needle regrasp.
The needle regrasp task is comprised of
3 subtasks: (1) pick up the needle with the dominant arm,
(2) hand it over to the non-dominant arm, and (3) hand it
back to the dominant arm. This is an essential step before
the dominant arm throws the suture to pierce through the tis-
sue. We first collect 1889 demonstrations with one skilled
operator. We train a baseline ACT model with these demon-
strations and then collect 366 DAgger demonstrations to
correct the policy with human intervention. In total, this
yields 2255 episodes. The average duration of the task is
8.8 ± 2.9 s.
Evaluation configurations.
Every policy is evaluated on
the same ten needle configurations, shown in Figure S4.
These are chosen for a wide variety of needle poses and po-
sitions throughout the suture pad. We initialize the arms at
the beginning of each policy such that the grippers are along
each side of the image boundaries. These needle configura-
tions are outside of the training set to ensure generalizability
of the models.
C. Surgical DINOv2 Tokenizer
Pretraining details.
We refer to the DINOv2 as a tok-
enizer rather than the encoder to reduce confusion with the
ACT encoder. We pre-train the surgical vision model using
the corpus described in Sec. 3.2. Training employs a dy-
namic video data loader that samples and decodes frames
directly from surgical videos on the fly, rather than rely-
ing on a fixed set of pre-extracted frames. Following DI-
NOv2 [31], we adopt a two-stage pretraining strategy. We
first pretrain the ViT-L/14 at 224 × 224 resolution for 625K
iterations, followed by 10K iterations of high-resolution
adaptation at 518 × 518. This strategy enables the model
to capture finer spatial details important for dense predic-
tion tasks, such as segmentation and detection, without in-
curring the computational cost of high-resolution training
from scratch.
Both stages are trained on eight NVIDIA
H200 GPUs, using reduced global batch sizes relative to the
original DINOv2 configuration due to computational con-
straints. Additional training hyperparameters are provided
in Tab. S2.
C.1. Vision model comparison study
To ensure the use of the best vision model, we train vanilla
ACT with (1) off-the-shelf DINOv2, trained on LVD-142M,
(2) off-the-shelf DINOv3, trained on LVD 1689M, and (3)
the surgical DINOv2 used in the paper. To ensure equal
comparison with the in-house DINOv2, we use the ViT-L
4 register token variants, use IN-1k image normalization
stats, and also train at 518px resolution. Since DINOv3 uses
patch size of 16, we set the image size to 592 instead so that
the number of tokens are the same.

E.2. Stabilizing flow-matching fine-tuning
Our first FM runs (v1) diverged during fine-tuning: the
training loss fell to about 0.07 and then rose to about 1.0
between 9k and 22k steps. Three changes fixed this (v2):
running the head in fp32 under mixed precision, a cosine
learning-rate schedule decaying to 10% of its peak, and an
exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.9995)
used for evaluation. All FM results in the paper use v2.
E.3. Baselines
Diffusion Policy.
We train Diffusion Policy [11] with its
official implementation and its real-robot UNet recipe, re-
placing only the image tokenizer so that it matches FlowS-
cope: our frozen surgical DINOv2 at 518×518, whose to-
kens are pooled into 32 spatial-softmax keypoints and a
128-d global feature. Demonstrations are converted with
the same frame stride as ACT (every 4th native frame);
the policy observes the left image and the 26-d robot state
for nobs=2 steps, predicts a horizon of 16 steps (64 native
frames), and executes 8. Actions use the same represen-
tation as ACT (per arm: translation relative to the previ-
ous step, absolute rotation matrix, absolute gripper angle),
range-normalized as in Diffusion Policy. The Condition-
alUnet1D is trained with 100 DDPM noise levels (squared-
cosine schedule, ϵ-prediction) and sampled with 8 DDIM
steps.
We train with AdamW (learning rate 10−4, 500
warmup steps, cosine decay), batch 64, and an EMA of the
weights, for 60k gradient steps on one A100, and save six
evenly spaced checkpoints. Validation loss reaches its min-
imum near 20k steps and rises afterwards; we roll out 5
checkpoints similar to FlowScope.
π0.5 model.
We fine-tune π0.5 [35] from the public base

G.2. Offline metrics
The differences in Sec. 5 do not show up in the usual offline
metrics (Tab. S9, bottom). On held-out demonstrations, the
four fine-tuned policies have nearly the same action error:
0.30–0.31 mm per step in translation, 8.8–9.5◦in rotation,
and 0.10–0.11 in jaw angle. The offline test below, robust-
ness to image perturbations, is similarly flat.
Image perturbations.
We apply 12 perturbations to 400
held-out frames from the validation demonstrations: bright-
ness scaled by 0.75 and by 1.25, contrast reduced to 0.6,
saturation reduced to 0.4, gamma 0.7 and 1.4, a warm and
a cool white-balance shift (red and blue channels scaled by
1.15 and 0.85 or the reverse), Gaussian blur with σ = 2
and 4 px, additive Gaussian noise with standard deviation
0.03, and an occluding square covering 15% of the image,
filled with the image’s mean color at a random position. All
perturbations act on the input image before the frozen DI-
NOv2, so every policy receives the same perturbed features
and differences come only from the trunk. For each policy,
we integrate the predicted chunk into per-arm 3D positions
and measure how much farther they land from the demon-

Discussion.
The policies differ only in closed loop, when
their own actions determine what they see next, including
states that the demonstrations rarely contain, such as recov-
ering from a missed grasp. The dense probes of needle ge-
ometry in Sec. 5 tracked real-robot success where offline
action error did not, which echoes the finding that object-
centric structure predicts policy success better than down-
stream accuracy [6]. We suggest probing what a policy en-
codes, not only how accurately it imitates, when comparing
pretraining methods.
G.3. Dense probe details
The dense probes of Tab. 3 are linear: batch normaliza-
tion followed by a 1×1 convolution on the 37×37 grid of
trunk features at the image tokens, upsampled to the im-
age resolution. Semantic masks come from a segmenta-
tion model trained on internal data, and disparity from Fast-
FoundationStereo [43]. We use the needle pickup dataset
demonstration data to perform these experiments.
To train the probes, we use the AdamW optimizer
with a weight decay of 1e-4 with a linear warmup and
cosine decay scheduler.
We sweep over learning rates
{0.0003, 0.001, 0.003} and show the highest performance.
We use BCE DICE loss and train with 63152 frames and
validate on 4539 frames. Examples of the labeled dataset
for the probing experiments can be seen in Fig. S7.

H.2. Stereo policies
FlowScope uses stereo only to build its pretraining labels;
the deployed policy sees the left image alone. A natural
alternative is to give the policy both eyes and let it extract
geometry itself. We test this with ACT, in the same setting
as our other policies (frozen DINOv2 at 518×518, chunks
of 30 actions at stride 4, batch 128, 100k steps, photometric
augmentation with the same gains applied to both eyes). We
build on StereoPolicy [16] for these experiments.
Both images pass through the same frozen DINOv2 and
the same input projection. The two token grids share their
positional embeddings, since they cover the same pixel grid,
and a learned eye embedding marks left and right. We com-
pare three variants. Mono uses the left eye only and is the
matched control. Concat passes the left and right tokens
(2 × 1,369) directly to the ACT encoder. Fusion follows
