Abstract
JEPA-style world models support visual planning by pre-
dicting future representations conditioned on future ac-
tions. However, similar observations can conceal differ-
ent motion states, making temporal context essential for in-
terpreting future action effects.
We propose PACE-WM,
a parallel world model that uses executed-action history
to contextualize future prediction and planning.
Specif-
ically, PACE-WM maintains a recurrent memory of exe-
cuted actions and uses this memory to condition a causal
Transformer. PACE-WM predicts multiple future represen-
tations in parallel without recursive predicted-state feed-
back within each prediction window. We further introduce
Reference-Anchored Minimum Progress (RAMP) for action
selection during planning. RAMP scores candidate actions
by their predicted goal improvement along direct and split
paths relative to a shared reference.
Across four visual
planning tasks, PACE-WM with RAMP achieves an average

1. Introduction
World models support visual planning by predicting how
an environment responds to candidate actions.
Joint-
embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) predict visual
representations without reconstructing pixels [2, 6], pro-
viding a foundation for modeling future outcomes in la-
tent space. Recent approaches extend representation predic-
tion to action-conditioned dynamics, enabling goal-directed
planning from visual observations [28, 38, 48]. A plan-
ner compares predicted outcomes with a goal representa-
tion and replans after executing selected actions. Effective
planning therefore requires representations that preserve
action-relevant dynamics.
Repeated evaluation of candi-
date sequences also makes prediction efficiency essential
for closed-loop control.

Visual planning requires understanding how future ac-
tions interact with ongoing motion. Visually similar robot
configurations can conceal different motion directions or
speeds, making the underlying dynamic state ambiguous.
The paired examples in Fig. 1 illustrate this ambiguity
through opposing motion directions and different speeds.
Executed-action history provides clues to these differences
by recording how past controls have reinforced or opposed
motion. This context can help distinguish the effects of fu-
ture actions that visual appearance alone leaves ambiguous.
While visual history also supports state estimation [18, 48],
past actions offer complementary dynamic context that can
be updated incrementally without encoding additional im-
ages. Beyond modeling dynamic context, visual planning
also requires efficient evaluation and selection of candidate
action sequences. First, autoregressive rollouts [28, 45, 47]
evaluate candidates through sequential model calls, increas-
ing planning overhead. This motivates predicting multiple
future representations in parallel [12, 15]. Second, direct
prediction and prediction through an intermediate state may
assign different goal progress to the same action sequence,
motivating a planning criterion that accounts for both esti-
mates. We therefore combine executed-action memory, par-
allel prediction, and goal-directed planning.
We propose PACE-WM, a parallel world model that
uses a recurrent encoder to summarize executed actions into
persistent memory. Conditioned on this memory and future
action prefixes, PACE-WM predicts multiple future rep-
resentations in parallel through a causal Transformer [41].
During closed-loop planning, PACE-WM updates the ac-
tion memory with executed actions. This context helps the
model account for how ongoing motion influences the ef-
fects of subsequent actions. For action selection, we in-
troduce Reference-Anchored Minimum Progress (RAMP),
which compares direct and split predictions in terms of goal
improvement relative to a shared reference action sequence.
At unit weight, minimizing its cost is equivalent to maxi-
mizing the smaller of the two predicted improvements. This
provides CEM [11] with a goal-directed and conservative
scoring rule that accounts for disagreement between predic-
tion paths when ranking candidate action sequences.
PACE-WM achieves an average success rate of 95.83%
across four visual planning tasks, increasing to 98.00% with
RAMP, as shown in Fig. 1.
Under matched CEM set-
tings, PACE-WM with RAMP plans approximately 7–9×
faster than autoregressive LeWM [28]. To examine the role
of executed-action context beyond aggregate performance,
we further construct four controlled benchmarks to evalu-
ate motion disambiguation, goal-relative motion, and goal
adaptation. We demonstrate the benefits of action memory
through comparisons with existing world models and inde-
pendently trained visual-history and no-history variants.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We propose PACE-WM, which combines recurrent
executed-action memory with causal parallel prediction
to model future action effects in their dynamic context
and support efficient visual planning.
• We introduce RAMP, a reference-anchored planning cri-
terion that maximizes the smaller goal improvement pre-
dicted by direct and split paths at unit weight.
• We construct four controlled benchmarks to evaluate
motion-dependent planning and validate our approach
through experiments on four visual planning tasks, com-
ponent ablations, and planning efficiency analyses.
2. Related Work
JEPA-style world models for visual planning.
Joint-
embedding prediction learns visual representations without
pixel reconstruction [2, 6]. Action-conditioned latent dy-
namics extend this approach to visual goal-directed plan-
ning [3, 28, 38, 48]. Representation learning spans pre-
trained visual features [32, 48] and jointly learned en-
coders [28], with anti-collapse regularization providing a
foundation for learning nontrivial embeddings [5, 44]. Sub-
sequent work improves the structure of latent representa-
tions [1, 8, 25, 45, 47] and their sensitivity to actions [7, 13,
23, 26, 46]. Alongside these representation advances, recent
methods [12, 15] predict multiple future representations in
parallel, reducing the sequential computation required by
autoregressive rollouts. PACE-WM brings executed-action
context into parallel latent prediction, allowing future action
sequences to be evaluated in light of past controls.
History-conditioned dynamics.
Recurrent and autore-
gressive world models integrate past observations and ac-
tions for control [17, 18, 29].
Recent approaches use
image-action histories to infer motion and slowly varying
disturbances [24], develop efficient visual-history mecha-
nisms [16], or condition parallel prediction on temporal dif-
ferences between visual representations [12]. These meth-
ods highlight the role of temporal context in predicting fu-
ture dynamics.
PACE-WM maintains a recurrent mem-
ory of executed actions to contextualize parallel prediction,
providing a complementary source of dynamic information
without encoding historical images.
Planning objectives and consistency. Model-based con-
trol methods [10, 19] use learned dynamics to guide ac-
tion selection, with planning efficiency improved through
search optimization [34, 35] and amortized action map-
pings [31, 39]. Beyond latent-space proximity to the goal,
temporal geometry [30, 43] guides planning and policy
learning, while trajectory costs and demonstration-informed
scores [14, 21] refine candidate evaluation. Consistency-
based methods [15, 37, 42] constrain prediction paths or
action consequences, while D-JEPA [27] learns decision
alignment from executed outcomes. RAMP compares goal

3. Method
PACE-WM combines executed-action memory, parallel la-
tent prediction, and goal-directed planning, as illustrated in
Fig. 2. We summarize past controls in a recurrent memory
and use this context to condition a causal Transformer that
predicts multiple future representations in parallel. At plan-
ning time, RAMP evaluates candidate action sequences by
their goal progress under direct and split predictions relative
to a shared reference.

3.4. Reference-Anchored Minimum Progress
Direct and split predictions can assign different goal
progress to the same candidate action sequence. We in-
troduce RAMP as a conservative planning criterion that
accounts for this disagreement by comparing each path’s
predicted progress relative to a shared reference. At unit
weight, it favors the smaller of the two predicted improve-
ments, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Reference-relative progress. At CEM iteration i, we use
the sampling-distribution mean Aref = µ(i) as the refer-
ence, anchoring comparisons to the current search center.
We include this sequence in the candidate batch and eval-
uate it through both paths using the same zt and mt as the
other candidates. The paths share the reference actions but

Positive ∆p(A) indicates predicted improvement over
the reference.
Reference subtraction cancels addi-

RAMP penalizes the amount by which direct-path progress
exceeds split-path progress:
P(A) = [∆D(A) −∆S(A)]+,
[x]+ = max(0, x). (16)

JRAMP(A) = JD(A) + λP(A),
(17)
where λ ≥0 controls the penalty strength. Setting λ = 0
recovers direct goal-distance scoring.

For a fixed reference and λ = 1,

4. Experiments
We evaluate PACE-WM’s planning performance and effi-
ciency across four visual control tasks. To examine the role
of executed-action memory, we use four controlled bench-
marks covering motion disambiguation, goal-relative mo-
tion, and goal adaptation. We then analyze how RAMP af-
fects action selection, followed by component ablations and
parameter studies that assess module contributions and sen-
sitivity to key settings. Additional experimental results and
analyses are provided in Appendix D.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Tasks and metrics.
We evaluate visual goal reaching
on TwoRoom [38], Reacher [40], PushT [9], and OG-
Bench Cube [33]. We also construct four controlled Reacher
benchmarks to examine the role of executed-action history
in motion disambiguation, goal-relative motion, and goal
adaptation, as detailed in Sec. 4.3. Performance is measured
by success rate under each task’s success criterion.
Implementation details. Our causal Transformer uses 6
layers, 6 attention heads, and a hidden dimension of 192.
The action-memory LSTM has a hidden dimension of 32.
We train with AdamW using a learning rate of 5 × 10−5,
weight decay of 10−3, and gradient clipping at 1. The SI-
GReg weight is β = 0.09. Experiments run on NVIDIA
RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs. Further implementation
and planning details are provided in Appendix A.
Planning protocol. Unless otherwise specified, each CEM
call optimizes 5 action blocks of 5 controls each over 30
iterations, with 300 candidates and 30 elites per iteration.
The controller executes the resulting 25-control sequence
and replans from a new image if needed, within a total bud-
get of 50 controls. RAMP uses λ = 1 and splits predictions
after k = 2 blocks. For planner comparisons, Base uses ter-
minal latent goal distance alone, while SC [15] adds a latent
self-consistency penalty.

5. Conclusion
We present PACE-WM, a JEPA-style world model that
combines executed-action memory with parallel latent
prediction for visual planning. RAMP complements this
architecture by evaluating reference-relative goal progress
through direct and split predictions.
Experiments across
four tasks demonstrate improved planning success and
substantially lower planning time than autoregressive
LeWM. Controlled benchmarks and ablations show that
action history helps interpret ongoing motion and adapt
control to changing goals, supporting its role in efficient
visual planning. Future work could explore more expres-
sive action-memory models and adaptive planning criteria.

Overview

A. Implementation and Evaluation Protocol
A.1. Model and Training
Architecture.
We use a ViT-Tiny visual encoder with
224 × 224 inputs, 14 × 14 patches, 12 layers, 3 attention
heads, and width 192. Its CLS token is mapped to a 192-
dimensional latent representation through a two-layer pro-
jection head with BatchNorm and GELU. The encoder is
initialized without pretrained weights. The predictor uses
sinusoidal positional encodings and a two-layer output head
with LayerNorm and GELU, without a final residual addi-
tion of the current embedding.
Optimization. Training supervises the predicted latent rep-
resentations after each action block, with prediction hori-
zons ranging from 1 to 5 blocks. We follow the task-specific
batch-size settings of Fast-LeWM [15] and use three inde-
pendent training seeds per configuration.
A.2. Planning Settings
We summarize the RAMP planning procedure, includ-
ing reference insertion, candidate-specific memory updates,
and closed-loop execution, in Algorithm S1. Planner com-
parisons use identical checkpoints and evaluation cases
within each task and training seed. Timing experiments run
exclusively on a single GPU.
B. Mathematical Analysis
Proof of the max–min interpretation.
Fix a reference
action sequence Aref within a CEM iteration and set λ = 1.
Substituting JD(A) = JD(Aref) −∆D(A) into the RAMP
objective gives:
JRAMP(A) = JD(Aref) −∆D(A)
+ [∆D(A) −∆S(A)]+.
(S1)
We distinguish two cases based on predicted progress:
Case 1: ∆D(A) ≥∆S(A).
The penalty is ∆D(A) −

We compare LeWM, Fast-LeWM, and PACE-WM on the

tent representations, and angles are recovered using atan2.

arate trajectories for probe fitting and evaluation. We report
angular RMSE in radians.
Fast-LeWM exhibits lower prediction error than LeWM

port PACE-WM’s history-conditioned parallel architecture
for predicting future joint configurations.
