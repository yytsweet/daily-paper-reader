Abstract
World action models (WAMs) exhibit strong manipulation
capabilities yet often fail to follow test-time language in-
structions. Prior work shows that non-following (NF) WAMs
encode information about the instructed target, but this in-
formation does not control their behavior. By comparing
NF WAM with its instruction-following (IF) counterpart, we
find that target information is substantially more linearly
decodable in IF WAMs, despite being encoded in both. Mo-
tivated by this observation, we introduce linear decodability
supervision (LDS), a simple yet effective auxiliary training
objective that promotes the linear decodability of the in-
structed target information. On LIBERO-CF, InternW0-∆
with LDS achieves a success rate of 74.6% and a grounding
rate of 89.2%, surpassing the strongest baseline by 26.8 and
42.9 percentage points, respectively. The effectiveness of
LDS challenges the prevailing interpretation that encoded
target information does not contribute to WAMs’ behav-
ior. To reconcile this apparent contradiction, we propose a
prior–instruction competition mechanism, which explains
NF and IF behavior as arising from the dominance of prior
and instruction signals, respectively. We provide causal ev-
idence for this mechanism by evaluating two training-free
interventions derived from it. We reinterpret LDS through
this mechanism, showing that it acts as a form of instruction
signal enhancement. Finally, more comprehensive evalua-
tions across diverse manipulation tasks and visual domains
demonstrate the consistent effectiveness of LDS.
1. Introduction
Instruction following is a fundamental capability of vision-
language-action (VLA) models and world action models

(WAM), requiring them to manipulate objects according
to natural language instructions. Despite their ability to

works find that these models have a strong bias to execute
training-time instruction, ignoring instructions given at test

issue by recalibrating attention toward instruction-relevant to-

rather than competing with visual tokens for attention mass
within a shared self-attention operation.
To study IF in WAMs, we start from training an IF WAM
with collected counterfactual data and comparing its inter-

to [8], we probe instruction-related information across differ-

IF and NF WAMs encode such information. However, as

two tags contribute to WAM’s behavior. They compete with
each other, and the winning one finally determines the action.
Therefore, NF and IF WAMs can be explained as the win-
ning of prior tag P and instruction tag I, respectively. We
validate our proposed hypothesis through two training-free
interventions derived from it: (1) enhancing the instruction
tag I; (2) transiting the prior tag P toward the instructed
target. Remarkably, enhancing I improves OpenWAM’s

transiting P achieves 50.8/84.0%, on a picking-target subset

Together, these results provide causal evidence supporting
our proposed mechanism. We further revisit the effective-
ness of LDS through this mechanistic lens. Since LDS aims
to correctly identify the visual target under counterfactual
instructions and the easiest way to do so is to exploit the
existing I, LDS can be seen as a specific form of enhancing
I. We evaluate the explanation by empirically measuring
the magnitude and direction of I and P and find the result
supports the above explanation. Specifically, the magnitude
of I increases whereas the magnitude of P changes only
modestly. Meanwhile, the direction of I and P before and
after LDS remains approximately the same.
Finally, we conduct more extensive experiments to evalu-
ate the effectiveness of LDS. On LIBERO-CF, InternW0-∆
equipped with LDS achieves a success/grounding rate of
74.6/89.2%, outperforming the strongest baseline by 26.8
and 42.9 percentage points, respectively. On LangGap and
RoboTwin-IF, LDS further demonstrates its effectiveness
in both object selection and placement-destination ground-
ing, achieving success rates (SR) of 71.4% and 47.4% with
InternW0-∆, respectively. This implies a more general
prior-instruction competition mechanism of visual targets
in WAMs. Experiments on RoboTwin-IF and real-world
tasks further establish the effectiveness of LDS across di-
verse visual domains beyond LIBERO-based benchmarks.
Evaluations on held-out splits of LIBERO-CF and LangGap
demonstrate strong generalization to unseen initial configu-
rations, with LDS maintaining robust performance even in
completely out-of-distribution scenes. Our main contribu-
tions are summarized as follows:
1. A simple yet effective way to improve IF in WAMs.
We propose LDS, an auxiliary training objective that
improves IF of WAMs without the need of counterfactual
demonstrations collection. Experimental results show
LDS outperforming existing methods by a large margin.
2. A novel prior-instruction competing mechanism. We
provide a novel way of understanding “encode but not
control” phenomenon by introducing competition with
prior signals. We evaluate the correctness of the proposed
mechanism through causal interventions and explain the
effectiveness of LDS under such mechanism.
2. Related Works
World Action Models. WAMs learn robot policies by jointly
modeling visual dynamics and actions, often leveraging pre-
trained video generation models. DreamZero [16] builds on
Wan2.1 [14], while LingBot-VA [10], Fast-WAM [17], and
OpenWAM [15] adopt Wan2.2. Their shared architectural
2


foundations motivate our investigation of how instruction
information interacts with visual representations to influence
action generation. Other WAMs, such as Cosmos Policy [9]
and ImageWAM [20], employ different generative back-
bones. Our mechanistic findings therefore primarily concern
Wan-based WAMs.
Instruction Following in Embodied Policies.
Re-
cent studies reveal that embodied policies may rely on
visual shortcuts rather than faithfully following language
instructions. LIBERO-CF [4], LangGap [7], and Instruct-
Move [21] expose such failures through instruction-sensitive
evaluations, while Counterfactual Action Guidance [4] and
IGAR [19] seek to mitigate them. However, the underlying
mechanisms in WAMs remain underexplored.
Importantly, decodable information need not causally in-
fluence model behavior [3]. Recent work [8] demonstrates
this decodability-control gap in both VLAs and WAMs,
where instructed targets remain encoded despite visually
biased actions. Building on this finding, we investigate com-
peting instruction-dependent and visual-prior-associated sig-
nals in WAMs, establish their behavioral roles through repre-
sentation interventions, and show how auxiliary linear-probe
supervision strengthens IF by amplifying a behaviorally rel-
evant instruction signal.
3. Improving Instruction Following of WAMs
via Linear Decodability Supervision
In this section, we investigate whether the linear decodability
of instructed target information in WAMs is relevant to IF
behavior. We first construct IF WAMs through collecting
and training on counterfactual demonstrations, enabling a
controlled comparison with their NF counterparts. We find
that IF WAMs exhibit substantially stronger linear decod-
ability of instructed target information than NF WAMs, and
that higher decodability is associated with higher IF success
rates. Motivated by these observations, we introduce linear-
decodability supervision (LDS) that explicitly promotes such
linear decodability during training. We reveal that LDS leads
to substantial IF improvements.
3.1. Constructing Instruction-Following WAMs
We start by constructing IF WAMs to enable direct com-
parison on internal states with its NF counterpart. Previous
studies suggest that IF failures can arise from insufficient
counterfactual supervision [4, 5]. When actions and future
are strongly predictable from the current visual observation,
WAMs may exploit an observation-conditioned shortcut and
ignore language instruction. To mitigate this shortcut, train-
ing should expose models to situations where the same visual
observation admits different target behaviors depending on
the instruction, making language information necessary to
distinguish the correct behavior.

obtained the IF WAMs for comparison through collecting

counterfactual demonstrations, as in describe in Section 3.1,

collecting new demonstrations.
4. Understanding Instruction Following of
WAMs Through Competing Signals
The effectiveness of LDS is surprising. Existing work [3,
8]suggests that linearly decodable information may lie out-
side the subspaces to which downstream readers are sensi-
tive. However, the success of LDS raises the possibility that
instruction-dependent information is already represented in
directions that can influence downstream video-action gener-
ation. In this section, we propose a prior-instruction competi-
tion mechanism to reconcile these observations: instruction-
dependent signals can influence behavior but are outweighed
by competing learned priors. We evaluate the correctness
of this mechanism by applying causal interventions derived
from it. Experiments show that both interventions substan-
tially improve IF success rate on LIBERO-CF, providing
strong evidence on the correctness of our proposed mecha-
nism. Finally, we revisit LDS through this mechanistic lens
to explain why it can improve IF of WAMs.

5. Simulation Experiments
We evaluate whether LDS improves instruction following
in varies of tasks, embodiment, and visual domains (RQ1),
supports grounding and execution of new placement goals
(RQ2), and generalizes to initial states and scenes excluded
from grounding supervision (RQ3).
5.1. Settings
Benchmarks. We evaluate instruction following on three
complementary simulation benchmarks: LIBERO-CF [4],
LangGap [7], and RoboTwin-IF (Appendix A). LIBERO-
CF and LangGap share LIBERO’s visual domain and third-
person scene-camera view. LIBERO-CF assigns alternative
feasible goals to LIBERO scenes, testing whether the policy
follows the new instruction or repeats the original training
task. LangGap introduces 59 tasks that vary the manipulated
object, placement destination, spatial reference, or action
within fixed scene layouts. Our RoboTwin-IF (Appendix A)
uses RoboTwin’s distinct visual assets and first-person head-
camera view in a dual-arm setting. We evaluate 25 Pick and
25 Placement scenes with paired instructions that share iden-
tical initial observations but specify different target objects
or destinations, respectively.
Training. For LIBERO-CF and LangGap, we use the
released LIBERO checkpoints of OpenWAM, LingBot-VA,
and InternW0-∆as Base models. For RoboTwin-IF, we ran-
domly select one of the two instructions in each evaluated
scene and fine-tune the released RoboTwin checkpoints on
the corresponding trajectories to obtain Base models. We
then fine-tune each Base model using the linear-probe su-
pervision objective introduced in Section 3.3 for 1024 steps.
We jointly optimize the native flow-matching loss and an
auxiliary grounding loss weighted by λ = 0.25. We take
layer ℓ∈{9, 19, 29} and (λ1, λ2, λ3) = (0.5, 0.45, 0.05).
Evaluation. Success measures completion of the evalu-
ated instruction. Grounding measures whether the gripper
reaches the target object on LIBERO-CF and Pick-subset in
RoboTwin-IF. For Placement, Grounding measures whether
the grasped object reaches and remains in the instructed
destination region. LIBERO-CF evaluates 50 tasks with 50
initial states each and scores the instructed goal (Faithful, F.)
and the original training goal (Biased, B.). LangGap uses

6. Real-World Experiments
We test whether LDS improves instruction following when
the trajectory-training data contain explicit visual shortcuts.
8


We compare Base and LDS variants of OpenWAM, LingBot-
VA, and InternW0-∆on an AgileX Cobot Magic dual-arm
robot using Press-Button and Place-in-Slot (Figure 5).
6.1. Settings
Both variants use the same biased trajectory data; LDS adds
first-frame target-localization supervision. Each Base or
LDS variant is evaluated for 10 trials per task. For Press-
Button, there are red, green, and blue buttons on the table.
Training data only contains trajectory of pressing the red
button. At test-time, X ∈{green, blue} and we test five
trials for each color. For Place-in-Slot, training data come
from recordings with fixed piece combinations and place-
ment orders. Consequently, the visible set of remaining
pieces strongly predict which piece is placed next. At test
time, X is randomly selected on each trial from carrot, egg-
plant, broccoli, corn, and cucumber. Data preparation and
annotation details are provided in Appendix C.
6.2. Performance
Figure 5 shows that LDS improves instruction following
across all three WAMs despite the shortcuts in their trajectory
data. On Press-Button, OpenWAM and LingBot-VA Base
both achieve 0% success, while InternW0-∆Base achieves
40%. With LDS, all three models reach 100% success on
the green and blue targets, neither of which has a trajectory
demonstration in the training set.
On Place-in-Slot, Base success is 0% for OpenWAM and
LingBot-VA and 20% for InternW0-∆. OpenWAM w/ LDS
and InternW0-∆w/ LDS both reach 100% success; LingBot-
VA w/ LDS reaches 80% Grounding and 60% success. To-
gether, these results show that LDS can make real-robot
behavior more responsive to the instruction while retaining
the same biased training data.
7. Conclusion
We study why WAMs encode instructed targets yet act on
learned visual priors.
Our prior-instruction competition
mechanism explains how an existing instruction signal can
be outweighed by a stronger prior signal in downstream tar-
get selection. Training-free interventions that amplify the
instruction signal or redirect the prior-associated component
provide causal support for this account. We also propose
LDS and explain why it work through the lens of our pro-
posed mechansim. Simulation and real-world experiments
demonstrate improved target grounding with generalization.

A. The RoboTwin-IF Benchmark
RoboTwin-IF is an instruction-following (IF) benchmark
and training corpus for bimanual manipulation. It is built on
top of the RoboTwin 2.0 simulation platform [2]. Because
RoboTwin-IF has not yet been released, this appendix de-
scribes how it is constructed, reports dataset statistics, and
lists the task configuration we use to evaluate LDS in the
main text.
A.1. Motivation and Design Principle
In most manipulation benchmarks, the initial observation
largely determines the task. In a standard pick-and-place
episode there is usually only one plausible object to grasp
and one plausible receptacle. A policy can therefore achieve
a high success rate while ignoring most of the instruction,
and its success rate does not tell us whether it actually
grounds language. Figure 6 illustrates how such shortcuts
and underspecified instructions can hide failures of instruc-
tion following. RoboTwin-IF instead follows the principle
different goals, same initial observation.
Every RoboTwin-IF scene is built so that several distinct
goals are simultaneously legal and physically achievable
from the same initial state, including the same robot config-
uration. Only the instruction distinguishes between these
goals. Two instructions that apply to the same scene and
differ in exactly one controlled semantic factor form a coun-
terfactual minimal pair. Because the two episodes share
pixels, proprioception, and object poses, any systematic be-
havioral difference must come from the language input.
Platform.
All scenes are simulated in SAPIEN with the
RoboTwin 2.0 aloha-agilex embodiment. This is a
dual-arm robot with two 6-DoF arms and parallel-jaw grip-
pers. Its state and action space is a 14-dimensional joint-
position vector, with six joints and one normalized gripper
opening per arm. The observations are synchronized RGB
streams from one head camera and two wrist cameras, each
at 320 × 240 resolution. Physics is integrated at 250 Hz, and
observations and actions are recorded at 25 Hz. We use the
clean RoboTwin 2.0 rendering profile, without domain ran-
domization, so that counterfactual pairs differ only in their
instructions.
Object
library.

the
RoboTwin 2.0 object library:

In total, the released scenes contain 95 asset classes, 434

Instruction-following axes.
RoboTwin-IF organizes lan-
guage variation into six axes. Each axis names the single
factor that differs between the two members of a minimal
pair (Table 5). Axes D1a–D1c vary the referring expression,
that is, which object is manipulated. D2a and D2b vary the
goal, that is, which state or spatial relation must hold at the
end of the episode. D4 varies the embodiment, that is, which
arm must perform the action. Each axis takes values from
a closed vocabulary of 174 cells in total. An abstract scene
family can combine at most one D1 axis and at most one D2
axis, with or without D4, all within a single manipulation
step. This rule gives 23 legal axis signatures (6 single-axis,
11 two-axis, and 6 three-axis).
A.2. Counterfactual Data Generation Pipeline
Many earlier generators are recipe-first: they sample a task
template and then fill its slots one at a time. Slot-by-slot
sampling breaks the counterfactual guarantees we need. For
example, replacing a “red mug” with another red object can
make the descriptor non-unique, change grasp feasibility,
or invalidate the success checker. RoboTwin-IF is instead
constraint-first. All semantic, geometric, and physical re-
quirements of a contrast are written as an explicit constraint
set. Scenes are then solved from these constraints, and every
stage either proves that its output satisfies them or records
a structured exclusion. Figure 7 summarizes the pipeline,
which has eight stages.
1. Asset and pair evidence.
Every object model has re-
viewed attributes, a canonical resting pose, and grasp contact
annotations. In simulation we verify which skills each model
supports, such as left-arm or right-arm grasping, articulation,
pressing, clicking, and reorientation. We also verify which
subject–receiver pairs can settle stably in each terminal rela-
tion. Downstream stages may only use capabilities that have
this physical evidence.
2. Abstract scene families.
An abstract scene family pairs
an axis signature with an archetype and expands into a role
graph. The nodes of the role graph are abstract roles, such
as a subject, its contrast peers, a landmark, a receiver, or
anchors. Its edges are flat, typed constraints, for example:
the descriptor must be unique among the peers; the subject
must be graspable by the specified arm; the receiver must
accept the subject in the requested relation. Each family also
declares its goal-variant schema, which is the set of goals

3. Combination-level assignment.
A finite-domain solver
enumerates complete role-to-model assignments that satisfy
every semantic, asset, and pair constraint jointly. It never
samples slot by slot. Across the 37 families with a non-empty
domain there are 9.2×107 feasible assignments. We keep at
most 2,048 assignments per family, chosen deterministically
so that every marginal is covered: role–model, role–category,
attribute value, and pair relation. This yields 47,269 realized
families.
4. Goal realization.
Instantiating the variant schema of
each realized family gives typed concrete goals. The release
contains 161,296 goal variants, linked by 164,850 declared
minimal-pair edges. Each goal is stored in a structured
intermediate representation, from which the instruction, the
success checker, and a symbolic skill plan are compiled.

5. Unit building and layout solving.
Objects that must
stay together, such as a subject and the receiver it rests in, are
grouped into rigid units. A solver then places these units on
the support plane under reachability, non-overlap, camera-
visibility, and reservation constraints. For every goal variant,
an exact-mesh motion planner must certify a collision-free
grasp-and-lift for the arm that variant requires. The layout
is sampled once per scene and shared by all goal variants.
Choosing a variant never moves any object, so no variant-
specific visual cue can leak the goal. Each realized family
gets three independent layout seeds, giving 81,396 solved
SceneSpecs. We also audit the dataset for layout shortcuts.
For example, no single extreme position of the manipulated
object (leftmost or rightmost) accounts for more than 43%
of goal variants, and no single reachability zone accounts for
more than 44%.
6. Instructions and checkers.
For each goal we write
three or four natural-language paraphrase templates and sam-
ple one with a seed. We check that the resulting reference
picks out exactly one object, using the reviewed nouns and
attributes of every object in the scene. Goals that fail this
check (1,056 tasks, 0.5%) are excluded rather than repaired.
Each task also receives an executable checker, expressed in
the same goal representation, relative to the shared initial
state. This stage produces 208,155 task records.
7. Minimal-pair verification.
For every declared edge we
decompose both goals into typed D1a/D1b/D1c/D2a/D2b/D4
projections and an invariant skeleton. The edge is accepted
only if the two goals share the same SceneSpec and initial
observation, differ in exactly one D-code, and have no se-
mantic difference outside that code. Valid pairs are grouped
into contrast sets, which are complete cliques of K goals
that differ only along one axis. We find K = 2 for most
families, K = 3 for the two-anchor D2b family (between,
left of the pair, or right of the pair), and K = 4 for D1b
quadrants. Of 158,628 declared edges, 157,668 are valid,
and they form 135,081 complete contrast sets.
8. Physical validation and cross-checking.
Each task is
executed in simulation with a scene-conditioned scripted ex-
pert. Before execution, a descriptor gate renders the shared
initial head camera view and requires each referenced ob-
ject to be perceptually resolvable. The gate requires a mask
area of at least 60 pixels and a bounding-box fill of at least
0.12. For spatial and ordinal references it also requires an

Supervision split.
For each scene, one of the two instruc-
tions is drawn uniformly at random (Python random, seed
42) and fixed for all models and runs. Only this instruction
is paired with trajectories: its three accepted expert demon-
strations, giving 50 instructions and 150 demonstrations in
total. The counterpart instruction is never paired with an
action trajectory. LDS adds linear-decodability supervision
on top of this trajectory training, and it applies this supervi-
sion to both instructions in each scene, 100 queries in total.
For Pick scenes the target is the instructed object’s visible
mask in the initial frame. For D2b scenes the target is the
instructed destination region on the table, also labeled in the
first input frame. Reference-object masks are never used as
destination targets. The grounding objective and training
procedure are described in Appendix D. The readout heads
are used only during training and are discarded at inference.
Both models start from the officially released RoboTwin
checkpoint of their backbone. The trajectory-only baseline
is trained on the 150 demonstrations with the model’s native
loss. LDS continues from this checkpoint and keeps the same
demonstrations and native loss on its action branch. Because
LDS sees both instructions during localization training, we
call the counterpart instructions localization-supervised, not
language-out-of-distribution.
Rollouts and metrics.
Every instruction is evaluated with
ten seeds (0–9), which control both simulator initialization
and policy sampling noise. Each checkpoint is therefore
evaluated on 50 × 2 × 10 = 1,000 closed-loop rollouts (500
Pick and 500 D2b). For a given scene and seed, both instruc-
tions start from the bit-identical initial state. Policies receive
the three rendered RGB streams, the measured propriocep-
tive state, and the instruction. They receive no expert action
prefix and no auxiliary-head output. Each model runs with
its native action chunking and denoising configuration at a
25 Hz control rate. An episode lasts at most 1,000 control
steps (40 s). We use the grasp-attachment physics and the
canonical checkers from data collection without modifica-
tion. We report three metrics:
• Pick contact rate: the fraction of Pick rollouts in which a
gripper physically touches the instructed object (summed
contact impulse > 10−6) at any time. Contacts between
objects, or between an object and the table, do not count.
• Pick success rate: the fraction of Pick rollouts in which
the canonical Pick checker succeeds. The checker requires
the instructed object to be grasped, lifted off the table, and
held stably, and it applies the scene-integrity guard. Either
arm may be used.
• D2b success rate: the fraction of D2b rollouts in which
the canonical relation checker succeeds. The checker re-
quires the subject to be grasped, transferred, released in
the instructed destination relation, and settled stably, again
under the scene-integrity guard.
Rollouts are assigned to workers in complete (scene, seed)
groups, so the two instructions of a minimal pair are always
evaluated together. Infrastructure errors are retried and are
never counted as task failures. A metric is reported only after
all of its rollouts have completed.

B.1. Stimuli and Label Construction
Controlled multi-instruction scenes.
We build a panel
of 256 scenes. Each scene contains a set of candidate ob-

does not depend on the instruction, such as an “objectness”
detector, therefore gets TR = 1, OR = 0 or the reverse,
averaging 0.5 over the two instructions. We confirmed on
synthetic features that an objectness reader scores 0.5 and a
perfect reader scores 1.0.
Aggregation.
BA is computed for each instruction, aver-
aged over the instructions of a scene, and then averaged over
scenes to give the value of a (layer, site) cell in the heatmap.
One scene is excluded for both models because only one
of its two candidates is visible in the head view. In that
scene, TR and OR cannot both be defined for any instruction.
All reported statistics therefore use n = 255 scenes. Our
summary metric for a model is the mean BA over all 270
cells.
Statistical testing.
We fixed the testing protocol before
running the comparison. Each scene gives one summary
value per model. We test the difference between the two
models with a paired, two-sided sign-flip permutation test
that resamples layout groups (20,000 permutations), with
p = (#{|∆(r)| ≥|∆|} + 1)/(R + 1). A layout-group
bootstrap gives a 95% confidence interval, and we also report
a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For the cell-wise maps, we
test each of the 270 cells with the same paired test (2,000
permutations per cell). We control the false discovery rate at
5% with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

The probe measures whether target identity can
be decoded linearly from the visual tokens. It does not
show that the policy uses this information causally when it
generates actions. The two models share an architecture, and
the IF checkpoint is initialized from the NF checkpoint. The
probe panel is drawn from the IF fine-tuning corpus. The
cross-validation split holds out layouts from the probe fit.

C. Real-Robot Data and Annotation
This appendix describes the data used in our real-robot ex-
periments. We cover the source recordings, the temporal and
spatial annotations, and the procedure that turns long-horizon
demonstrations into the single-stage, instruction-conditioned
clips used for training. We study two tasks: pressing a col-
ored button (Press-Button) and placing a vegetable-shaped
puzzle piece into its matching slot (Place-in-Slot). Both tasks
follow the design principle of RoboTwin-IF (Appendix A).
The tabletop contains several objects that are legal targets,
and only the instruction identifies the one the robot should
act on.
C.1. Platform and Source Recordings
Embodiment and sensing.
We collected all demonstra-
tions on an AgileX Cobot Magic dual-arm platform. It
has two 6-DoF arms with parallel-jaw grippers, which is
the same morphology as the aloha-agilex embodiment
used in simulation. Demonstrations were recorded through
leader–follower teleoperation. At every time step we log the
leader (“master”) arm commands and the measured follower
(“puppet”) state. The proprioceptive stream contains six joint
angles (rad) and one gripper opening (m) per arm, which
gives a 14-dimensional state. Three calibrated RGB cam-
eras record synchronously at 1280×720: one head-mounted
camera and one camera on each wrist. Depth is also recorded
but is not used in this work. All streams are time-aligned
and stored at 30 Hz in the LeRobot v3 format [1]. Only
successful demonstrations are kept.
Long-horizon task families.
The raw recordings are long-
horizon, multi-stage demonstrations. Each one is condi-
tioned on an ordered instruction.
• Sequential button pressing. Three buttons (red, green,
blue) are placed on the table. The robot first observes the
scene and then presses four buttons in a prescribed color
order, for example “Press the buttons in the order: red →
green →blue →red.” Five collections (B1–B5) use five
different orders.
• Sequential slot placement. A wooden puzzle board has
vegetable-shaped slots, and loose pieces from a pool of
five categories (carrot, eggplant, broccoli, corn, cucumber)
lie next to it. The robot inserts three pieces in a prescribed
order, for example “Place the eggplant, then the corn, then
the cucumber into their matching slots.” Five collections
(S1–S5) use different object triples and orders.
A third family (towel folding) was recorded in the same
campaign. It has no discrete target choice, so we do not use
it. In total, the ten collections for the two tasks contain 1,227
demonstrations (about 13.1 hours of interaction). Table 14
lists the individual collections.
C.2. Temporal Subtask Annotation
Every demonstration is manually segmented into contigu-
ous, non-overlapping subtask intervals. Each interval has a
start frame, an end frame, and a free-form label. The label
vocabulary is task-specific.
• Button pressing: observe and memorize the color se-
quence; press the k-th target button (color c) for k ∈
{1, . . . , 4}; and return arm to home pose. Each annotated
episode has eight segments.
• Slot placement: complete the k-th target puzzle (object
o) for k ∈{1, 2, 3}, and return arm to home pose. Each
annotated episode has four to six segments, depending
on whether the operator returned the arm home between
placements.
The annotations are matched to the recordings through a
platform-level episode identifier, not through the positional
episode index, because the positional index changes when-
ever episodes are removed during format conversion. We
check every annotation automatically for missing segments,
gaps, overlaps, and intervals that end before or after the
recording. We also inspect annotations visually in a review
21


interface that plays the three camera streams in sync with
the joint and gripper trajectories and the segment timeline.
C.3.
From
Long-Horizon
Demonstrations
to
Instruction-Conditioned Clips
Our goal is to study instruction grounding at the level of
a single target choice. We therefore split each demonstra-
tion into single-stage clips. Each clip starts from a neutral
arm configuration and ends when one target interaction is
complete. Each clip is paired with a single-target instruction
from a fixed template:
“Press the red button.”
“Place the ⟨object⟩into its matching slot.”
We use the following selection rules.
1. Target segments. For button pressing we keep only seg-
ments whose target is the red button. For slot placement
we keep every placement segment and map the object
name in its label to the template. Observation and hom-
ing segments are never kept as clips.
2. Reset-start constraint. A segment is kept only if the
segment immediately before it is a homing or observation
segment, or if it is the first segment of the episode. This
ensures that every clip starts from the home configuration
and not halfway through a previous motion. In collec-
tions S3 and S4 the operator returned home after every
placement, so all placements qualify. In S2 and S5 some
placements were chained without an intermediate reset,
and those placements are discarded.
3. Kinematic verification. Some annotations call a segment
a reset even though the arm never returned home. To catch
these cases, we require the joint configuration at the first
frame of the clip to stay within 0.3 rad (max-norm over
the 12 arm joints) of the episode’s initial configuration.
4. Minimum length. Clips shorter than 33 frames (1.1 s)
are discarded. This is the length of one action chunk plus
its anchor frame in our policy parameterization.
We exclude collection S1 entirely because part of it has
no temporal annotation. Of the 1,101 demonstrations in
the remaining nine collections, 65 have no annotation and
are skipped. The selection yields 1,726 candidate segments.
The kinematic check rejects 15 of them, and no segment
fails the length check. The final dataset has 1,711 clips
(353,878 frames, about 3.3 hours). We extract each clip with
frame-exact boundaries from all three camera streams, and
we record its provenance (source collection, episode, and
frame range) so that every clip can be traced back to the
original recording. Table 14 gives per-collection counts and
Table 15 gives per-instruction statistics.
Language–scene shortcuts in the training data.
The re-
sulting corpus deliberately keeps the shortcuts found in typ-
ical real-robot data, and we use it as our biased training
distribution. In the button task, every clip has the same in-
struction, even though three equally reachable buttons are
visible. The instruction therefore carries no information
during training. In the placement task, the order of objects
is fixed within each collection, so which piece is targeted
correlates strongly with the scene and with which pieces are
left on the table. In 245 placement clips only one candidate
piece remains, and the scene alone determines the target. A
policy can fit this data well without grounding the instruction,
which makes it a demanding test of whether the localization
supervision described below makes the policy attend to the
referred object.
C.4. First-Frame Target Localization Annotation
For the localization objective used by LDS (Appendix D),
every clip needs a pixel-level segmentation of the instructed
target and of the competing candidate objects in the first head-
camera frame. We obtain these masks with a detect–segment–
classify pipeline built from frozen foundation models and
then verify them with targeted human review.
Automatic proposal.
We take the first frame of each clip at
the native 1280×720 resolution. Open-vocabulary detection
with Grounding DINO [11] proposes candidate boxes.
• For button clips, we query the three button colors. We
keep boxes below an area limit and apply non-maximum
suppression (IoU 0.5).
• For placement clips, we query generic piece descriptions
(“puzzle piece”, “small wooden puzzle piece”) and the five
category names. We filter detections by absolute size and
aspect. We also discard any detection that lies inside the
detected puzzle board, so that pieces already inserted are
not counted as candidates. We then apply non-maximum
suppression (IoU 0.4) and resolve nested or merged boxes.
If no piece is found, we repeat detection with relaxed
thresholds.
Each surviving box prompts SAM 2.1 [12] for an instance
mask. Button identity comes directly from the color query.
For each placement candidate, a zero-shot SigLIP 2 classi-
fier [13] assigns one of the five vegetable categories from
a crop around the candidate. The target mask is the candi-
date whose class matches the instruction. All other detected
candidates are kept as distractor masks.
Human verification.
A clip goes to a human review queue
if any of the following holds: (i) the automatic stage flagged
it, for example because no candidate matched the instruc-
tion or the detections were suspected to be merged; (ii) any
candidate’s class probability is below 0.7; or (iii) two candi-
dates share a class, so the target is ambiguous. The reviewer
sees every candidate mask overlaid on the frame. For each
candidate, the reviewer either confirms or corrects its class

D.5. Optimization and Model Integration
Base checkpoints.
For LIBERO-CF and LangGap, LDS
starts from each model’s released LIBERO checkpoint. For
RoboTwin-IF, a Base checkpoint is first obtained by fine-
tuning the released RoboTwin checkpoint on demonstrations
for one selected instruction per scene. LDS then contin-
ues from that Base checkpoint while retaining its original
trajectory supervision.
Separate optimizers.
The WAM and the auxiliary heads
use separate optimizers. The WAM learning rate is 10−5,
and the head learning rate is 3 × 10−4. The WAM optimizer
uses the combined objective; the head optimizer uses only
grounding gradients. Table 16 summarizes these settings,
and Algorithm 1 gives the logical training update. This de-
scription is independent of how native and grounding batches
are distributed across devices.
Model integration and inference.
The same LDS objec-
tive is applied to the three WAM families. Each model’s
head-camera representations and label grid are aligned with
its own visual layout. LDS adds auxiliary supervision to the
native architecture; the linear heads do not supply inputs to
the policy. Evaluation uses the updated WAM parameters
with all auxiliary heads removed.
E. Scene Hold-Out Protocol and Composition-
Standardized Aggregation
This appendix specifies the hold-out protocol behind the gen-
eralization results of Table 4, which reports episode-pooled
rates. It defines the units over which grounding supervi-
sion is withheld and describes the split. As a supplementary
comparison, we also report composition-standardized rates
because the coverage groups contain different mixtures of
task types.
E.1. Units of Supervision
Both benchmarks are built on LIBERO, and we distinguish
four units.
• Initial state. Each task comes with a fixed set of official
initial states: 50 per task in LIBERO-CF and 10 in Lang-
Gap. Initial states of the same task share the object set
and layout and differ only in the randomized poses of the
objects.
• Task. A task is one BDDL specification paired with one
instruction. An evaluation episode is a (task, initial state)
pair.
• Physical scene. A physical scene groups all tasks that
share the same physical description, meaning the same
object set and placement regions in the BDDL physical
sections, or the same official initial-state file. We take
the transitive closure of both relations, so tasks connected
through either one always end up in the same scene. A
scene typically contains one to four tasks.
• Task kernel. The task kernel abstracts away the con-
crete scene.
It records how an instruction departs
from the original task of its scene:
which element
changes (the object to pick, the destination, or both),
the spatial predicate (on or in), and the destination cat-
egory with instance identifiers removed. Examples are
pick:on:plate (a different object goes to the same
plate) and destination:on:flat stove (the same
object goes to a different receptacle, the stove). Goals
with several literals and articulated actions (drawers, stove
knobs) get a separate other: kernel.
Grounding supervision.
LDS supervises a localization
view: the first input frame of a supervised task, together
with labels of the instructed target and alternative candidates.
Pick views label objects; Place views label placement desti-
nations. Pick views use official initial states 0, 1, and 2 of
each supervised task. Place views use the initial states with
valid destination-localization labels. The number of included
initial states can therefore differ between task types.
Within the LIBERO-CF supervision panel, Place views
come from the two tasks whose counterfactual goals move
the object to a different receptacle body (Long-4 and Long-6).
The LangGap supervision panel contains twelve destination-
change tasks. Both types use first-frame supervision, consis-
tent with the main text. The hold-out affects only grounding
supervision; demonstration trajectories used by the native
action objective are unchanged.

E.3. Coverage Groups
We evaluate every model under the full official protocol: all
50 LIBERO-CF tasks with 50 initial states each, and all 59
LangGap tasks with 10 initial states each. Each episode
is assigned to a group according to the supervision of the
evaluated model.
• Sup. The (task, initial state) pair was itself a localization
view.
• Unsup. init. The task was supervised, but not from this
initial state.
• Unsup. scene. The task was never supervised, but its
kernel was supervised in another scene.
• Unsup. kernel. The kernel was never supervised. We
exclude this group from the standardized comparison
and make no generalization claim for it. It contains 50
LIBERO-CF and 70 LangGap episodes.
The Unsup. scene group contains the held-out tasks. In
LIBERO-CF it also contains three evaluation tasks that are
absent from the supervision panel but share a supervised
kernel (Long-1, Long-2, and Long-3). Table 18 lists the size
of each group.
E.4. Composition Imbalance Across Coverage
Groups
Pick and Place views sample initial states at very different
rates. A supervised Pick task contributes exactly three of its
initial states to Sup., which is 6% of a LIBERO-CF task and
30% of a LangGap task. A supervised Place task contributes
the initial states with valid destination labels, which is 83%
of a LangGap Place task on average. Place episodes therefore
make up a much larger share of Sup. than of Unsup. init
(Table 18). In LangGap, Place episodes make up 40.7% of
Sup. but only 5.9% of Unsup. init, although they make up
23.1% of the benchmark’s tasks.
This imbalance matters because the two task types differ
sharply in difficulty. Place requires an acquisition, a trans-
port, and a relational placement, and for most models it has
a much lower success rate than Pick (Table 20). Write pg
and rg for the Pick and Place success rates in group g, and
πg for its Place share. The episode-pooled rate is then

F. Mechanistic Evidence for LDS Design
Choices
This appendix examines two choices in LDS: applying the
auxiliary loss at the first denoising step, and supervising
instruction-conditioned visual representations. We trace how
an instruction affects action generation in Motus, an inde-
pendent mechanistic case study. Activation interventions
show that most of its measurable instruction effect enters
through the video stream during the first denoising update.
These findings motivate our design; they do not establish
that first-step supervision is optimal for every WAM. We
describe the diagnostic setting (§F.1), the evidence for first-
step supervision (§F.2), the visual pathway (§F.3), and the
implications for spatial grounding (§F.4).

F.2. Why Apply LDS at the First Denoising Step?
Most instruction influence enters in step 0.
Cross-
attention reads the instruction at every layer and denoising
step, but these reads have very different behavioral effects.
We replace the receiver’s cross-attention outputs with the
donor’s either before a split step i or from i onward (Fig-
ure 14a). In the fine, stride-1 sweep over four units, patching
step 0 alone gives λ = 0.95, whereas patching steps 1–19
gives 0.13 and steps 2–19 gives 0.00. A stride-3 sweep over
all 36 units gives 1.00 for steps < 3 and 0.02 for steps ≥3.
The wider sweep supports an early-step concentration, while
the smaller, finer sweep localizes most of that effect to the
first call.
The latent carries the effect forward.
Replacing the
video and action latents passed from step 0 to step 1 with the
donor’s gives λ = 0.96, even though the remaining steps use
the receiver’s instruction (Figure 14b). Thus, an instruction
effect established by the first update can persist through later
updates with conflicting language conditioning. This sup-
ports applying supervision where the initial state of action
generation is formed.
Observation-based state replacement.
We also test early-
state persistence using a controlled pair of two-cup scenes
that differ in color saturation (0.08 versus 0.55), with the
instruction “Pick up the red cup” and noise held fixed. This
is an observation intervention, not an additional language
minimal pair. The receiver’s VLM uses the donor image
in this experiment, so the runs differ only in their video
input. At a layer boundary within step 0, we replace all three
receiver residual streams with the donor’s; the remaining
computation uses receiver conditioning.
At each tested boundary (layers 0–3), the step-0 video
and action velocities become bitwise identical to the donor’s.
The patched run enters the right-arm-only mode on the same
6 of 16 seeds as the donor, compared with 1 of 16 for the
unpatched receiver (Figure 15). However, three donor seeds
that move both arms become left-arm-only after patching,
and the mean relative trajectory L2 difference is 0.03. Early-
state replacement therefore preserves the right-arm-only out-
come in this test, without fixing every aspect of the motion.
The left-target control succeeds on all 16 seeds under every
condition.
Relation to first-frame representations.
In the probing
configuration of §B.2, the clean first-frame tokens are re-
computed identically across denoising calls under the first-
frame-causal attention mask. Reading them once therefore
avoids repeating the same readout in that configuration. This
architecture-specific observation complements the Motus
interventions, which concern when instruction information
affects the generated action.
Implication for LDS.
These results motivate supervising
first-frame visual representations at the first denoising call,
before an instruction-dependent behavior has been carried
into later latents. We compute the auxiliary loss at this call
only, while retaining the native flow-matching objective and
full inference sampler (Appendix D). This avoids evaluating
the auxiliary loss at every denoising step. The interventions
explain the timing choice, but do not compare training with
first-step, later-step, or all-step LDS; such an ablation would
be needed to establish their relative effectiveness.
F.3. Why Supervise the Visual Representations?
Language entry routes.
Motus has two language entry
points: UMT5-to-video cross-attention and the Qwen3-VL
understanding stream (Figure 16). We separate them by giv-
ing a receiver the donor’s text encoding while retaining its
own VLM input, or vice versa. On the tested responsive
pairs, the UMT5 swap gives λ = 0.998–1.003, whereas the
VLM-input swap gives −0.003–0.053 (Figure 17a). The
near-null VLM effect does not mean its features lack instruc-
tion information: changing one word changes understanding
tokens at image positions by 9–17%, and the action stream
assigns 61% of its attention to understanding tokens, against
a uniform share of 33%.
Content replacement and video-to-action transfer.
Re-
placing the understanding stream with a fixed tensor from an
unrelated scene preserves 98–101% of the original instruc-
tion effect on the tested pairs (Figure 17b). Its instruction-
dependent content is therefore largely dispensable for these
responses. Across the 87 contrast sets, video and action
instruction effects are coupled; in responsive sets, the seed-
normalized action effect is 1.7–5.0 times the video effect
(Figure 17c). More directly, patching the donor’s video
residual while letting the action stream recompute yields
λ = 0.998 (95% CI [0.98, 1.01], 36 units). Together, these
interventions identify a behaviorally effective route from
language, through video representations and joint attention,
to action generation.
Implication for LDS.
The video representation is a plau-
sible intervention site because it lies on the measured path
from language to action. LDS supervises the instructed tar-
get in visual tokens that the policy already uses, rather than
relying on a separate readout to control actions at inference.
This motivates the choice of representation; it does not show
that action-side adaptation cannot help, or that every WAM
has Motus’s routing structure.

F.4. Why Use Target-Aligned Spatial Supervision?
Attention magnitude does not localize behavioral influ-
ence.
Single-layer patches within step 0 concentrate the
measured effect in layers 1–10: no layer above 10 has mean
λ ≥0.05 (Figure 14c). No single layer exceeds a mean
of 0.24, although individual layers (10, 2, or 7) suffice on
10 of 36 units. Layer 0 gives the instruction tokens more
attention than layers 1–10, yet its maximum λ is only 0.027.
Attention mass peaks at layers 24–28, which have negligible
effects in this scan. Hence, increasing attention mass alone
is not a direct measure of improved behavioral use. These
are locations of causal language writes; they do not identify
the optimal layers for an auxiliary readout. In particular, this
experiment does not ablate the LDS layer choice {9, 19, 29}.

From global modes to spatial targets.
For these pairs,
language primarily changes the mixing of value vectors al-

resulting write is a vector shared across video-token posi-

magnitude. This is consistent with the observed global arm-
choice and spatial-reference effects, but does not establish
object-instance grounding. LDS instead asks the representa-
tion to distinguish target, alternative-object, and background
regions under the instruction (§D.3). The spatial objective
directly specifies the desired target information. The Motus
experiments motivate this choice; they do not measure how
its write geometry changes after LDS.

The case study supports intervening early in the visual path-

short predictions (eight video frames and 16 actions), with
most analyses conditioned on six responsive contrast sets.

G. How LDS Changes the Internal Representa-
tion of OpenWAM
This appendix supplements the main-text explanation of
why LDS works. We measure the instruction-independent
component U and the instruction-dependent component I
before and after LDS, then test their behavioral relevance
with representation interventions. At layer 8, the results
support amplification of an existing instruction direction and
a reduction of the magnitude of the competing U projection
along an estimated action-read direction. We also report how
this description varies across depth.
G.1. Models, Scenes, and Feature Extraction
Models.
We compare the released OpenWAM LIBERO
checkpoint (Base) with the same checkpoint after 1,024
LDS updates (Base + LDS). This is the run used for the
present mechanistic analysis. Training combines native flow
matching on LIBERO trajectories with first-frame grounding
supervision on LIBERO-CF, using heads at blocks 9, 19, and
29 (Appendix D). Counterfactual grounding queries supply
no action or future-video labels; the heads are discarded at in-
ference. In the diagnostic panel below, Base completes 5/132
counterfactual goals, while Base + LDS completes 77/132
and retains 131/132 completions on the original instructions.
Scenes.
The panel contains 132 LIBERO-CF scenes with
exactly two candidate objects. Following the main text, the
original instruction names the prior-preferred object B, and
the counterfactual instruction names A. Both use the same
observation, verified by matching image hashes in all 132
scenes. Of these, 96 scenes belong to layout groups that
received grounding supervision (train); 36 belong to five
layout groups that received none (held-out). The main-text
representation comparison reports the held-out set. This
panel is separate from the coverage-group evaluation in Ap-
pendix E; its counts and success rates are not full-benchmark
results.
Features.
First-frame tokens in OpenWAM attend only
to the first frame, so instruction information reaches them
through per-block cross-attention. At each block ℓ, we pool
its output over the main-camera first-frame tokens for each
candidate c ∈{A, B}, weighting tokens by ground-truth
mask coverage. This yields xℓ
c ∈R3072. All features come
from the first denoiser call on the unmodified observation.
Block indices are zero-based.

Scope.
The analysis covers one OpenWAM LIBERO
checkpoint and one 1,024-update LDS run on 132 two-
candidate scenes. Mask-based edits are diagnostic and use
one rollout per scene. Transplants and the deeper-edit com-
parisons use the first action chunk; the multi-layer steering
edit is also checked in closed loop. The comparison with
Motus is an analogy between independent case studies. The
symmetric/antisymmetric decomposition is exact for each
feature pair, while its mechanistic interpretation relies on the
main text’s modeling assumptions. Identical observations
support attributing the contrast to the instruction; they do not
separate visual differences from prior-associated information
within U. Scene-bootstrap intervals also do not account for
shared layout groups.
