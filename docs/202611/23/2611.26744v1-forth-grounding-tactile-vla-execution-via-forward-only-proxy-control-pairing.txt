FORTH: Grounding Tactile VLA Execution via Forward-Only
Proxy-Control Pairing
Junde Li1, Wei-Han Yu1, Xiangyi Chu1, Yaoxin Li1, Jiawei Xu1, Ka-Fai Un1, Pui-In Mak1
1University of Macau
Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are widely cred-
ited with force control, operating on the unverified as-
sumption that proxy loss minimization implies physical
force grounding. In this work, we demonstrate that this
assumption breaks down at three core deployment levels:
frozen visual backbones discard contact geometry, down-
stream losses mask token-level gradient alignment, and
discrete indicators trigger optimization collapse. To ad-
dress these disconnections, we present FORTH, a forward-
only measurement harness and execution framework that
explicitly pairs the optimization objective at each level
with its physical control observable. FORTH incorporates
three grounded pillars: a layer-local, forward-only tactile
encoder that preserves spatial contact geometry without
backpropagation, a token-parallel zeroth-order adapter en-
abling activation-space updates, and SlipGuard, a contin-
uous tactile verification gate that fires on incipient slip.
On an elasto-plastic structural shell, the SlipGuard con-
troller peaks at 29× lower grip force than the reflex ramp
of the same shell, preventing structural crush where the
baseline leaves permanent deformation.
Furthermore,
FORTH matches state-of-the-art execution performance
with a 97.6% average success rate across three evaluated
LIBERO suites, while reducing peak activation memory
by 7.12× compared to a backpropagated step on the same
backbone. Enhanced perception enables deeper physical
intelligence. The code and models are open-sourced at
https://github.com/Jordanlllll/FORTH.

Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are rapidly ad-
vancing from tabletop picking to contact-rich manipula-
tion. To operate effectively under contact, state-of-the-art
systems increasingly augment pretrained visual backbones
with tactile or force sensing modalities [16, 40, 41, 87].
Concurrently, tactile sensing hardware and representa-
tions have matured: optical tactile sensors, high-fidelity
contact simulators, and learned representations provide
policies with spatially resolved contact maps rather than
scalar load readings [20, 37, 71, 77, 80]. While an emerg-
ing family of tactile VLAs reports force-aware execu-
tion across tasks like insertion, wiping, and grasping
[2, 8, 10, 11, 35, 49, 51, 52, 84, 86, 88, 90], performance
across these systems is predominantly evaluated using a
single proxy metric per task level. Such scalar metrics ob-
scure the underlying physical reality: the critical quantities
governing whether an object survives an episode are rarely
measured or reported together. This discrepancy stems
from a missing evaluation and reporting convention.
Existing research programs address isolated compute
and memory bottlenecks without closing this measure-
ment gap. Layer-local learning objectives replace global
backpropagation with forward-only computations [1, 17,
23, 29, 36, 38, 44, 55, 61, 63, 65], yet report perfor-
mance strictly via local loss values. Zeroth-order (ZO)
and gradient-free optimizers trade memory-intensive back-
ward passes for forward queries [9, 19, 32, 46, 48, 66,
67, 81, 85], evaluating only estimated gradient directional
alignment. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and
quantization constrain these updates within frozen back-
bones [13, 25, 26], while low-level controllers treat phys-
ical slip as a binary classification target scored by de-
tection accuracy.
Crucially, an improvement in these
algorithmic proxies can decouple from physical interac-
tion quality—a lower local loss does not inherently pre-
vent object damage—and no current benchmark protocol
[12, 22, 30, 34, 42, 45, 47, 50, 57, 58, 74, 79, 91] system-
atically measures and reports these paired dynamics.
To resolve this decoupling, we introduce FORTH, a uni-
fied harness governed by a paired metric convention across
all three execution levels: report the optimized algorithmic
quantity alongside the physical quantity it aims to con-
trol, accepting a policy level only when both metrics move
in lockstep. Operating entirely forward-only and layer-
locally on frozen 4-bit backbones without global backward
passes, FORTH integrates three key components: (1) a tac-
tile feature trunk composed of two Forward-Forward (FF)
blocks; (2) a token-parallel zeroth-order adapter of rank
8 attached to the action head; and (3) SlipGuard, a one-
class shear-ratio gate with a bounded real-time corrector.
Rather than introducing a massive new backbone, FORTH

Related Work
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Policies
Vision-language-action models predict an action sequence
from an image and an instruction [3, 5, 33]. Their scale
comes from a pretrained multimodal backbone rather than
from robot data, and the head that turns features into com-
mands is small enough to adapt on one GPU. Alongside
them sit action-chunking transformers for low-cost biman-
2


ual teleoperation [89], tokenizers that compress a chunk
to a short sequence [62] and open-world variants of the
recipe [27, 28, 82].
Touch enters that stack in three ways: as semantic-
aligned tokens projected into the same space as text [11],
as a residual representation added to a frozen visual pol-
icy [86], or as a predictive model that forecasts contact
before it occurs [31, 84, 90]. Neighboring designs fuse
heterogeneous tactile sensors behind one policy [35, 40],
learn the touch representation from future visual super-
vision [10], or name material and object properties from
touch without closing a control loop [83].
A separate line learns force implicitly rather than condi-
tioning on it [21, 41]: grip strength emerges from the task
reward [8, 59, 60], and the force in the logs is whatever the
policy found sufficient.
2.2
Forward-Only Update Rules
Forward-only learning replaces the backward pass with a
local objective. Forward-Forward trains each layer on its
own contrastive goodness [23], and the similarity kernel
of layer representations contracts toward rank one with
depth [78]. Local error signals and forward gradients are
the neighboring non-contrastive route [55, 63]; contrastive,
predictive-coding and scalable variants extend it [1, 17,
36, 61]. On hardware, INT8, sparse, binary-stochastic
and closed-box trainers report lower energy, memory or
compute than backpropagation at the edge [29, 38, 44, 65,
72].
Zeroth-order optimization estimates a descent direction
from function evaluations alone, from the black-box attack
estimator [7] and random search [48, 66] through adap-
tive moments [9] to MeZO [46], with variance-reduced
variants [19] and convergence analyses [85]. Applications
reach VLA adaptation [32], quantized fine-tuning [67] and
prompt tuning [81].
These methods are judged by downstream loss or recall.
A population argument, in which independent per-token
perturbations average out, predicts that estimator quality
improves with token count; that prediction has not been
measured against the true gradient on a physical signal.
Adaptation and evaluation practice supply the rest: low-
rank and quantized adapters [13, 25, 26], one-bit back-
bones [76], benchmarks and simulators [30, 42, 45, 50, 53,
57, 79], and replication practice [43, 56, 75].
2.3
Slip Detection and Tactile Sensing
Slip detection has a reflex-control literature that pre-
dates learned policies. Visuo-tactile classifiers run on
multi-scale temporal convolutions [18], and incipient-slip
measurement tracks the transition during in-hand pivot-
ing [4, 39, 64, 73]. Geometric reconstruction recovers ex-
trinsic contact from touch and proprioception [52], and the
same contact signal drives sub-millimeter insertion [51].
A second class puts that signal in a fast loop under a
slow planner: a reactive dexterous controller [2, 54], or a
vibration signal routed straight to action [49].
On the representation side the split is between an online-
refined tactile prediction that absorbs contact drift [87]
and a decomposition that separates force from surface
texture [88]. Both matter for the representation level: an
aggregate can be predictive of contact and still carry no
usable force.
Evaluation in this line is detection rate, latency and false
positives on a held-out session, which scores the detector
while the force it commands can stay wrong.
The substrate matured alongside the policies: gel-sensor
hardware [37], simulators [71, 77], visuo-tactile pretrain-
ing [20], sim-to-real transfer [74] and a recent survey [80].
2.4
Proxy-Based Evaluation
Reporting conventions here are inherited from benchmark
suites that score task success alone, or at most one scalar
force reading beside it [12, 14, 15, 22, 30, 34, 42, 47,
50, 58, 79, 91]. Adapter and parameter-efficient prac-
tice keeps the same convention: the reported columns are
loss and success, and the controlled physical quantity is
not among them [13, 25, 26]. Contact-aware instrumenta-
tion measures the sensor rather than the policy’s outcome
[20, 37, 71, 74, 77]. The pairing defined next therefore
keeps each line’s objective as given and adds the column
none of them reports.

Proxy-Control Framework
3.1
Deployment Stack Taxonomy
The FORTH framework organizes the tactile Vision-
Language-Action (VLA) pipeline into a three-level hierar-
chy, where each level is governed by a specific proxy signal
that approximates a critical physical control quantity.

4.3
Force Channels in Policy Input
Physical force signals cannot be introduced into the VLA
execution policy as instantaneous scalar readings. To make
the tactile signal usable for robust proxy control, force
must enter the network as a temporally aggregated feature
channel structured alongside spatial patch representations.
We test this interface with matched arms and controls.
The channel integrates force across a sliding temporal win-
dow before policy decoding, which decouples language-
level force intent from real-time load.

7
Discussion
7.1
Real-Sensor Degradation
Empirically calibrated rather than physically derived, the
threshold shifts under per-sensor offsets. On real-sensor
recordings, this yields a pre-onset false-positive rate of
0.024 on the force-level statistic and 0.031 on the ratio
statistic. Figure 5 illustrates the failure mode captured
at this boundary: the policy’s action objective continues
to report a low error even as the object misses the target
receptacle.
7.2
Sensitivity
Three empirical findings bound how much of the per-
formance gain depends on the gating mechanism. First,
SlipGuard transfers without retuning: across 1,200 cross-
domain episodes spanning five geometries, six masses up
to 1 kg and two mid-lift friction steps it succeeds 100.0%
of the time at a median peak force of 2.34× the require-
ment. Second, the command responds 6 ms after initial slip
and then relaxes (+2.26 N), whereas pure reflex continues
tightening until it crushes the body (33.17 mm, 63× yield).
Third, adaptivity proves decisive once disturbances ex-
ceed the static margin: under a mild step, a tuned constant
matches the adaptive arms (1.51× on one object, ties on
two), but when required force exceeds the delivered con-
stant, the constant arm recovers 0/2 episodes versus 2/2 for
every adaptive arm.
7.3
Tactile Hardware Requirements
Deployment requires three elements: a shear/normal sensor
pair sampled at the control rate, one fault-free calibration
8


set of nominal episodes, and a closed-loop force cap the
policy cannot raise. Because SlipGuard is one-class, it
needs neither fault data nor a force/torque sensor beyond
the pad pair. The plant’s native 500 Hz loop serves as the
servo layer, the adapter updates at 1 Hz, and a 48 × 64
tactile frame is the only added input, on top of a 4.6 GB
4-bit backbone.
8
Conclusion
Previous methods were scored by a proxy never paired with
the quantity each level must control; FORTH reports the pair.
At the representation level, a layer-local Forward-Forward
trunk matches backpropagated accuracy while remaining
forward-only. At the update-rule level, a token-parallel
zeroth-order adapter trains a rank-8 adapter on the action
head and cuts peak activation memory 7.12× against a
backpropagated step. At the controller level, SlipGuard
fires on incipient slip and peaks 29× lower in grip force
than a reflex ramp, leaving the object intact where the
baseline crushes it. Across three evaluated LIBERO suites
the harness reaches a 97.6% average success rate with
the pairing intact (Table 4). Enhanced perception enables
deeper physical intelligence.
