Abstract
NitroGen is an open vision-action foundation model for game control. It is trained by
behavior cloning on online gameplay videos. In this paper, we study the frozen controller
in Hollow Knight and Trackmania as examples along three dimensions: action adaptation,
visual instruction following, and spatial exploration. In five independent six-minute trials,
the action distribution changes across game contexts. Horizontal movement drops from 53.4%
in the initial corridor to 41.4% at a later scene that requires jumping. Jump actions rise
from 11.5% to 24.5%, which suggests that NitroGen responds to changes in the local visual
state, but it still fails to finish the jump scene. Visual prompts provide only weak steering.
Graphical arrows affect behavior more than text prompts, but neither prompt type produces
reliable instruction following. Visual-state clustering shows concentrated visitation. In the
full additional dataset, NitroGen visits more clusters than Random but fewer than Human on
average. Its mean entropy is lower than Random’s. The Human–NitroGen entropy ordering
depends on the cluster count. Our results indicate that NitroGen can adapt its actions to
local context, but it lacks the planning, recovery, and exploration needed for longer-horizon
game control.

Introduction
Generalist game-playing agents are a useful setting for studying pixel-based control. The agent must read the
screen and output actions directly. It also needs to recover from mistakes and continue toward a goal over
time. Prior work has studied game agents with reinforcement learning in Atari and StarCraft II (Mnih et al.,
2015; Badia et al., 2020; Vinyals et al., 2019), LLM/API-based control in systems such as Voyager (Wang
et al., 2023), and embodied-agent benchmarks such as MineDojo (Fan et al., 2022).
NitroGen is an open vision-to-action foundation model for game control, jointly developed by researchers
from NVIDIA, Stanford University, and the California Institute of Technology (Caltech). It maps raw RGB
frames to gamepad actions without access to the game-specific APIs or internal game states. The released
500M-parameter controller uses a DiT vision-action architecture and is trained by behavior cloning on roughly
40,000 hours of public human gameplay videos from more than 1,000 games (Magne et al., 2026). It has a
universal simulator wrapper that provides a common interface for commercial game binaries. Action patterns
are learned during pretraining. We freeze those learned weights during every evaluation. NitroGen is designed
as a fast reactive controller, not as a complete long-horizon planning system.

We study the capabilities and limitations of this frozen controller. The main question is whether NitroGen
only produces local reflexes, or whether its behavior can support longer game progress. This matters for
inference-time interventions. A high-level module may detect that the agent is stuck and propose a macro-
action. A visual prompt may add an arrow or text cue to the screen. However, these methods can help only
if the frozen controller can respond to the new signal and execute the intended action.
We therefore focus on the controller without adding any new planning module. A short-term action predictor
may produce reasonable actions in a local scene, but still fail as a game-playing agent. Game progress also
requires goal maintenance, recovery, navigation, and exploration. If the controller cannot reliably perform
the needed low-level behavior, then better high-level advice may not lead to better play.
We evaluate NitroGen with three analyses. We call them D1, D2, and D3, respectively. D1 tests whether its
action distribution changes across game contexts. D2 tests whether visual prompts can steer its behavior. D3
measures how much of the game space it explores compared with random and human baselines. Our results
show that the three studies can distinguish local reactive control from longer-horizon failure.

Related Work
Game-playing agents and generalization.
Game environments have long been used as benchmarks
for assessing agents. The Arcade Learning Environment created a shared platform for evaluating general
agents (Bellemare et al., 2013). DQN and Agent57 showed strong reinforcement-learning performance in
Atari, while AlphaStar did so in StarCraft II (Mnih et al., 2015; Badia et al., 2020; Vinyals et al., 2019).
Procgen shifted the focus from fixed-game performance to generalization on generated levels (Cobbe et al.,
2020). Voyager depends on language-model planning together with executable skills. Gato trains one sequence
model across multiple embodied domains (Wang et al., 2023; Reed et al., 2022). These systems center on
task performance, planning, or cross-domain behavior. They do not directly test how a frozen vision-action
controller responds when it must recover from local failure without reward updates.
Learning game control from large-scale demonstrations.
MineRL and MineDojo offer large-scale
datasets for Minecraft agents, including human demonstrations and online knowledge (Guss et al., 2019;
Fan et al., 2022). Video PreTraining (VPT) applies behavior cloning on online video to learn game-control
priors (Baker et al., 2022). NitroGen follows a similar path. It is a generalist pixel-to-gamepad model trained
on gameplay videos from more than 1,000 games (Magne et al., 2026). However, behavior cloning can suffer
from covariate shift and compounding errors during closed-loop rollout (Ross et al., 2011), and imitation
policies can exhibit causal confusion (de Haan et al., 2019). We study the released frozen controller to
determine whether its local action changes are enough for instruction following, obstacle completion, and
exploration.
Visuomotor conditioning.
Some visuomotor policies use task or language conditioning during training.
BC-Z, RT-1, RT-2, VIMA, and language-conditioned imitation learning include task descriptions, language,
or multimodal commands as part of the policy input (Jang et al., 2022; Brohan et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al.,
2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Lynch & Sermanet, 2020). Our NitroGen setting is different. The evaluated controller
receives only pixels. Text and arrows placed on the screen are therefore visual changes to the observation,
not native command inputs.
Planning and exploration.
Some agent systems divide low-level control from higher-level planning.
SayCan combines language planning with learned affordances, while MuZero and DreamerV3 use learned
models for search or latent prediction (Ahn et al., 2022; Schrittwieser et al., 2020; Hafner et al., 2023). Other
research looks at exploration. DIAYN, Go-Explore, and Plan2Explore encourage agents to discover diverse
skills or states (Eysenbach et al., 2018; Ecoffet et al., 2021; Sekar et al., 2020). Our work does not introduce
a new planner or exploration objective. It provides a diagnostic evaluation of NitroGen. The model shifts its
local action distribution when the visual context changes, yet this does not guarantee obstacle completion,
prompt controllability, or broad state coverage.

3
Problem Formulation
We study NitroGen as a frozen reactive controller that maps game frames to gamepad actions. In our
implementation, the action representation consists of a 21-dimensional button vector plus left and right
joystick values. The model output is converted into gamepad input through thresholding and joystick scaling.
This interface is suitable for fast reactive motor control, but it does not expose an explicit memory, goal
representation, symbolic state, or text-instruction channel.
We focus on three measurable properties. First, we measure whether NitroGen changes its action distribution
as Hollow Knight progresses from a corridor that requires horizontal navigation to an obstacle that requires
jumping. Second, if visual prompting is to be useful, the model must treat an overlaid arrow or instruction
as a control-relevant cue rather than as irrelevant image content. Third, an agent that is supposed to play
games rather than only produce plausible controller traces should visit a sufficiently broad set of visual states.

The central evaluation question is whether NitroGen’s failures can be characterized as stable action- and
state-level patterns rather than isolated task failures. We organize the problem into three measurable
questions:
1. Context-dependent action adaptation: Does NitroGen change its control strategy when gameplay
moves from horizontal navigation to a jump-dependent obstacle, and is the change sufficient to make
progress?
2. Instruction blindness: Can NitroGen use visual or textual cues embedded in the input frame to
change its movement direction?
3. Exploration constraints: Does NitroGen cover a broad visual state space, or does it remain
confined to a small region?

This setup is used to test the capabilities of NitroGen, not to improve it during inference. The tested prompts
are limited to arrows and short text cues. They should not be treated as the full set of possible prompts.
Human gameplay is used as a proxy ground truth because NitroGen is trained from human gameplay videos,

We note that Hollow Knight and Trackmania are stress tests, not representative benchmarks for all games.
Hollow Knight exposes horizontal navigation, multi-step jumps, and recovery after failed jumps. Trackmania
exposes fast directional control. We do not establish how often either game, or a similar control regime,
appeared in NitroGen pretraining. Distribution shift may contribute to the measured behavior. Our claims
concern the tested games and the released frozen controller.

We use three measurements to test the related limits. D1 compares the action distribution across two Hollow
Knight scenes. D2 adds arrows or text to the input frames and measures whether the controller changes its
behavior. D3 clusters the observed frames and compares the state coverage of NitroGen, random play, and
human play. The studies test action choice, response to visual cues, and exploration.
The three studies distinguish local reaction from longer game progress. We observe that NitroGen can change
its action distribution when the scene changes. However, it does not mean that it can clear the jump, follow
prompts reliably, or cover a large part of the environment. This observation matters before adding a planner
or a VLM module. Even if such a module selects a better next step, the final gamepad action still has to
come from the frozen controller.

Approach
4.1
Context-Dependent Action Evaluation
We ran five Hollow Knight trials from the same initial corridor. Each trial used the frozen NitroGen checkpoint.
Each trial contains 21,600 environment frames at 60 frames per second (FPS), equal to six minutes of game
time. Model inference pauses the game between environment steps. Wall-clock inference latency is therefore
excluded. Before each trial, we reset the model and manually returned the game to the same initial state.
We use three windows: Immediate (0–1 s), Early (0–60 s), and Late (300–360 s). Early is the main corridor
window. Late is the later obstacle window. The corridor is deliberately constrained. The main local
requirement is horizontal movement to the left or right. The later scene is a jump puzzle that blocks the
next scene. Passing it requires roughly four continuous jumps with horizontal control. The Early and Late
comparison is a context comparison, not a pure time comparison.

4.2
Visual Instruction-Following Evaluation
We evaluate three prompt conditions: no prompt, a right-arrow image overlay, and the text instruction “Go
right. Move the character to the right.” Each condition has 100 trials. We measure the fraction of right and
left movement. Then we report mean right movement, mean left movement, and P(right > left).
The prompt study asks whether a rightward cue increases rightward movement. We test both an arrow
prompt and a text prompt. An arrow is a visual cue. Text requires language understanding from pixels.
The simple prompts set a low bar for a control signal. The test does not rank prompt designs. It asks
whether a clear on-screen cue can act as a dependable basic command. NitroGen is not trained with a
language-conditioned action interface, so weak text response is expected. Language-conditioned imitation
learning includes language during policy training (Lynch & Sermanet, 2020).
For trial i, let ri and ℓi be the fraction of measured frames with rightward and leftward movement. P(R > L)
is the fraction of trials where ri > ℓi. Mean right and left proportions measure action frequency. P(R > L)
measures trial-level directional dominance.

Higher entropy means broader and more balanced visual-state coverage. Clustered visual entropy is only a

The metric is not a direct score of game progress. It measures visual confinement. Random play may visit
states that do not help winning. Human play may visit states through goal-directed progress. Since NitroGen
is trained from human gameplay videos, we treat human gameplay as a close ground truth for purposeful
state coverage, not as an exact optimal policy. If NitroGen covers fewer states than Random under the same
embedding and clustering pipeline, then the frozen controller is spatially conservative at the level of observed
visual states.
4.4
Interpretation Criteria
We use relative thresholds. In D1, the main evidence is paired change across repeated trials. In the corridor
scene, we expect more horizontal movement while more jumping should be tried at the later obstacle. A
consistent action shift supports visual-context sensitivity, but it is not sufficient evidence of successful play.
In D1, successful long-horizon execution requires clearing the jump obstacle.
For D1, all five paired trials show less horizontal activity and more jumping in the Late window. None
clears the obstacle. For D2, even the best graphical prompt gives an incomplete shift. The arrow suppresses
leftward movement in Hollow Knight but increases rightward movement in Trackmania. Prompt response is

Note that the claims of our paper are limited to the tested frozen controller, prompt forms, and games. We

Future NitroGen checkpoints may behave differently. The practical question is which failure mode should be
addressed next.
Clearly, no single metric is sufficient for drawing a conclusion. Although a higher jump rate at a jump obstacle
is locally appropriate, it does not necessarily demonstrate planning. Low state entropy can also be acceptable
if an agent follows a narrow successful path. The important pattern is the combination of adaptation to
local action, failure to complete the obstacle, weak instruction response, and low state coverage relative to
baselines.

5.3
Spatial Exploration
Table 9 reports the entropy and cluster counts for the original D3 recordings. The following figures and
effective-state counts also describe these recordings. NitroGen visits 18 of 50 clusters. Random visits 22.
Human play visits 39. NitroGen also has the lowest entropy: H = 2.55, compared with H = 2.84 for
Random and H = 3.28 for Human. The gap is substantial under the same embedding and clustering pipeline.
NitroGen underperforms both Random and Human in measured visual-state coverage. Fig. 5 shows the more
concentrated NitroGen visitation pattern. Fig. 6 shows its narrower region in the visual embedding. Bootstrap
resampling with n = 1000 keeps the entropy distributions visually separated in Fig. 7. This frame-level
bootstrap does not establish the ordering across independent runs. Appendix C reports run-level uncertainty
for the additional trajectories. We do not claim exhaustive coverage of all NitroGen environments.
The Random comparison is important. Human play should cover broader and more meaningful states
because it reflects purposeful navigation. Random play has no goal. In the full additional dataset, NitroGen
visits more clusters than Random but fewer than Human on average. Its mean entropy remains lower than
Random’s. Coverage and entropy measure different properties, and the coverage ordering changes in the
same-resolution check at K = 200. D1 and D3 are compatible. NitroGen can shift local actions and still
remain spatially confined over the full trajectory.
In effective state counts, Keff = eH. NitroGen reaches about 12.8 effective states. Random reaches about
17.1. Human play reaches about 26.6. The human baseline covers roughly twice as many effective visual
states as NitroGen.

Discussion: Physical Interpretation of Results
The three analyses point to the limits of the released controller. NitroGen changes its local action distribution
when the game context changes. In D1, it reduces horizontal control and increases jumping after reaching
a jump-dependent obstacle. This is positive evidence of local adaptation. It is not evidence of successful
long-horizon play because the agent never clears the obstacle.
D2 shows a second boundary. Pixel-level prompts have limited control over the frozen policy. Graphical
arrows can shift actions in some settings, but text prompts are weaker. This is expected for a model trained
as a pixel-to-action controller rather than as a language-conditioned policy. D3 adds a trajectory-level view.
In the full additional dataset, NitroGen visits more visual clusters than Random but fewer than Human on
average. Its mean entropy is lower than Random’s. The controller can look locally responsive while remaining
spatially confined.
6.1
Relation to Behavior Cloning and Steering
These findings are consistent with known limits of behavior cloning. A policy can reproduce locally appropriate
actions without representing the decision process that generated them. DAgger identifies the distribution-shift
problem for cloned policies (Ross et al., 2011). Causal confusion gives a related explanation: an imitation
learner may rely on correlations that do not support recovery or goal maintenance (de Haan et al., 2019).
Our experiments do not identify the internal cause. They show the external pattern: local adaptation does
not reliably produce obstacle completion, prompt controllability, or broad exploration.
This matters for inference-time steering. A high-level module may recognize that the agent is stuck, but the
low-level controller still has to execute the correction. Systems such as SayCan separate high-level planning
from low-level affordances (Ahn et al., 2022). Language- and task-conditioned policies such as RT-2 and
VIMA expose conditioning channels during training (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023). NitroGen, in
the evaluated interface, does not. Text or arrows rendered into the frame are only visual changes. They are
not native commands.

6.2
Scope of the Baselines
D1 uses each trial as its own paired reference across Early and Late windows. It does not need a random
action baseline. Random and Human baselines are used in D3 for visual-state coverage. Human play is a
proxy ground truth because NitroGen is trained from human gameplay videos. It is not an exact optimal
policy. Random play is not a desirable gameplay strategy either. Its role is diagnostic: if NitroGen covers
fewer states than Random under the same embedding and clustering pipeline, then the controller is spatially
conservative in the measured trajectories.

6.3
Limitations
Our study is diagnostic in nature and limited in scale. D1 uses five six-minute Hollow Knight trials from
one initial state. The Early and Late windows contain different gameplay contexts. Their difference is
not a pure effect of elapsed time. D2 tests one rightward-steering setup. Other prompts may behave
differently. Placement, duration, and visual salience may also matter. D3 measures visual-state coverage with
a SigLIP–PCA–k-means pipeline. It does not measure game progress directly. Entropy values may change
with another embedding or clustering method (Guo et al., 2021).
The overall evidence is consistent across the three dimensions studied: D1 shows local action adaptation
and a task-completion boundary. D2 shows weak prompt controllability. D3 shows restricted visual-state
exploration. No single metric in and of itself determines the final conclusion.
6.4
Broader Impact
Large-scale online gameplay data may raise copyright and privacy concerns. This diagnostic study does not
assess whether the original training data addressed those concerns. The observed control failures also caution
against deploying such controllers without safeguards in interactive systems that require high reliability.
6.5
Future Work
Future work should separate perception, planning, and control more directly. One option is to keep NitroGen
as a low-level motor prior and add an action-space interface for high-level commands. Another option is to
train an explicit instruction-conditioned module. Future evaluations should also use controlled pairs of game
states, more games, and recovery-oriented scenarios. Exploration analysis should be repeated with other
embeddings and clustering schemes to test the robustness of the NitroGen–Random–Human ordering.
7
Conclusions
The tested NitroGen controller shows context-sensitive local adaptation. It also shows limited long-horizon
execution. Across five Hollow Knight trials, it shifts from horizontal navigation toward more frequent jumping
after reaching a jump-dependent obstacle. However, it never clears the obstacle. Visual prompts provide
limited steering. In the full additional dataset, NitroGen visits more visual clusters than Random but fewer
12


than Human on average. Its mean entropy is lower than Random’s. The Human–NitroGen entropy ordering
depends on the cluster count. These results do not establish a fixed ordering of exploration ability.
The limitations of NitroGen are clear. The frozen controller can select locally relevant action types, but it
does not reliably turn those reactions into obstacle completion, external controllability, or broad exploration.
While NitroGen remains useful as a reactive visual-motor foundation, future systems might need stronger
planning, recovery, and control interfaces.

APPENDIX
To test the validity of the main conclusions of our study regarding D1, D2, and D3, additional experiments
were conducted. The main findings corroborate the key conclusions reported in the main part of the paper.
The details of the extensive experiments we conducted are reported in the following three Appendices.
A
Additional D1 Experiments
A.1
Setup
We tested three starting regions in Hollow Knight: Spawn, Corridor, and the jump section. This analysis uses
19 trials with complete local action logs. There are 10 Spawn trials, four Corridor trials, and five jump-section
trials. An earlier summary also included 11 externally supplied rows. We exclude those rows here because
their full action logs were not available for this analysis.
Each trial contains 21,600 issued control steps. We compare steps 0–3,599 with steps 18,000–21,599. These
are the first and last 60 seconds under the nominal 60-Hz control schedule. They are not measurements from
an engine-frame counter. Both windows come from the same trial.
We reset the model context before each trial. These trials did not use explicitly paired random seeds. We
used a game trainer and recorded routes to reach the starting regions. Position reset was not a full save-state
restore. Health, enemies, and prior game history could differ. Exact player coordinates were not matched.
This is therefore not a comparison in which only the scene changes while all other state is fixed.
We classify horizontal input as left, right, or no horizontal input. We use the stronger absolute value of
normalized left-stick X and net horizontal D-pad input. The stick wins ties. Values below the ±0.25 threshold
count as no horizontal input. This does not mean that the character is idle. Jump and attack are the
frequencies of the SOUTH and WEST buttons. Movement, jumping, and attacking can occur together.

Window Comparison
Figure A.1 shows the action distributions. Table A.1 gives the jump rates. The last-window jump rates from
Spawn and Corridor are 25.59% and 26.82%. The first-window rate at the jump section is 25.17%. The
similar rates are consistent with a response to the current scene. They do not show that jumping ability
improves over time. They also do not rule out every effect of elapsed time or rollout history.

B
Additional D2 Experiments
B.1
Directional Cues
We collected 372 valid Hollow Knight trials. We tested three starting regions and four cues: a right arrow, a
left arrow, rightward text, and leftward text. Each region/cue combination contains 31 trials. The left arrow
mirrors the right arrow within the same bounding box. The text cues say “Go right. Move the character to
the right.” and “Go left. Move the character to the left.” Green text appears along the bottom of the model
input. Each overlay is present in every input throughout the trial.
Each trial contains 600 control steps under a nominal 60-Hz schedule. This corresponds to 10 nominal control
seconds. Before each trial, we reset the game and followed a recorded route to the intended starting region.
We reset the model context after the route. Each trial uses a distinct recorded model seed. Cue order is
randomized within scene/repetition blocks. The routes do not guarantee identical player coordinates or full
character states.
Twenty trials used an earlier start-region gate. The other 352 used route-defined starts. We retain both
groups in the descriptive summary. One of the 372 valid recordings lacks a completed final reset. A separate
check excludes that recording. The direction-reversal intervals still include zero in that check and in the
route-only analysis.
B.2
Movement and Directional Dominance
We compute left and right action frequencies for each trial. Horizontal direction uses the same combined
stick/D-pad rule as Appendix A. We average the trial frequencies within each region/cue combination. We use
10,000 whole-trial bootstrap resamples for their 95% intervals. We do not treat control steps as independent
trials.
We also report P(R > L). This is the fraction of trials with more right than left actions. Ties remain in
the denominator but do not count as right-dominant. We use 95% Wilson intervals for this measure. The
definition is the same for left and right cues. It is not instruction-following accuracy.
Figure B.1 shows both measurements without repeating the movement plot. Left cues did not produce a
reliable leftward response. For example, at Spawn, rightward actions average 18.81% with the right arrow
and 25.10% with the left arrow. Rightward actions also remain more common than leftward actions under
both arrows.
We compared the right-minus-left action frequency under right and left cues within blocks. All six re-
gion/modality contrast intervals include zero.
None passes the Holm-adjusted 0.05 threshold for this
six-contrast family. This does not prove that the cues have no effect. It shows that these experiments did not
establish reliable direction following.
B.3
Separate Direction-Free Controls
We collected 200 complete control trials in a later campaign. Forty trials have no cue. Each of the four
pixel-scrambled cues also has 40 trials. Each control condition has 14 Spawn trials, 13 Corridor trials, and 13
jump-section trials. The campaign stopped before the planned 465 trials were complete. We exclude the
failed and uncollected trials.
We later collected 53 more no-cue trials. Together with the original 40, these give 93 no-cue trials, with 31 at
each starting region. The original 40 trials are shared between the 200-trial control summary and the 93-trial
no-cue summary. They are not counted twice. Figure B.2 reports the original 200-trial control campaign.
The scrambled overlays preserve the source cue’s colors, pixel histogram, and bounding box. They do not
preserve its spatial layout or spatial frequency. They therefore test a matched set of cue pixels without the
original directional arrangement. They do not control every possible visual property.

C
Additional D3 Experiments
C.1
Trajectories and Human Participants
The additional dataset contains 13 independent NitroGen trajectories, 14 independent Random trajectories,
and four human trajectories. Each human trajectory comes from a different participant. Each trajectory
contributes 900 RGB frames sampled at 1 FPS. There are 27,900 frames in total. All frames are processed to
256 × 256 pixels for the visual analysis.
The four participants had experience playing PC games. None had played Hollow Knight before. They
were unfamiliar with the tested segment. We recruited them as an intermediate-player baseline, but did not
formally test their skill levels. Results may differ for novice or expert players.
All trajectories begin from the common new-game starting point. Before each automated run, we restore the
reference save. Each human participant starts a new game before recording. Automated runs use distinct
recorded seeds and a 900-second active-time budget. Human recordings use accumulated game-foreground
time. These timing records are not a common calibrated engine-frame count.
The pausing regime also differs. Automated runs freeze the game during inference, frame capture, and logging.
Human participants play continuously without these pauses. Human recording pauses when the game loses
foreground focus. The game may continue during that interval. We therefore treat the human comparison as
descriptive.
C.2
Random Policy and Seeds
The Random policy selects uniformly from nine action combinations: left, right, left+jump, right+jump, jump,
left+attack, right+attack, attack, and no input. Each has probability 1/9. Each hold duration is independently
sampled from Uniform(0.5, 1.0) seconds. The policy uses numpy.default_rng(seed). Table C.1 lists the
recorded seeds. The model seed controls NitroGen sampling. The policy seed controls Random action and
hold-duration sampling.

C.3
Shared Visual-State Analysis
We use the frozen SigLIP visual encoder for all agents. Its frame features have 1,024 dimensions. We fit
PCA with 64 dimensions jointly on pooled features from all 31 trajectories. We then fit shared k-means

clusters with K = 50, 100, and 200. Each clustering uses seed 42 and 15 initializations. Clustering is not
fitted separately for each agent. The PCA projection is the same across the three values of K.
We compute three metrics separately for each trajectory: visited-cluster count, Shannon entropy, and the
share of frames in its five most visited clusters. Entropy uses natural logarithms. We then average the
trajectory metrics within each agent group. A visited cluster is a visual category, not a room or a geographic
location.
The native capture resolution was 1920 × 1080 for some automated runs and 1600 × 900 for the others. All
human recordings used 1600 × 900. Resizing all inputs to 256 × 256 does not remove every difference in
rendering. We therefore report a same-resolution check as well as the full dataset.

C.5
Run-Level Uncertainty and Limits
For NitroGen and Random, we bootstrap complete trajectories 20,000 times. Each sampled trajectory keeps
all its frames. Figure C.3 shows the bootstrap distribution of mean entropy. These intervals use fixed
PCA and cluster assignments. They measure run variation under that visual-state definition. They do not
include all uncertainty from fitting the clusters. We show individual human values and their mean, not a
population-level human interval.
The full-dataset two-sided run-level permutation p-values for NitroGen–Random entropy are 0.208, 0.159, and
0.091 at K = 50, 100, and 200. None is below 0.05. These are raw, unadjusted p-values. The comparisons
are exploratory. The three values of K reuse the same trajectories and are not independent replications.
The 1600 × 900 check contains five NitroGen, seven Random, and four Human trajectories. NitroGen’s
mean visited-cluster counts are 29.40, 47.20, and 80.60. Random’s are 27.71, 46.14, and 84.29. Human’s are
40.50, 65.25, and 104.25. Thus the NitroGen–Random coverage ordering changes at K = 200 in this check.
NitroGen still has lower mean entropy than Random at all three values of K. Its mean entropies are 2.62,
3.13, and 3.73, compared with Random’s 2.97, 3.50, and 4.10.

D3: Run-Level Entropy Bootstrap
Figure C.3: Mean-entropy bootstrap from 20,000 resamples of complete automated trajectories. Dashed lines
mark automated-group means. Shaded bands mark their conditional 95% bootstrap intervals. Blue diamonds
show the four observed human entropies; the solid blue line marks their mean. Each panel uses its own fixed
shared clusters. Reported p-values are unadjusted two-sided run-level permutation results.
The new results partly support the original D3 observation of concentrated NitroGen visitation. They do
not support a fixed ordering of exploration ability across all three agents. Visited-cluster count and entropy
measure different properties. A trajectory can briefly visit many clusters but spend most of its time in a
few. Human recordings also contain maps, dialogue, and other interface states. These can increase visual
coverage without indicating new geographic areas. Different timing and pausing procedures further limit the
comparison. More matched runs and direct measures of game progress are needed for broader claims.
