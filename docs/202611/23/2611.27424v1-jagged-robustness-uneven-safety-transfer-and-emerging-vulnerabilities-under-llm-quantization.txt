Jagged Robustness: Uneven Safety Transfer and
Emerging Vulnerabilities under LLM Quantization
Ramneek Agnihotri*
Ruhr University Bochum
Yingte Xu*
Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy
Hans Vath
Ruhr University Bochum
Abstract—Quantization enables efficient inference in language
models through model parameter compression. This work audits
the impact of quantization on capability and safety domains
including model safeguards, instruction-following, ethics and bias.
Through benchmarks, we find that model safeguards and bias
can degrade under quantization, while other capabilities such
as truthfulness remain more stable in Qwen3-4B. We define this
uneven transfer of LLM capabilities under quantization as Jagged
Robustness. We find multiple factors driving Jagged Robustness:
task complexity, evaluation method and generation coherence.
Moreover, we verify the degradation of model safeguards by
observing increased compliance with unsafe requests under
quantization, and demonstrate universal adversarial suffix attacks
that are benign for the full model but jailbreak quantized
models. The findings indicate that it is risky to assume that the
performance and robustness can transfer evenly across different
capability domains from full models to quantized models.
I. INTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in
real-world, safety-critical environments. Quantization methods
are necessary to run these models on hardware with limited
resources. Recent works have shown that quantization can
diminish the performance of specific models in certain capa-
bility domains, including reasoning [1], bias [2] and model
safeguards [3]. In this work, we build on previous research by
auditing LLM quantization decay across diverse benchmarks,
revealing the concept of Jagged Robustness: the non-uniform
degradation of capabilities and safeguards across domains and
evaluation types due to quantization.
We address the following research questions:
• RQ1: Do the capabilities of a quantized model transfer
consistently across various domains?
• RQ2: What factors determine whether a model’s perfor-
mance on a benchmark remains robust or degrades under
quantization?
• RQ3: Does quantization increase an LLM’s vulnerability
to adversarial attacks targeting its safeguards?
We investigate these effects passively using multiple-choice
and free-generation benchmarks, and actively by deploying a
quantization-aware adversarial suffix attack. Through passive
evaluations, we establish a wide view of the LLM’s behavior
under quantization. Concretely, we evaluate the model across
several quantization configurations (AWQ, INT4 and INT8),
comparing them to the BF16 baseline across domains such as
reasoning, code generation, and model safeguards.

Our key findings are the following:
• Not all model capabilities transfer evenly under quan-

as instruction-following, safety refusal, and deception,
while other domains remain stable, demonstrating Jagged

• We identify factors for benchmark degradation such as
task nuance, evaluation method and model coherence.
• We identify adversarial suffix attacks that are benign for
the BF16 model, but successfully jailbreak the INT4
model.
In summary, our findings suggest that models cannot be
assumed to have the same safeguards or capabilities when
quantized, implying that model safeguards and capabilities
must be actively audited post-quantization before deployment,
especially in safety-critical domains.

II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
Quantization maps higher-precision representations (such as
BF16) of the model parameters to lower-precision formats (such
as 8-bit integers). This sharply reduces the memory footprint,
but can also introduce a loss of model accuracy. We focus on
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ), which applies quantization
to a pre-trained model without additional training, and on
Weight Quantization, as these are the most widely adopted
strategies in practice due to their low computational costs.
Benchmarks assess model performance across a diverse range
of evaluation protocols. In multiple-choice and log-likelihood
ranking, the model is presented with predefined answer options.
Performance is evaluated either by regex-parsing or calculating
the log-likelihood of each answer candidate and selecting the
highest-probability option. In free-form generation, the model

In prior work, free-form generation (FFG) revealed severe
degradation in safety alignment [3]. Marcuzzi et al. [4] further
explored the reduction in safety alignment due to quantization.
Beyond pure safety alignment, Hong et al. [5] also highlighted
the uneven impact of model compression on different alignment
domains. Especially uneven safety transfer in quantization
was evaluated through mechanistic interpretability methods by
Kadadekar [6]. These findings motivated our work to focus
on specific factors that cause Jagged Robustness such as task
complexity or generation coherence.
Safety alignment can also be deliberately challenged through
jailbreak attempts, where an adversary feeds malicious prompts
to the model to elicit harmful responses [7], [8]. One prominent
approach is the adversarial suffix attack, which optimizes a
prompt suffix to jailbreak the model and induce misaligned
generations.
Moreover, Egashira et al. [9] demonstrated attacks exploiting
LLM quantization, while Dong et al. [10] introduced Q-
Misalign, establishing an attack that is benign in full precision
but malicious after quantization. Finally, Wang et al. [11] show
that a suffix can be optimized with an objective similar to ours
in Section IV-A.
III. BENCHMARKS
We evaluate quantization effects across diverse domains
using both log-likelihood ranking (LLR) and free-form genera-
tion (FFG) benchmark formats. For LLR tasks, we evaluate
capabilities including expert reasoning on GPQA, domain
knowledge on MMLU and MMLU-Pro, and general science
knowledge on ARC, while for the alignment domain we
evaluate social bias on BBQ, factuality on TruthfulQA, and
moral reasoning on ETHICS via the lm-evaluation-harness [12]
For FFG tasks, we test code generation on HumanEval and math
capability on MGSMdirect. Furthermore, we test instruction-
following capabilities (InFoBench), safety refusals on danger-
ous, illegal, or inappropriate user requests (SORRYBench), the
willingness of the model to comply with deceptive requests
(DeceptionBench), and general utility (WildChat-nontoxic).
2 . We evaluate the generated responses with a DeepSeek-
V4-Flash model as a judge, which determines whether a
response is compliant with benign user instructions (InFoBench
and WildChat-nontoxic) or improperly complies with harmful
requests (SORRYBench and DeceptionBench). All benchmark
experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with
a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens.

B. Free Generation
1) Experiment Setup
For our second set of benchmarks, we compared the model’s
instruction-following ability and general capabilities against
its safeguards. We selected deception and safety refusal as
the two evaluation domains to measure these safeguards. We
compared them against InFoBench, which measures instruction-
following, and WildChat-NonToxic, which contains general,
nontoxic prompts from real users.
We evaluated the Qwen3-4B model using a 5-shot con-
figuration, as we found this setup yielded more coherent
responses than a zero-shot setting. The responses were labeled
by DeepSeek-V4-Flash as the LLM judge across all four
benchmarks and the sample size is N = 300. Moreover,
WildChat, SORRYBench, and InFoBench used the same
evaluation system prompt, which was adapted from the system
prompt of the SORRYBench LLM judge.
2) Results
In Table 2 DeceptionBench shows a significant increase in the

compared to INT4 and INT8. Finally, performance on WildChat-

We observe that instruction-following, safety refusal, and

shows that the model is still able to follow instructions reliably
in most standard cases. On the other hand, particularly in INT4,
the model shows a clear willingness to perform deceptive or

IV. QUANTIZATION-AWARE ADVERSARIAL SUFFIX ATTACK
Passive benchmark evaluations do not account for adversaries
who deliberately exploit the gap between full and quantized
models. Prior quantization-aware attacks [9], [10] require the
adversary to poison the released weights, and PrecisionDiff [11]
searches for precision-induced disagreements on individual
prompts. We ask whether an input-level attack suffices: given
an unmodified open-source model, is there a single universal
suffix that the full model refuses, but that jailbreaks its INT4
weight-only quantized version, as illustrated in Figure 3? Our
claims are restricted to this setting.

Gradients through INT4. Our INT4 model is Qwen3-4B

therefore use a fake-quantized proxy: the weights of every linear
layer except the LM head are quantized group-wise (group
size 128, asymmetric min-max, 4 bits) and dequantized back
to BF16. We verified that the proxy weights are bit-identical to
Quanto’s dequantized INT4 weights, so gradients are obtained
by ordinary backpropagation through the proxy. All reported
evaluations use the real INT4 model.
We evaluate the harmful compliance rate of the BF16 and
INT4 models, with and without the discovered adversarial
suffix, using two judges. The primary LM-Judge follows
SORRYBench and uses its fine-tuned Mistral-7B-Instruct model
to judge whether the generation is an essential fulfillment of the
request. The RE-Judge follows GCG [13] and checks whether
the generation contains typical refusal expressions, such as “I
cannot” or “I am sorry”.
C. Results Analysis
We attack Qwen3-4B in BF16 and INT4 on the base split
of SORRYBench, optimizing a 20-token suffix on 24 sampled
prompts and evaluating on the 426 held-out prompts. We set
λ = 1 and run the search for 500 steps, which took 3 hours
on a node with 4 × A100-40G GPUs (Table 6).
Under the LM-Judge, the suffix widens the compliance
gap between INT4 and BF16 on the test split from 8.22 to
13.62 points. This effect is modest, and it comes mostly from
the BF16 model refusing more often (35.92% to 32.63%),
while INT4 compliance barely changes (44.13% to 46.24%).

V. CONCLUSION
RQ1: Do the capabilities of a quantized model transfer
consistently across various domains?
We have shown that models perform inconsistently on
multiple-choice benchmarks when evaluated using LLR, as
shown in Table 1. In the FFG experiment using the LM judge in
Table 2, we observed that benchmarks that measure model safe-
guards, such as DeceptionBench [14] and SORRYBench [15],
significantly degrade. We conclude that model capabilities do
not transfer uniformly across domains, providing empirical
evidence for Jagged Robustness.
RQ2: What factors determine whether a model’s perfor-
mance on a benchmark remains robust or degrades under
quantization?
In our benchmarks in Table 2, we found that tasks that
are more subjective or vague are more affected by quantiza-
tion than unambiguous tasks. Specifically, the more nuanced
behaviors assessed in DeceptionBench and the open-ended, real-
world prompts in WildChat-NonToxic are more sensitive to
performance decline compared to the explicit safety violations


evaluated in SORRYBench and the deterministic instructions
of InFoBench.
The evaluation method dictates the observed performance
stability under quantization, especially in the smaller Qwen3-
1.7B model. As shown in Table 3 and Table 4, quantization
affects free generation and log-likelihood ranking (LLR)
benchmarks unequally. If the model falls below a certain size
threshold and is quantized too aggressively, it loses its free-
form generation capability, yet it still evaluates validly under
LLR benchmarks. The example in Figure 1 shows that, in
free generation, the model can cascade down an incoherent
generation path. This is completely prevented in the LLR
setting, where the candidate answers are pre-defined and fixed.
Furthermore, another reason why LLR evaluates more
consistently than FFG is that it does not require an LLM judge
or regex parsing. This makes it less sensitive to judgment
failures or parsing errors.
RQ3: Does quantization increase an LLM’s vulnerability
to adversarial attacks targeting its safeguards?
Our benchmarks (Table 2) show that model safeguards
degrade significantly, making Qwen3-4B more likely to gen-
erate deceptive answers and less likely to refuse unsafe
requests. To explore this further, we exploited vulnerabilities
in the quantized models using an adversarial suffix attack.
We discovered adversarial suffixes that trigger more harmful
compliance only on quantized models. This indicates that one
cannot assume the transferability of attack robustness from the
full model to the quantized one.
VI. LIMITATIONS
The benchmark evaluations in Table 2 are judged via a
language model. LLM-as-a-judge is an inconsistent measure
of the model performance. In our quantization-aware attack,
the model tends to produce superficially compliant text, rather
than genuinely informative harmful text. From the perspective
of experiment design, we didn’t investigate the other direction,
whether there are adversarial suffixes that jailbreak only the full
model. We only evaluated the Qwen3 model family, therefore
it is unknown if our results generalize to other model families
or sizes. Lastly, our benchmarks were not run with different
seeds, on only 300 samples, and no confidence intervals were
captured, making it hard to distinguish close data points from
noise or cherry-picking results.
VII. FUTURE WORK
In future work, we want to explore the causes of Jagged Ro-
bustness using quantitative evidence supplied by interpretability
methods. We could analyze the differences between internal
activations and parameters of models and their quantized
versions. The aim is to develop a more objective measure of
quantization decay, compared to benchmark accuracy and attack
success rate. Moreover, our investigation into model collapse
caused by quantization, as demonstrated in Table 5, can be
expanded by analyzing the underlying causes of this behavior.
For example, one could measure the entropy of the model’s
output distribution under free generation and evaluate whether
cascading sampling errors trigger generation collapse. Further-
more, future work could explore whether Jagged Robustness
transfers to other model sizes and architectures. On the attack
side, assessing whether this method succeeds across additional
target models and evaluation datasets, such as DeceptionBench,
is a promising research direction. We could extend our notion
of Jagged Robustness to attack vulnerability if we observe
uneven degradation between different evaluation datasets under
quantization in this domain.
