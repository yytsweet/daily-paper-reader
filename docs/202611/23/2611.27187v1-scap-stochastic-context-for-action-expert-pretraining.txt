Abstract
Single-modality action-expert pretraining controls compe-
tition from visual shortcuts before vision–language–action
(VLA) adaptation. Yet single-modality pretraining can im-
pair the vision–language interactions needed for subse-
quent joint prediction.
We construct a diagnostic model
in which VA or LA pretraining can disable an initially ac-
tive pathway for joint vision–language modeling. In this
model, subsequent training with complete visual and lan-
guage inputs cannot restore the pathway, even with unlim-
ited adaptation. We therefore introduce Stochastic Context
for Action Expert Pretraining (SCAP), a new action-expert
pretraining stage that precedes full-input training. During
this stage, visual and text tokens are randomly masked be-
fore entering the VLM. In this unified diagnostic model,
SCAP can preserve the joint vision–language modeling
pathway during pretraining and subsequent full-input train-
ing. Compared with direct full-input training, it can also re-
duce reliance on visual shortcuts and increase reliance on
language instructions. SCAP consistently improves perfor-
mance across four different VLA architectures. In real-robot
experiments, it achieves 76.67% average success across
three instruction-conditioned manipulation tasks, outper-
forming full-input pretraining by 25 percentage points.
1. Introduction
Vision–language–action (VLA) models build robot poli-
cies on pretrained vision–language models (VLMs), us-
ing an action expert to turn the observed scene and the
instruction into continuous actions [3]. However, scene–
instruction correlations in demonstrations can encourage vi-
sual shortcuts, allowing policies to ignore language [17, 38].
Because the action expert is randomly initialized, it can
readily inherit these biases and may, in turn, degrade the
pretrained VLM’s representations through backpropagation
during joint fine-tuning. Action expert pretraining is thus

worth exploring to foster language dependence.
Existing recipes counter visual shortcuts by control-

APT [35] and
BayesVLA [34] adopt vision–action (VA) pretraining, first
learning a visuomotor prior and then introducing language.
Conversely, LA4VLA [18] uses language–action (LA) pre-
training without vision to learn instruction–action associa-
tions, exploring both sequential and mixed LA–VLA train-
ing.
Despite their differences, these methods follow the
same paradigm of incorporating a unimodal pretraining
stage preceding full pretraining.
However, single-modality staging can damage the path-
way needed to combine vision and language. For an in-
struction such as “pick up the cup that is the same color
as the plate,” VA pretraining provides the scene without the
instruction specifying color matching, while LA pretraining
provides the instruction without the visual evidence needed
to identify the matching cup. Neither stage directly super-
vises this joint computation, yet the parameters supporting
it continue to change. The risk is therefore not merely that
joint modeling is left untrained, but that its pathway can be
shut down. Under the specified population gradient flow,
subsequent full-input training cannot reopen it, even with
unlimited adaptation. This raises a question: can action ex-

pert pretraining mitigate visual shortcuts while preserving
joint vision–language modeling?
We answer with Stochastic Context for Action Expert
Pretraining (SCAP). SCAP follows a two-stage training
scheme: we first pretrain the action expert by randomly
masking both visual and text tokens, then continue training
with full visual and language inputs. By varying the visi-
bility of both modalities, this pretraining exposes the expert
to diverse combinations of visual and linguistic cues, dis-
couraging visual shortcuts while retaining joint supervision
whenever complementary cues remain visible (Fig. 1). VA,
LA, and full-input pretraining are special cases of SCAP
obtained with different mask distributions.
In the same construction, SCAP meets both require-
ments. First, if masks retain both joint cues sufficiently of-
ten, the joint modeling path never closes (Sec. 3.3). Second,
random masking gives cues from either modality a chance
of being hidden, reducing overreliance on a single modal-
ity. This advantage persists through subsequent full-input
adaptation (Sec. 3.4).
Experiments demonstrate that SCAP outperforms alter-
native pretraining methods, with consistent gains across
four VLA architectures and multiple model scales.
By
intervening on test-set instructions, we further show that
SCAP improves robustness to paraphrasing and sensitivity
to changes in task intent. On three real-robot manipulation
tasks, SCAP achieves an average success rate of 76.67%,
exceeding full-input pretraining by 25 percentage points.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We identify a key limitation of unimodal action expert
pretraining: it can shut down certain pathways for joint
vision–language modeling.
• We propose SCAP, which randomly masks visual and text
tokens during action expert pretraining to address visual
shortcuts while preserving vision–language modeling.
• We establish a controlled evaluation protocol spanning
multi-task simulation, paired language interventions, and
real-robot joint grounding to test instruction reliance and
the preservation of joint conditioning.
2. Related Work
Action generation and expert pretraining.
Recent VLA
models, including π0.5 [25] and GR00T N1.7 [22], cou-
ple pretrained VLMs with action experts for continuous
robot control. Action expert pretraining seeks to establish
useful priors before full VLA adaptation. APT [35] and
BayesVLA [34] derive staged training from a Bayesian fac-
torization of the VLA policy into a vision–action prior and
an instruction likelihood. LA4VLA [18] learns instruction–
action associations from atomic language–action episodes
without visual input. Jing et al. [13] pretrain an action-
only encoder–decoder and transfer its motion prior through
decoder reuse and latent distillation.
SCAP generalizes
modality-restricted conditioning through simple stochastic
masking to mitigate visual shortcuts while preserving joint
vision–language learning.
Dropout and multimodal masking.
Dropout regularizes
neural networks by randomly removing units [28], with es-
tablished connections to adaptive regularization [30]. Mod-
Drop [20] applies multimodal dropout to gesture recogni-
tion, while masked autoencoders [10] reconstruct missing
image content. SCAP uses masking to address a different
training problem: it randomly masks visual and language
tokens at the VLM input to mitigate visual shortcuts arising
from modality imbalance in VLA pretraining data.

3.5. Architecture and Two-Stage Training
Model configuration.
Our configuration uses a mixture-
of-transformers (MoT) architecture with a Qwen3.5-9B
vision–language backbone [26] and an approximately
900M-parameter action expert. Figure 2 illustrates the two-
stage training process and the overall model architecture.
Stage 1: masked context, expert-only updates.
We ini-
tialize and freeze the pretrained VLM. After visual encod-
ing and before the language backbone, SCAP uniformly se-
lects a fixed-size subset (30%) of pooled image-content and
instruction tokens and zeros their complete embeddings in
place. Token positions are preserved, with no mask-token
replacement or rescaling. Only the expert and action-side
projections are optimized using Eq. (2); Appendix F gives
implementation details.
Stage 2: complete context, joint model updates.
We
carry the Stage-1 weights into full-context VLA training.
SCAP input masking is disabled, and the VLM is unfrozen;
both ϕ and θ are updated on complete visual observations
and instructions using the same action-target convention.
Inference uses complete, unmasked inputs.
4. Experiments
We evaluate SCAP through multi-task performance, lan-
guage robustness and intent sensitivity, and language-
conditioned real-robot manipulation.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks.
We
use
two
simulation
benchmarks.
RoboTwin 2.0 [7] comprises 50 bimanual manipulation
tasks with Clean (Easy) and Randomized (Hard) evaluation
settings. RoboCasa-GR1 [23, 27] contains 24 humanoid
tabletop tasks, including 6 PnPClose and 18 PosttrainPnP
tasks. We report success rates for each setting or group and
the within-benchmark average.
Implementation Details.
Both pretraining stages are
trained for 100k optimization steps with a global batch size
of 512, while benchmark-specific SFT uses a global batch
size of 256. We adopt AdamW with a weight decay of 0.01
and an initial action-expert learning rate of 10−4. A 1000-
step warmup is followed by cosine learning-rate decay, with
a minimum VLM learning rate of 10−6 in Stage 2. Visual
observations comprise up to three camera views at 224×224
resolution, and the policy predicts 16-step action chunks.
4.2. Training Data
We use approximately 1.2 million action-labeled trajec-
tories from real-robot and synthetic datasets, processed
with a shared pipeline.
State/action fields share a 46-
dimensional layout with quaternion end-effector poses and
calibrated gripper commands, while retaining native coor-
dinate frames and reference points. Quality checks assess
pose availability, kinematic consistency, temporal smooth-
ness, state–action alignment, and numerical validity. Con-
trolled routes share data within each stage; instruction in-
terventions are evaluation-only. Appendix G details source
composition, conversion rules, filtering statistics, and re-
porting limitations.

4.4. Ablation Studies
Text Perturbations.
Guided by the visual-shortcut anal-
ysis in Sec. 3.4, we compare Qwen3.5-9B and five training
routes on four RoboCasa-GR1 pick-and-place tasks [27].
Paraphrases preserve intent; minimal object or destina-
tion edits change the goal in the same scene, probing
cue–instruction conflict rather than directly flipping visual
cues.
Each task uses 100 shared scenes and two para-
phrases per scene, with independently reset rollouts, com-
plete inputs, and matched inference settings. Table 2 re-
ports equal-weight task means. SCAP’s high paraphrase
success and small drop from original instructions indicate
wording robustness while retaining semantic sensitivity.
Lower old-goal execution and higher new-goal success in-
dicate redirection to the requested goal rather than inac-
tion. Paired correctness requires both original- and edited-
instruction rollouts to succeed and cannot exceed either
single-instruction success rate. It combines goal selection
and physical execution, whose residual failures cannot be
separated by aggregate scores. Together, these results sup-
port reduced visual shortcuts and preserved joint vision–
language grounding after full-context adaptation.

5. Conclusion
We show that single-modality action-expert pretraining can
compromise joint vision–language modeling. To address
this, we introduce SCAP, a pretraining stage that randomly
masks visual and text tokens before full-input training. Our
diagnostic analysis shows that SCAP can preserve joint
modeling pathways while reducing visual shortcuts and
strengthening reliance on language instructions.
Exper-
iments across different VLA architectures and real-robot
manipulation tasks demonstrate consistent performance im-
provements. More broadly, SCAP provides a unified frame-
work for action-expert pretraining based on mask distribu-
tions, with vision-only, language-only, and full-input pre-
training recovered as special cases.

F. Token-Masking Implementation
Sampling.
For each sample, we pool the expanded image-
content positions and instruction tokens into N eligible po-
sitions and uniformly select K = ⌊Nr + 0.5⌋for embed-
ding zeroing without replacement, with r = 0.3 by de-
fault. This is fixed-count sampling over both modalities,
not independent Bernoulli sampling or a separate quota per
modality. System-prompt tokens, role and vision delimiters,
and padding are excluded. All instruction-content embed-
dings may be zeroed; no minimum-content constraint is im-
posed. At r = 1, all eligible content embeddings are ze-
roed, while protected-token embeddings and the separately
supplied state remain available.
Encoding and conditioning.
All valid images first pass
through the vision encoder.
Selected multimodal token
embeddings are then zeroed in place before the language
backbone, retaining sequence positions and original multi-
dimensional RoPE coordinates. This is content-embedding
masking, not token deletion, sequence compaction, or re-
placement with a learned mask embedding.
Sequence-
validity masks accompany the inputs through the back-
bone and action decoder to identify padding. The expert
reads the full-attention-layer K/V from this masked-input
forward pass. Zero input embeddings need not yield zero
hidden states or K/V, since subsequent contextual process-
ing can propagate retained information to masked positions.
No retention-dependent rescaling or additional embedding-
dimension dropout is applied.
Masking does not reduce
vision-encoder computation, and unmasked visual features
may encode information from other image regions.
Validation and theoretical scope.
Stage-1 validation re-
tains masking with a separate fixed-seed random stream;
evaluation mode alone does not disable this preprocessing.
Stage-2 training, downstream benchmark evaluation, and
inference use complete inputs. With R = N −K eligi-
ble tokens left unmasked, two distinct eligible tokens are
jointly unmasked with probability R(R −1)/[N(N −1)]
for N > 1, rather than (R/N)2. These token-level proba-
bilities are not the diagnostic’s semantic-cue probabilities:
the independent-cue analysis characterizes the mechanism,
not an exact guarantee for the implemented VLA.

H.2. Demonstrations and Idle-Frame Removal
Each task contains two instruction conditions with 50
demonstrations each. Base selection refers to the red or
natural-wood base; roof selection refers to the orange trian-
gular or red rounded roof. Color matching counts both roof
placements as one episode. All three task datasets use the
same idle-frame removal rule. A transition is idle only when
the maximum absolute change across the seven joint angles
is below 10−4 rad and the gripper-width change is below
10−4 m. Leading, trailing, and internal near-stationary tran-
sitions are removed; adjacent retained frames are rechecked
iteratively.
There is no minimum stationary-duration re-
quirement or fixed waiting-frame window around gripper
events.
The procedure retains the starting frames of active tran-
sitions and the final active target frame, while selecting all
three images and state synchronously. If original retained
indices are r0, r1, . . ., the reconstructed state is x[rk] and
the action target is x[rk+1]. Labels therefore point to the
next retained measured state, not necessarily the original
next frame. The last observation has no successor and is not
assigned a cross-episode action target. Original frame map-
pings, timestamps, and inter-frame intervals are retained,
even though exported video is rearranged at 30 fps. A 32-
target chunk consequently has no fixed original-time dura-
tion.
Conversion checks preserve the number of gripper open-
ing/closing events and inspect jumps across removed inter-
vals. Additional joint, gripper, and end-effector translation
changes relative to the original interval’s largest single-step
change are bounded by 0.0005 rad, 0.0001 m, and 0.0005 m,
respectively. These are splice checks, not a justification for
removing contact waits during deployment. Episode lists
and held-out test layouts are fixed separately from check-
point selection.

The expert is a 32-layer hybrid-attention MoT with hid-
den width 1024 and MLP width 4096. Adaptation predicts
32 actions, with no action-position embedding. Each task-
specific policy is trained for 10,000 updates on eight GPUs
with per-GPU batch size 8 and no gradient accumulation
(global batch 64). AdamW uses learning rate 5 × 10−5,
weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping 1.0, and a 500-update
warmup followed by cosine decay. VLM weights use BF16;
training uses DeepSpeed ZeRO-1 with expert and VLM gra-
dient checkpointing disabled. Evaluation always uses the
final 10,000-update checkpoint.
H.5. Paired Layouts and Counterbalancing
Each route uses the same 10 held-out layouts per task,
with two instructions evaluated separately after resetting the
scene, policy history, and action cache. Instruction order is
randomized.
For base selection, the red and natural-wood bases ex-
change positions across layouts; the instruction names the
base color, not a spatial location. For roof selection, the
triangular and rounded roofs exchange candidate positions
across layouts. For color matching, roof positions and body
positions vary independently, so that a fixed same-color or
different-color instruction requires different spatial pairings
across layouts.
