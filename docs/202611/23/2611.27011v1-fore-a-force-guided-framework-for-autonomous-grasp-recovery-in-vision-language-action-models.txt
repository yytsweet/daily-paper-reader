FoRe: A Force-Guided Framework for Autonomous Grasp Recovery in
Vision-Language-Action Models
Shuai Peng
Yongkai Liao
Yuxin Cheng
Haoyuan Wang
Jian Huang
Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have demonstrated
strong performance in grasp-based manipulation, yet re-
liable recovery from local grasping failures remains chal-
lenging. Specifically, in pick-and-place tasks, policies may
continue transport after a failed grasp instead of retrying,
potentially reflecting overreliance on proprioceptive cues
for stage progression and limited retry supervision. Exist-
ing recovery approaches typically rely on deployment-time
guidance or demonstration augmentation. We introduce
Force-guided Recovery (FoRe), a framework for autonomous
recovery from local grasping failures. Using force feedback,
FoRe partitions demonstrations into approach–grasp and
transport–placement stages to train separate policies. To
supplement limited retry supervision, FoRe then initializes a
dedicated retry policy from the approach–grasp policy and
refines it through local reinforcement learning (RL) from
post-failure states. To avoid inappropriate stage progression,
a controller combines force feedback with grasp-command
status at inference to coordinate the stage policies, detect
grasp failures, and invoke the retry policy. We also con-
struct LIBERO-SUCTION, a force-augmented suction ma-
nipulation benchmark adapted from LIBERO, with complete
demonstration trajectories for policy training. FoRe consis-
tently improves task success rates for VLA-Adapter and π0.5
on LIBERO-SUCTION, LIBERO, and real-world tasks. On
LIBERO-SUCTION, average absolute gains are 18.4% and
6.5%, respectively. The corresponding real-world absolute
gains are 25.0% and 21.7%. FoRe also remains effective
with only one demonstration per task on both simulation
benchmarks.
1. Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models leverage pretrained
visual-language knowledge and diverse robot data for gener-
alizable manipulation [10, 11, 20, 25, 31], with advances in
action generation, spatial understanding, reasoning, and long-
horizon manipulation [2, 9, 12, 22, 30]. However, especially
in pick-and-place tasks, reliable recovery from local execu-
tion errors remains challenging: an imitation-trained VLA
policy may continue transport after a failed grasp, instead of
retrying [17, 29]. Such behavior can turn a recoverable local
error into overall task failure, motivating autonomous retries
and task resumption.
Two related limitations may explain why VLA policies
fail to recover after an unsuccessful grasp. First, as shown
in Fig. 1, manipulation tasks can be viewed as sequences
of stages, such as the approach–grasp stage and the trans-
port–placement stage, each with its own distinct objective.
Because robot proprioceptive states often correlate with task
progress in successful demonstrations, a policy may rely
excessively on these proprioceptive state cues to transition
to the corresponding stage, regardless of the actual grasp
outcome [4, 29]. Consequently, after a failed grasp, a robot
proprioceptive state resembling those associated with trans-
port may prompt the policy to persist in generating transport
actions rather than retrying the grasp. Second, training dom-
inated by uninterrupted successful demonstrations provides
limited supervision for interrupting nominal execution and
re-approaching the object after failure [29]. The policy may
therefore lack an effective response even when failure cues
are observable.
Recent studies have explored several approaches for
robotic recovery. One direction uses human feedback [1,
16, 23] or vision-language model (VLM) guidance [5, 14,
18, 28] to monitor execution outcomes and guide stage pro-
gression. Another direction augments demonstrations [29]
with varied robot poses and corrective trajectories to weaken
correlations between pose and task progress and expand retry
supervision. Force feedback provides direct physical cues
about contact and load changes, making it an effective signal
for detecting grasp failures. However, an explicit mechanism
that uses such feedback to coordinate grasp retries and task
resumption in VLA policies remains underexplored.
We introduce Force-guided Recovery (FoRe), a frame-
work for autonomous recovery from local grasping failures
in VLA-based manipulation. FoRe first uses force feedback
to partition successful demonstrations into approach–grasp
and transport–placement stages, training a separate policy for
each. To supplement limited retry supervision, FoRe then ini-
tializes a dedicated retry policy from the approach–grasp pol-
icy and refines it through local reinforcement learning (RL)

2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have substantially
advanced robotic manipulation by transferring pretrained
visual and linguistic knowledge to action generation [31].
OpenVLA [11] demonstrates strong language grounding and
adaptation to new tasks, while OpenVLA-OFT [12] further
improves task performance and inference efficiency. π0 [2]
and π0.5 [9] extend VLA capabilities to dexterous control
and long-horizon manipulation in diverse environments. Spa-
tialVLA [22] incorporates 3D spatial representations to im-
prove spatial understanding, while CoT-VLA [30] introduces
visual chain-of-thought reasoning by predicting intermediate
goal images before action generation.
Despite their strong generalization within the training
task distribution, these models have limited autonomous
recovery capabilities. Consequently, minor execution errors
can escalate into task failures, substantially compromising
the reliability of VLA task execution.
2.2. Failure Recovery in Robotic Manipulation
Human-guided approaches use language feedback to redi-
rect execution and improve robot policies. RT-H [1] supports
interventions through language descriptions of low-level mo-
tions, while Yell at Your Robot [23] incorporates real-time
verbal corrections into hierarchical policies. OLAF [16]
uses verbal corrections to relabel actions for policy updates.
Obtaining such corrective feedback requires human participa-
tion, which can limit scalability. VLM-based approaches au-
tomate failure analysis and recovery guidance: FailSafe [14]
predicts recovery actions, and CycleVLA [18] combines
VLM-guided subtask backtracking with minimum Bayes
risk decoding for retries. FLARE [29] combines learned
retries with reset skills invoked by an online multimodal
monitor, while ReCoVLA [7] uses VLM-based failure de-
tection to activate residual recovery policies. Online VLM
supervision can introduce additional inference overhead and
latency [18].

2.3. Reinforcement Learning for VLA Models
RL improves VLA policies through interaction and task-
outcome feedback, with gains in performance and execution
generalization [13, 17]. ConRFT and RECAP study RL-
based post-training for real-world policy improvement [3, 8].
PLD trains residual specialists to collect recovery trajectories
for fine-tuning the base policy [27]. SRPO uses model-
generated successful trajectories as references for progress-
based rewards on failed attempts [6].
Compared to the above works, FoRe incorporates RL as
a local refinement step within a broader recovery framework,
optimizing only a dedicated retry policy from post-failure
states.

3.2. Framework Overview
The FoRe aims to interrupt inappropriate task progression
after a failed grasp and enable autonomous grasp retries
through physical interaction feedback. Using force-guided
stage annotation (Sec. 3.3), we partition Ddemo into ap-
proach–grasp and transport–placement subsets, then inde-
pendently fine-tune the approach–grasp policy M1 and the
transport–placement policy M2 on their respective subsets,
as illustrated in Fig. 2(a).
As shown in Fig. 2(b), we initialize the retry policy M3
from M1 and refine it through local RL focused on grasp
retries (Sec. 3.4).
At inference, a force-guided controller (Sec. 3.5) uses
force measurements to select M1 for nominal approach and
grasp, M2 for transport and placement, or M3 for retries
following detected grasp failures, as shown in Fig. 2(c).

3.4. RL-Based Retry Policy Optimization
Successful demonstrations provide limited supervision for re-
tries after failed grasps. To learn the retry behavior, we initial-
ize the retry policy M3 from M1 to reuse the approach–grasp
behavior learned through stage-specific training. We then
refine M3 through local RL in grasp-retry scenarios to adapt
this behavior to states encountered after failed grasps.
To construct grasp-retry scenarios, we sample robot poses
from an early temporal window of each Stage 2 demonstra-
tion segment. These early transport poses resemble those
the robot may assume after a failed grasp. During initial-
ization, we set the robot to the sampled pose and leave the
target object at its original, ungrasped pose. The resulting
scene simulates the state after a failed grasp and serves as
the starting point for optimizing M3 through RL.
For each initialized scenario, as shown in Fig. 2(b), we
collect G stochastic rollouts sharing the same initial state
and instruction ℓ. Let oi,t = (vi,t, xi,t, ℓ) denote the pol-

stochastic sampling to the action-generation mechanism of
each backbone. For flow-based action generation, repre-
sented by π0.5 [9], we sample Gaussian initial noise and iter-

3.5. Force-Guided Policy Switching
At inference, as shown in Fig. 2(c), the controller combines
force measurements with grasp-command status to switch
among three policies: M1 for nominal approach and grasp,
M2 for transport and placement, and M3 for retries following
detected grasp failures.
Nominal stage transitions. During nominal execution, the
controller starts with M1 for routine approach and grasp,
and switches to M2 for transport and placement once ut = 1
and the force magnitude ρt exceeds ϵc, indicating contact
during an active grasp attempt. After placement, a release
command (ut = 0) indicates completion of the release, upon
which the controller returns to M1 for the next grasp cycle if
the task continues.

magnitude remains approximately stable at a nonzero level

4. Experiments
We evaluate whether the FoRe improves end-to-end task
completion across manipulation tasks and VLA backbones.
Our experiments address two questions:
Question 1. How does the FoRe affect task success relative
to VLA baselines in simulation and on a physical robot?
(Secs. 4.1 and 4.2)
Question 2. Does FoRe remain effective with one trajectory
per task? (Sec. 4.3)
We evaluate FoRe on LIBERO-SUCTION, LIBERO [15],
and real-world tasks, including one-demonstration training
on both simulation benchmarks. Experiments use a server
with eight NVIDIA 4090 GPUs.
4.1. Performance on Simulation Benchmarks
LIBERO-SUCTION Benchmark. We construct LIBERO-
SUCTION to study autonomous grasp recovery in suction
manipulation, adapting LIBERO with a suction gripper and
force feedback for monitoring physical interaction. Its 22
tasks span Suction-Spatial, Suction-Object, and Suction-
Goal, assessing spatial relations, object identification, and
goal-directed execution, respectively. The benchmark in-
cludes 1,100 complete demonstration trajectories for VLA
training, matched to the evaluation environments.
LIBERO Benchmark.
We also evaluate FoRe on
four LIBERO task suites—Spatial, Object, Goal, and
Long—using the original gripper and environments aug-
mented with force feedback. Together, the two benchmarks
cover parallel-jaw and suction-based grasping.
Baselines.
We compare eight VLA baselines:
Open-
VLA [11], OpenVLA-OFT [12], π0 [2], π0-FAST [21],
SmolVLA [24], GR00T N1 [19], VLA-Adapter [26], and
π0.5 [9]. We evaluate FoRe on VLA-Adapter and π0.5, com-
paring its performance with CycleVLA [18].
Evaluation protocol. Each model is evaluated over 50
episodes per task: 1,100 episodes across 22 tasks in three
LIBERO-SUCTION suites, and 2,000 episodes across 40
tasks in four LIBERO suites. We report success rates (%)
per suite and across all evaluated tasks in each benchmark.

We ablate FoRe’s components and switching rules on
LIBERO-SUCTION using VLA-Adapter, the full demon-
stration set, and the protocol in Sec. 4.1 (Table 5).
Disabling failure-triggered retries (w/o Grasp Retries)
while retaining M1, M2, and their force-guided switching
rules reduces overall success from 87.6% to 76.2%. Reusing
M1 directly for retries without RL refinement (w/o Retry
Policy Optimization) achieves 82.4%. Thus, the approach–
grasp policy alone provides an absolute gain of 6.2% over
disabling retries, indicating a degree of retry capability, while
RL refinement contributes a further 5.2% by adapting this
policy to retry scenarios.
Replacing M1 and M2 with a unified policy P trained
on complete trajectories (w/o Stage-Specific SFT) reduces
overall success to 78.7%, an absolute decrease of 8.9%. This
variant retains phase tracking for switching; M3 is initialized
from P and optimized with the same RL procedure and retry-
state sampling protocol. This result supports stage-specific
training, which isolates the approach–grasp policy as the
initialization for M3, providing a better starting point for RL
refinement and more effective grasp retries.
We further examine switching rules while keeping demon-
stration annotation and policy training unchanged. Remov-
ing the ON/OFF grasp-command conditions while retain-
ing force thresholds (w/o Gripper-State Conditions) reduces
overall success to 80.1%, an absolute decrease of 7.5%. Here,
low force during M2 always invokes M3, making intentional
release indistinguishable from grasp failure and potentially
triggering unnecessary retries that interrupt task progression.
Removing force from switching rules (w/o Force Switch-
ing) reduces overall success to 74.3%, an absolute decrease
of 13.3%. ON and OFF commands now trigger M1 →M2
and M2 →M1, respectively, while M3 is never invoked with-
out force-based failure detection. This result underscores the
necessity of force feedback for post-failure recovery.
6. Conclusion and Limitation
We presented FoRe, a framework that combines force-guided
stage control with local retry learning for autonomous grasp
recovery in VLA-based manipulation. We observed con-
tinued transport after failed grasps, potentially reflecting
overreliance on proprioceptive cues for stage progression
and limited retry supervision. To address these issues, FoRe
combines separate training of two stage-specific policies
with local RL refinement of a dedicated retry policy, using
force feedback alongside grasp-command status to detect
grasp failures and control stage transitions. Experiments on
LIBERO-SUCTION, LIBERO, and real-world tasks show
consistent improvements, with average real-world success
increasing from 26.7% to 51.7% for VLA-Adapter and from
48.3% to 70.0% for π0.5. These findings suggest that force
feedback grounds stage control and retry learning in phys-
ical grasp outcomes, enabling more reliable autonomous
recovery in VLA policies.
However, as an external recovery framework, FoRe relies
on the approach–grasp policy to establish contact with the
target object. If the policy consistently fails to make contact
with the target, this may impair the ability of FoRe’s force-
guided controller to advance stages, detect grasp failures,
and invoke the retry policy, thereby limiting autonomous
grasp recovery. Future work will focus on guiding VLA
policies to reliably bring the gripper into contact with the
target object.
