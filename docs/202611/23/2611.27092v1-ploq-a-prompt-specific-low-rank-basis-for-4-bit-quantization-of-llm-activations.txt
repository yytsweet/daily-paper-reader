PLOQ: A PROMPT-SPECIFIC LOW-RANK BASIS FOR 4-BIT QUANTIZATION
OF LLM ACTIVATIONS
Faraz Tahmasebi 1 Michael Pellauer 2 Hyoukjun Kwon 1 Sitao Huang 1
ABSTRACT
Low-bit activation quantization of large language models remains challenging, as a small number of dominant
directions dictate the quantization range. State-of-the-art methods isolate these directions in a high-precision
subspace through transformations calibrated offline and folded into the weights, which fixes the subspace for all
prompts, leaving each prompt’s few unique directions unprotected, and rules it out wherever no weight can absorb
the transformation. We present PLoQ, a prompt-adaptive low-rank decomposition that extracts the dominant
activation components before quantization, quantizes them separately, and restores them within the layer’s GEMM,
without modifying the weights. The low-rank component’s basis is estimated from each prompt during prefill and
reused throughout decoding. At the same bit width as state-of-the-art mixed-precision quantization (4-bit weights,
activations and K/V cache with an eighth of the channels at 8 bits, about 4.2 bits per activation on average),
PLoQ outperforms it on all six Llama and Qwen models from 1B to 70B parameters, at up to 8k tokens (16k on
two of them), recovering up to 46% of the perplexity that the state of the art still loses to 4-bit activations. On
Llama-3.1-8B at 8k tokens it adds only 4% to prefill and 8% to decoding.

INTRODUCTION
Quantization is the most widely used way to reduce the
inference cost of large language models. Weight-only quan-
tization is mature: 4-bit weights lose little accuracy (Frantar
et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024b), and vector quantization with
incoherence processing reaches 2 to 3 bits (Chee et al., 2023;
Tseng et al., 2024a; Egiazarian et al., 2024; Tseng et al.,
2024b). The K/V cache can likewise be stored in 2 to 4
bits (Liu et al., 2024b; Hooper et al., 2024). These methods
reduce memory footprint and traffic, but the matrix mul-
tiplications still run in 16-bit arithmetic. Quantizing the
activations as well (W4A4) enables 4-bit tensor-core arith-
metic and further lowers memory traffic (Ashkboos et al.,
2024b; Lin et al., 2025).
Activations, however, are challenging to quantize at low
precision. A small number of dominant directions carry
magnitudes far exceeding the rest and dictate the per-token
quantization range, leaving the remaining components with
too few effective levels (Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al.,
2023). Rotation-based methods multiply the activations
by orthogonal matrices folded into the adjacent weights,
which spreads the outliers across channels and makes the
distribution smoother (Ashkboos et al., 2024b; Liu et al.,

2025; Lin et al., 2024a; Sun et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025); this
made W4A4 inference with a 4-bit K/V cache (W4A4KV4)
practical. Mixed-precision methods go further (Zhao et al.,
2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024a; Saxena et al., 2025): they
identify the dominant channels or directions on calibration
data and keep them in a small 8-bit block, an eighth of the
channels at three of the four inputs of each layer, which
raises the average activation width from 4 to about 4.2 bits;
they achieve the highest accuracy in this regime. Even so,
activation quantization remains the main source of accuracy
loss: on Llama-3.2-1B and Llama-3.1-8B, quantizing the
activations adds about twice the perplexity that quantizing
the weights and the K/V cache does (Table 3).
These transformations, however, are computed offline and
folded into the weights, which imposes two limitations.
First, the protected subspace is static, whereas the dominant
directions vary considerably across prompts (Section 2.3),
and adapting the whole subspace to each prompt is costly
(Fig. 1). Second, the transformation is applicable only where
a weight can absorb it. The input of the down projection, the
elementwise product of the gate and up projections in the
feed-forward block, has no weight that can absorb a rotation:
rotation-based methods apply only an online Hadamard
transform there, and mixed-precision methods that protect
a subspace quantize it entirely at 4 bits, although its activa-
tions are as concentrated in a few directions as those of the
other layer inputs.

tion for activation quantization. PLoQ does not transform
the activation. It decomposes it: the components along a
small set of dominant directions are extracted before quanti-
zation, quantized on a dedicated scale, and restored as ad-
ditional channels of the layer’s GEMM. This changes what
can be protected, and when. Because no basis is folded into
the weights, the decomposition applies at every layer input,
including the down-projection input that no offline method
can protect. Because nothing is precomputed, the directions
can be chosen per prompt: PLoQ estimates them from each
prompt during prefill, with a randomized range finder fused
into the existing kernels, and reuses them throughout decod-
ing. The low-rank component takes the place of part of the
8-bit block, so the average bit width is unchanged, about 4.2
bits per activation.
Across Llama and Qwen models from 1B to 70B parame-
ters, PLoQ consistently improves on the strongest mixed-
precision method, ResQ (Saxena et al., 2025), at equal bit
width and outperforms rotation-based methods on every
model. The improvement holds from 2k to 16k tokens and
carries over to zero-shot benchmarks. The prompt-adaptive
basis is central to these gains: on the Llama models, a cali-
brated basis of the same rank achieves only 55–77% of its
improvement, and the share is smallest on the largest model
(WikiText-2 perplexity on Llama-3.1-70B: 4.024 with the
calibrated 8-bit block alone, 3.878 with a calibrated low-
rank component, 3.758 with the prompt-adaptive one). On
an RTX 4090, PLoQ adds 4% to prefill latency at 8k tokens.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We show that offline transformations leave a significant
share of the 4-bit activation error unaddressed, as their
subspace is static and unavailable at some layer inputs,
and that capturing a few prompt-specific directions
significantly reduces the remaining error (Section 2.3).
• We propose PLoQ, a prompt-adaptive low-rank decom-
position that protects the dominant directions of any
layer input at equal bit width without modifying the
weights: the directions are estimated from each prompt
and their coefficients restored inside the GEMM (Sec-
tion 3).
• We make the per-prompt fit practical on GPUs: a single
persistent kernel fused into the existing producers and
overlapped with the 8-bit GEMM, so that PLoQ adds
4% to prefill at 8k tokens (Section 4).
• We evaluate PLoQ on six models from 1B to 70B pa-
rameters against mixed-precision, rotation-based and
outlier-channel methods, and analyze the choice of
basis, including calibrated and shrinkage variants (Sec-
tion 5).

BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
2.1
Quantizing activations
A linear layer computes Y = XW ⊤for activations X ∈
RS×H (one row per token) and weights W ∈RN×H.
In W4A4 inference both operands are quantized to 4-bit
integers. Activations are quantized per token, with one
scale per row set by the row’s range, so that the scale
can be computed on the fly as each token arrives. Ev-
ery entry of a token is then rounded on a grid of step
∆t = (maxj xtj −minj xtj)/15. LLM activations are
heavy-tailed: a few feature dimensions are consistently
much larger than the rest (Dettmers et al., 2022), and some
tokens produce extreme values in otherwise quiet chan-
nels (Xiao et al., 2023). Any such value stretches its token’s
∆t, and every other entry of that token is rounded coarsely.

For any orthogonal matrix Q, XW ⊤= (XQ)(WQ)⊤.

weights offline therefore changes the distribution that is
quantized without changing the layer’s output. Rotation-
based methods choose Q to spread every direction over all
channels: a randomized Hadamard transform makes each
channel a mixture of all the others, so an outlier’s energy
is shared rather than concentrated, and the per-token range
shrinks (Ashkboos et al., 2024b). The rotation can also be

Mixed-precision methods instead keep the components that
dominate the error at a higher precision and quantize the
rest at 4 bits. Most select channels by a saliency metric.
LLM.int8() detects, at run time, the feature dimensions
whose magnitude exceeds a threshold and computes them in
16 bits (Dettmers et al., 2022); OliVe pairs each outlier with
a neighboring value that it sacrifices, so that outliers can
be encoded locally in hardware (Guo et al., 2023). Atom
and QuIK keep a fixed set of outlier channels, identified on
calibration data, at 8 or 16 bits (Zhao et al., 2024; Ashk-
boos et al., 2024a); Tender splits the channels into groups
of similar magnitude with power-of-two related scales (Lee
et al., 2024b); and MicroMix gives each channel a 4-, 6- or
8-bit microscaling format according to its magnitude (Liu
et al., 2026). For weights, OWQ, SpQR and MixLLM select
columns, individual weights or output features by their sen-
sitivity to the loss (Lee et al., 2024a; Dettmers et al., 2024;
Zheng et al., 2026). A subspace generalizes a channel selec-
tion (Saxena et al., 2025): a basis U is computed from the
eigenvectors of the activation covariance on calibration data,
and its top-r directions, which carry most of the variance,
form a block kept at 8 bits. The other H −r directions
are rotated among themselves to flatten them and quantized
at 4 bits. With r = H/8, the average precision is 4.5 bits
per element, and the two blocks are quantized with separate
scales, so the dominant directions no longer set the scale
of the 4-bit part. We call such a block, of channels or of
directions, a calibrated block. In all of these methods it
is chosen once, offline, for every prompt. PLoQ keeps a
calibrated block and decomposes what it leaves at 4 bits; it
applies to any of them, and we evaluate it with the subspace
form, the most accurate at equal bits.
2.3
Limits of offline subspaces
A subspace is chosen offline because the basis change into
it must be folded into the weights to be free: into the weight
that produces the activation and, transposed, into the weight
that consumes it. In a Llama-style decoder layer this holds
for three of the four projection inputs. The query/key/value
and gate/up inputs are produced by an RMSNorm whose
scale is folded into the next weights, and the residual stream
is a sum of linear outputs, so a basis applied to it can be
folded into every weight that writes to or reads from it.
The attention-output input is a per-head combination of
value vectors, whose basis can be folded into the value
projection. The down-projection input is different: it is

can absorb the basis of a protected subspace. A dense ba-

60–81%, and the 32 leading directions of two different
prompts overlap by only 1–6% (Section D). A fixed basis

not for a given prompt. A sample of the prompt is enough
to find its own: fitted on a quarter of the prompt’s tokens, 32
directions hold 19–36% of the 4-bit energy of the other to-
kens, against 5–27% for the 32 calibrated ones and 25–40%
for the best possible (Fig. 3). Adapting the entire protected
block to each prompt, however, is costly (Fig. 1). Keeping
each prompt’s own leading eighth at 8 bits instead of the
calibrated one lowers the perplexity of Llama-3.1-8B at 8k
tokens from 6.335 to 6.157, but it needs a rank-H/8 fit,
projection and correction at every input. Even at the GPU’s
peak arithmetic rate this work would add 17% to the prefill
time, and measured without fusion it adds 38%. A rank-32
per-prompt component on top of the calibrated block, as in
PLoQ (Section 3), reaches 6.165 for 4%, and it is better on
tokens after the prompt’s first quarter (5.955 against 5.987).
The leading directions that the calibrated block misses are
few, and a low-rank component that needs no basis change
can capture them at every layer input, including the down
projection.

PLOQ: PROMPT-ADAPTIVE LOW-RANK
QUANTIZATION
The error of 4-bit activation quantization is concentrated in
a few dominant directions of each layer input, each spread
across all of its channels (Section 2.3). An offline rota-
tion, folded into the weights at no runtime cost, maps the
calibrated dominant directions onto r coordinates that are
then kept at 8 bits. Directions that depend on the prompt
cannot be protected this way, since mapping them onto coor-
dinates would require rotating the weights for every prompt,
at the cost of the layer itself. PLoQ therefore subtracts
these directions instead of rotating them (Fig. 2). At each
layer input it measures the activation’s component along
k prompt-specific directions, removes it from the part that
the offline rotation leaves at 4 bits, quantizes the k resulting
coefficients on their own scale, and restores their contribu-
tion after the GEMM through a small k × N matrix. The
remainder keeps its width but no longer contains the domi-
nant directions, so its quantization step is smaller (Fig. 4).
The calibrated 8-bit block is kept as is; only the 4-bit part of
the offline rotation is decomposed.

3.3
Equal average precision
Per token and layer input, the offline method stores 8r +
4(H −r) bits. PLoQ stores r −k values at 8 bits, H −r +k
residual values at 4 bits, and k coefficients at 4 bits:
8(r −k) + 4(H −r + k) + 4k = 8r + 4(H −r). (8)
Trading k protected directions for a rank-k component keeps
the average bits per element unchanged.
At the down-
projection input, where r = 0, the k coefficients are ad-
ditional: 0.2% of the model’s activation bits for k = 32 on
Llama-3.2-1B. The K/V cache is quantized as in the offline
method.
4
KERNEL FUSION FOR PLOQ
Algorithm 1 adds four operations to a layer input: the fit
(lines 1–4, once per prompt), forming the correction matrix
U (line 5), the projection and subtraction (line 7), and the
correction (line 9). For k = 32 their arithmetic is under 1%
of the layer’s GEMMs, so on a GPU their cost is latency
and memory traffic, not computation. A direct implemen-
tation, with each operation as its own kernel, would add
several passes over every activation and one over every out-
put. Our kernels follow one rule instead: add no pass over
a layer’s output and as few as possible over its input, by
fusing each operation into a kernel that the baseline already
runs or appending it to an existing GEMM. The baseline, a
calibrated 8-bit block with the rest at 4 bits, and PLoQ share
one W4A4 pipeline that we implement and tune for both,
with int4 and int8 tensor-core GEMMs in CUTLASS and
the producers and epilogues in Triton. This section follows
a prompt through prefill, where the basis is fitted and U
formed, and then through decoding, where both are reused.
4.1
Fitting the basis during prefill
The fit’s products, X⊤
f Ω, XfY0 and X⊤
f Z (lines 2–3), are
thin: k = 32 columns against a sample of S/4 rows, so they
are bounded by memory bandwidth. At the attention and
FFN inputs, the activation is the RMSNorm of the residual
stream, X = diag(ρ) h diag(w), so every product against
X is a product against h with the k-wide operand scaled
by ρ or w: the fit reads the residual stream directly, with
each token’s normalization factor ρ taken from the sum of
squares that the preceding residual addition accumulates in
its epilogue, and never writes the normalized sample. At
the down-projection input, the sketch of the fit rows is accu-
mulated inside the SwiGLU kernel that produces them. The
kernels map a tile’s rows to the spaced blocks of Eq. (5), so
the sample is never gathered into a separate buffer. These
producer fusions pay off once a batch holds about 8k tokens
or more; for shorter batches, separate kernels are faster, and
we use them there. The fit is a chain of dependent steps,
each too small to fill the GPU: three thin products, a column
normalization after each of the first two, the Gram matrix of
the result, its Cholesky factor, and the orthonormalization.
Launched separately they are about ten kernels per layer
input, and their cost is the latency of the chain rather than
its work. PLoQ runs the three products, both normaliza-
tions and the partial Gram matrices in one persistent kernel,
whose thread blocks are all resident at once and pass from
one step to the next through a barrier in global memory;
the sample is read from memory by the first product and
found in the L2 cache by the other two. A single warp then
factors the k × k Gram matrix (CholeskyQR (Yamamoto
et al., 2015)), and a last small kernel applies the factor. In-
termediate orthonormalizations would not change the span,
so a column normalization between the products keeps the
iteration stable at no cost.
Even fused, the fit sits on the critical path: the projection, the
quantizer and the GEMM all wait for V . The int8 GEMM
of the calibrated block does not: its input is the block itself,
which does not depend on V . PLoQ therefore quantizes the
calibrated block and runs its int8 GEMM on a second stream
while the fit runs, and the int4 GEMM then accumulates into
that GEMM’s output instead of the other way around. The
two GEMMs do the same work as before, and the fit, which
occupies few of the GPU’s multiprocessors, overlaps with a
GEMM that keeps the memory system busy; at 8k tokens
it is hidden entirely at the attention and FFN inputs. The
down-projection input has no 8-bit block, and its fit remains

4.2
Projection, subtraction and correction
Every token, in prefill and in decode, goes through lines 7–9.
The producer of each layer input, the kernel that quantizes
it for the GEMM, also performs the subtraction: it reads the
activation and the coefficients C, and writes the remainder’s
4-bit codes, its clipped per-token scale and the quantized co-
efficients in one launch. The projection C = XcV ⊤needs
every coordinate of a token before the subtraction can start,
so it is computed where the full token is already in hand:
in the RMSNorm pass at the attention and FFN inputs, and
in the SwiGLU kernel at the down-projection input, which
projects onto V before the online Hadamard transform by
using the transformed basis. The correction Q4(C) U is a
rank-k update of the GEMM output, fused into the epilogue
of the kernel that reads that output anyway: RoPE after the
query/key/value projection, the residual addition after the
attention-output and down projections, and SwiGLU after
gate/up. It adds no pass over the output.
The remaining prefill cost is the fit at the down-projection in-
put, the subtraction inside the quantizer, the k extra GEMM
rows and the padding of the wider 4-bit tier to the GEMM’s
tile, and a few small kernels per input. On Llama-3.1-8B
at 8k tokens these add 15 ms to the baseline’s 379 ms (Sec-
tion 5.4).

4.3
Decoding
During decoding, V and U are reused and nothing is fitted.
A single kernel per layer input performs the normalization,
the projection, the subtraction and both quantizer passes
on the new token. The projection is a reduction over the
whole token, which would cost the kernel a synchronization
across thread blocks; instead, the epilogue of the kernel that
produces the token, the residual addition or SwiGLU, accu-
mulates the token’s sum of squares and its projection onto
the next input’s V as it writes the token, and the producer
waits on a single barrier. The sum of squares is offered
to the baseline’s producers as well, since they need it for
RMSNorm. Each sequence keeps V at 4 bits and U at 8
bits, 58.5 MB for Llama-3.1-8B, about the K/V cache of
1.4k tokens; the down-projection producer also keeps its
basis multiplied by the Hadamard transform in 16 bits, an-
other 29 MB. With a calibrated basis, lines 1–5 move offline:
U is stored with the weights, and there is no fit and no
per-sequence state.

RELATED WORK
Quantizing LLMs.
Weight-only methods reach 4 bits
and below with second-order rounding or activation-aware
scaling (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024b). Quantiz-
ing activations as well is harder because of outlier chan-
nels (Dettmers et al., 2022). SmoothQuant migrates their
range into the weights (Xiao et al., 2023), OmniQuant learns
the clipping and scaling parameters (Shao et al., 2024), and
quantization-aware training recovers the rest at a training
cost (Liu et al., 2024a). Systems work makes W4A4 and
W4A8 fast on GPUs (Zhao et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al.,
2024a; Lin et al., 2025). K/V caches are often quantized per
channel for keys (Liu et al., 2024b; Hooper et al., 2024).
Rotations.
QuaRot applies randomized Hadamard trans-
forms that are folded into the weights, plus online Hadamard
transforms where folding is not possible (Ashkboos et al.,
2024b). SpinQuant learns the rotations (Liu et al., 2025);
DuQuant combines rotations with a permutation that spreads
outliers across blocks (Lin et al., 2024a); FlatQuant and OS-
TQuant learn affine or orthogonal-plus-scaling transforms
per layer (Sun et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). All are fixed
before deployment; at the down-projection input, where no
weight can absorb them, they are applied online and keep
the whole input at one precision.
Mixed precision.
Section 2.2 reviews the methods that
keep salient channels or a calibrated subspace at a higher
precision (Dettmers et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2023; Zhao
et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024a; Lee et al., 2024b; Liu
et al., 2026; Saxena et al., 2025). Their selection is made
offline, or, in LLM.int8(), at run time but per channel. PLoQ
keeps whatever block they protect and adds a per-prompt
low-rank component at every layer input.
Low-rank corrections.
Low-rank branches are used to
compensate weight quantization error: LoRC and LQER
add a low-rank approximation of the weight error (Yao et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2024), and SVDQuant absorbs outliers
into a 16-bit low-rank weight branch for W4A4 diffusion
models (Li et al., 2025). These branches correct weight error
in 16 bits; PLoQ’s component removes dominant activation
directions before quantization and carries them at 4 bits,
within the same bit budget.
Input-dependent quantization.
Rotated runtime smooth-
ing computes smoothing factors from the activations at run
time (Yi et al., 2024), and PrefixQuant isolates outlier to-
kens in a prefixed K/V cache (Chen et al., 2026). PLoQ
instead fits the dominant directions of each layer input to
the prompt during prefill and keeps them for decoding.
7
CONCLUSION
Offline transformations protect the dominant directions of
LLM activations only through a static subspace, and only
where a weight can absorb the basis. PLoQ lifts both restric-
tions: it extracts each prompt’s dominant low-rank compo-
nents before quantization, quantizes them separately, and
restores them in the layer’s GEMM, at every layer input at
equal bit width. On six models from 1B to 70B, it improves
on state-of-the-art mixed-precision quantization and outper-
forms rotation-based methods at modest overhead. The gain
holds as sequences grow (16k where measured), and its edge
over a calibrated basis tends to grow with model size.

BASES AND THEIR EVALUATION
Calibrated basis.
For each quantized input, the evaluation runs 16 WikiText-2 training sequences through the already-
quantized preceding layers, accumulates the second moment of the input’s low-precision coordinates (after the online
Hadamard transform at the down-projection input), and keeps its top-k eigenvectors. The basis is fixed before any test
sequence is seen.
Per-prompt and shrunk bases.
The per-prompt basis is estimated on the fit sample (64-token blocks every 256 tokens)
with a randomized range finder (Halko et al., 2011): a Gaussian sketch, one power iteration, and one CholeskyQR. The
shrunk basis (Eq. (7)) uses a rank-512 factor of the calibrated second moment.
Held-out scoring.
A per-prompt basis quantizes the tokens it was fitted on, so their perplexity reflects an in-sample fit.
Held-out perplexity scores only tokens outside the fit sample and after the first quarter of the prompt, for which no basis has
seen the token.

OVERHEAD ON AN A100
Table 14 repeats the overhead measurement of Table 7 on an A100 (80 GB PCIe), with the shared kernels tuned for this
GPU for both methods. One setting differs: on the A100 the producer fusions of the fit (Section 4.1) do not pay off at 8k, so
it uses separate kernels at both lengths (with them, the 8k overhead is 10.1%). The overhead is larger than on the RTX 4090.
The work PLoQ adds consists of thin products, reductions and short synchronized phases, whose speed follows the GPU’s
clock and per-SM throughput, where the A100 is behind; the GEMMs that dominate ResQ’s time are bound by memory
bandwidth, where it is ahead. Against FP16 both W4A4 methods gain less than on the RTX 4090, since the A100’s FP16
tensor-core rate is about twice the RTX 4090’s while its integer rates are similar.
