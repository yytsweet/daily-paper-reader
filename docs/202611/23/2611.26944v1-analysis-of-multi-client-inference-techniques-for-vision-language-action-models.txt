Analysis of Multi-Client Inference Techniques for
Vision-Language-Action Models

University of Toronto

Abstract—Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) are a new
class of machine learning model for generalizable robot control.
While they enable robots to perform complex tasks in unseen
environments, serving VLAs leaves hardware idle between infer-
ence passes and under-utilized during inference. Serving multiple
clients at once can improve utilization, but fundamental multi-
client techniques such as batching and co-execution have not been
characterized for VLAs. We therefore characterize the trade-
offs these known techniques impose across latency, throughput,
power, and hardware utilization. Our findings inform trade-offs
and challenges for future multi-VLA serving systems.
I. INTRODUCTION
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1]–[3] are an
emerging paradigm in robotics that aim to learn generalizable
robotics behavior. They leverage internet-scale vision encoders
(ViT) and vision-language models (VLM) as backbones to un-
derstand image observations and user text-prompt instructions.
A subsequent “action expert” (AE) stage generates a series of
actions to achieve the text-prompted goal (see Figure 1).

Unlike stand-alone VLMs, VLAs typically follow the
ViT encoding and VLM prefill stages with a constant-step
diffusion-based action policy [1], [2], rather than a variable-
length auto-regressive decode [4], [5]. The VLA “action
chunking” paradigm entails that multiple action tokens are
generated per inference pass, instead of a single decoded
language token like with large-language models (LLMs) or
VLMs. As a result, VLA workloads alternate between GPU
inference and on-robot action execution (see Figure 2).
While VLAs have improved robotic generalizability [1],
[6], we find they severely under-utilize GPU compute. Action
chunking leaves the GPU idle between inference passes for
>80% of total task completion time while the robot executes
generated actions. We also observe under-utilization during
inference passes: streaming multiprocessor (SM) throughput
averages 21-27% across models, with the AE’s utilization
being the lowest of all three stages (5-19%). Given the high
cost of GPUs, VLA operators are economically motivated to
maximize hardware utilization [7]. Since single-robot scenar-
ios leave hardware idle, multi-client VLA serving – scheduling
inference for multiple robotic clients on one device – is
a natural setting to study. This setting is most applicable
to warehouse and factory deployments where robot fleets
communicate with an on-premises server [8].
Multi-client serving enables techniques such as batching [4],
[5] and stage co-execution [9], [10] to improve inference-time
utilization, but these have not been characterized for VLAs.
Prior LLM/VLM serving work [4], [5], [9], [10] does not
account for the VLA’s AE or action chunking, and prior multi-
client VLA work [8], [11] details neither the heterogeneous
behavior of VLA stages nor stage co-execution.
We profile popular VLA models [1]–[3], spanning key VLA
architectural axes, to highlight fundamental challenges with
these serving techniques applied to VLAs. Our analysis of
batching shows that it alone cannot improve utilization across
all stages due to the VLM’s and ViT’s compute-bound matrix-
multiplication (GEMM) kernels. Furthermore, intuitions from
LLM serving do not transfer directly to VLAs, since the
latter cannot easily amortize the increased latency incurred
from a batched VLM and ViT. Stage co-execution yields
modest speedups as VLM GEMM kernels block AE kernel
dispatching or slow substantially on partitioned SMs.
Therefore, in this characterization study, we make the fol-
lowing contributions:
• We explain VLA GPU compute under-utilization, both be-
tween and during inference passes, motivating the multi-
client setting we characterize.
• We analyze VLA per-stage hardware utilization, power us-
age, and latency under batching, quantifying trade-offs and
fundamental challenges for multi-client VLA serving.
• We explain the behavior of VLAs under stage co-execution,
highlighting the limitations of co-execution techniques.

B. Evaluation Methodology
To reduce variability, our experiments report averages over
100 runs (fewer for NCU utilization measurements) after five
warm-up runs. We input random images and use a short
text prompt (40 tokens). Our measurements use CUDA syn-
chronization. Utilization metrics come from NVIDIA Nsight
Compute [13].
C. Hardware
We profile each model on an NVIDIA RTX4090 and H100.
The latter is a representative data-center GPU standard for
LLM serving [4]. Although some VLAs have been adapted
for deployment on embedded/onboard devices [14], we target
larger GPUs for two reasons. First, server-side network and
inference latencies are lower than onboard inference latency
in most scenarios [15]. Second, server-side GPUs enable larger
models with further improved robot generalization [6], [16].
III. INTER-REQUEST IDLENESS
Generating and executing action chunks creates alternating
idle-busy periods for the GPU (see Figure 2), which allows
for multi-client scheduling [11]. In Figure 3, we measure the
effect that this action chunking has on GPU idleness.
Fig. 3: Percentage of time the GPU is idle, over different chunk
sizes and control frequencies. Control frequency (in Hz) is the
rate at which the robot executes actions.
Figure 3 shows that the GPU is typically idle >80% of
the time. This idleness arises because generating an additional
action token adds minimal inference latency: increasing the
chunk size from 10 to 75 raises latency by <10%. However,
this same increase in chunk size corresponds to a 750%
increase in action execution time on the robot.
Therefore, action chunking leaves the GPU idle during the
majority of each request cycle, even at high control frequen-
cies and on consumer-grade GPUs. Because a single client
occupies the GPU ∼15% of the time, temporal interleaving
alone can support ∼5-10 clients before the GPU’s idle budget
is consumed. Beyond that, additional clients’ compute periods
must overlap, so utilization gains require techniques that share
the GPU under contention, such as batching and co-execution.
IV. MULTI-REQUEST BATCHING
A. Utilization and Power Analysis
GPUs are costly and consume substantial power. Conse-
quently, VLA model operators have a strong economic incen-
tive to maximize their utilization [7]. However, experiments
reveal that VLA inference fails to saturate GPU compute,
thereby raising the opportunity cost to serve these models.
Batching, a fundamental technique in machine learning serving
[5], [17], combines input tensors so as to better exploit the
GPU’s parallel compute units and achieve higher utilization
[5]. Figure 4 and Figure 5 measure the per-stage hardware
utilization and power (respectively) of three VLA models as
batch size increases. We make the following observations.
First, at a batch size of 1, the AE’s compute utilization
is low, with “SM Throughput %” ranging from 4.6 to 18.7%.
Profiling π0.5 on an RTX4090 (representative case) shows that
the AE’s most time-consuming kernels are GEMM kernels,
yet the average “Tensor Active %” remains below 2%. Two
factors account for this. First, a weight-loading bottleneck:
these kernels have high memory throughput (70-80%) but low
SM throughput (10%). Second, the stage’s key kernels are too
small to saturate the GPU, as the AE computes attention over
a short context, denoising a chunk of only 15 to 50 action
tokens at once [1] while cross-attending to the VLM output.
NCU profiling confirms this: the longest kernels run with low
grid and block sizes. Because they issue too few instructions to
overlap with outstanding memory requests, the weight-loading
latency is exposed on the critical path.
Second, TCU utilization increases with batch size. All
model stages exhibit some gain, but the effect is largest for the
AE. The greatest TCU throughput gains originate from feed-
forward network (FFN) kernels. These kernels grow linearly
with batch size [18], allowing more warps to be scheduled and
producing near-linear increases in TCU throughput.
Third, Figure 5 shows that the power usage of the AE is
characterized by the power required during the weight-loading
bottleneck, which stays constant as batch size increases. For
GR00T’s AE, which has higher TCU utilization at a batch size
of 1, batching the AE incurs a power overhead (∼1.5× at a
batch size of 4).
Fourth, the VLM and ViT stages exhibit higher compute
utilization than the AE. Attending to vision and text tokens
yields a context length of 700 to 1000 tokens, which results
in larger GEMM kernels with higher arithmetic intensity. VLM
GEMMs are also larger than the AE’s since their parameter
count is typically one order of magnitude larger. Our profile
traces show that the VLM’s execution time is dominated
by FFN GEMM kernels (65% of time). Batching therefore
has little effect on VLM compute utilization, as these time-
dominating kernels already have high compute utilization at a
batch size of one.

Fifth, L2 throughput increases with batch size for nearly all
models and stages, since the larger GEMM kernels produced
by batching exhibit greater data reuse through tiling [19].
Together, these results demonstrate that batching improves
utilization primarily for the AE, while the backbone’s utiliza-
tion remains largely unchanged. Therefore, increasing overall
utilization cannot be achieved through batching alone.
B. Latency Analysis
Batching can increase latency when the unbatched workload
already saturates the GPU’s compute units [17]. Because VLA
response time is critical for safety and task completion [15],
serving providers must balance utilization and throughput
against latency. Yet, this trade-off has not been quantified for
each VLA stage. In Figure 6 we measure the per-stage latency
overhead of batching and make the following observations.
First, the AE batches with minimal latency overhead (1.15-
1.33× at batch size 4, H100). The action-stage kernels perform
relatively little computation and are memory-bound. Thus,
once model weights are loaded, generating an additional batch
of tokens adds little latency. The AE’s low TCU utilization
(Figure 4) means that large batches cause minimal extra
stalling on the math pipeline.
Second, the VLM and ViT stages benefit less from batching,
with much smaller throughput gains than the AE. In π0.5’s
VLM, >65% of time is spent on dense FNN GEMM kernels
(batch size 1), and >50% of cycles stall on the math pipeline.
Larger batches only deepen this compute bottleneck, raising
latency.
Batching therefore affects VLA stages unevenly. The ViT
and VLM process larger token counts, producing GEMM ker-
nels that grow with batch size and dominate end-to-end latency
(44-71% at batch size 4). In LLM serving, comparable prefill
costs are amortized over a long, variable-length decode stage
[17]. However, VLAs cannot do this since action generation
comes from a fixed-length AE. Furthermore, VLA input image
tokens change on every pass, preventing prefill computation
reuse via KV-cache optimizations. The VLM backbone is thus
recomputed often and cannot be hidden by typical techniques
used by LLM serving.
V. CO-EXECUTION
GPU co-execution exploits the fact that a single application
rarely saturates all of a GPU’s heterogeneous resources (e.g.,
SMs, memory bandwidth, caches [9], [10]). Co-executing
memory-bound and compute-bound applications has proven to
be effective in other domains [20], since their bottlenecks fall
on different hardware, thus minimizing interference. Given the
AE’s under-utilized compute and the VLM stage’s higher com-
pute utilization (Section IV-A), we examine their co-execution
as a candidate for stage-wise pipeline-parallel serving [9].
Our experiments use standard co-execution methods: CUDA
streams and CUDA green contexts (GC). The latter is an
inter-SM partitioning method, which we tune for the highest
throughput. Table II reports the throughput gains from these
two methods.

From Table II we observe that the throughput of co-executed
stages only marginally improves over serial execution (1.02-
1.23×). Below, we detail VLA stage co-execution challenges.
First, profile traces show that the VLM’s large FFN and
Query-Key-Value (QKV) GEMM kernels are a common
source of action-stage stalls. These are the longest-running
VLM kernels, with grid sizes large enough to launch a thread
block per SM. Under multiple CUDA streams, a second kernel

can only co-execute once the earlier kernel has dispatched all
its thread blocks [20], so AE kernel dispatching is blocked.
Second, while inter-SM partitioning improves throughput
over CUDA streams, reduced compute units increase the
latency of certain key kernels. The VLM’s FFN and QKV
projection GEMM kernels increase significantly in latency (50-
100% for π0.5, 285-295% for GR00T) given that these kernels
are compute-intensive. Similarly, we observe that π0.5’s AE
sinusoidal time embedding kernels increase by over 225%
compared to their serial execution time, since these kernels
use FP64 arithmetic. Additionally, the AE’s GEMM kernels
increase in latency (16-108%) due to DRAM contention be-
tween the two stages (DRAM utilization increases by 33%).
Third, action kernels are short-lived (∼6µs), producing
unpredictable overlap with long VLM kernels. Other co-
execution methods therefore have relatively high overhead
(40µs for preemption-based methods [21]) or require re-
implementing proprietary kernels. Finer-grained temporal SM
repartitioning incurs high synchronization cost [9].
Thus, despite their contrasting resource needs, standard
methods fail to co-execute VLA stages efficiently. Under
CUDA streams, the VLM’s FFN and QKV kernels occupy
most SMs and block AE kernel scheduling. Under SM parti-
tioning, reduced SMs increase the latency of these compute-
bound GEMM kernels in the VLM, and cause DRAM con-
tention. Improving co-execution warrants more investigation.
VI. CONCLUSION
We detail how fundamental serving techniques, batching
and co-execution, affect key multi-client serving trade-offs for
VLAs: latency, throughput, hardware utilization, and power.
We find that batching alone cannot increase hardware utiliza-
tion across all stages, as the VLM backbone is dominated by
compute-bound GEMM kernels. We find that VLAs, unlike
LLMs, cannot amortize the latency that batching the VLM and
ViT stages introduces. Stage co-execution offers only modest
gains since VLM GEMM kernels either block AE kernels or
slow under SM partitioning. Short-lived AE kernels impede
optimal co-execution. Our takeaways inform the trade-offs and
challenges that multi-VLA serving systems must address.
