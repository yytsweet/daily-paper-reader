## ABSTRACT 

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) are served with accelerated decoders that cache context and commit, in one step, every masked position whose con-fidence clears a threshold. Yet post-training quantization of dLLMs has so far been calibrated and evaluated under fixed-step samplers without caching. We study W4A4 quantization under the deployed decoder and find that the decoder determines both where quantization harms accuracy and what it costs. Irreversible errors enter chiefly through forced commitments, made when no position clears the threshold: they are a minority of commitments but carry most quantization-induced flips. Quantization also levies a parallelism tax , extra forward passes per response: by compressing top logit gaps, it pushes positions below the threshold while leav-ing predictions largely intact. We propose D ECAL , which reweights layer-wise calibration by a commitment-risk prior measured on the deployed decoder, and an argmax-preserving commit temperature that recovers the tax. On LLaDA and Dream, D ECAL attains the best average accuracy among W4A4 methods, with lower run-to-run variance than the closest existing calibration method. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) generate text by iteratively unmasking a response with bidirectional context (Austin et al., 2021a; Sahoo et al., 2024), and open models such as LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025) and Dream (Ye et al., 2025) now rival autoregressive (AR) models of similar scale. Their speed in practice comes from accelerated decoders: Fast-dLLM (Wu et al., 2026b) combines block-wise approximate KV caching with confidence-thresholded parallel decoding, committing in one step every masked position whose confidence exceeds a threshold. Serving cost thus factors into the number of forward passes and the cost of each. Caching and parallel decoding reduce the former; low-bit post-training quantization (PTQ) (Frantar et al., 2023), for instance with rotations (QuaRot; Ashkboos et al., 2024) or learned affine transformations (FlatQuant; Sun et al., 2025), reduces the latter. Ideally, the two savings would compound. Existing dLLM quantization methods (Lin et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026), however, are calibrated and evaluated apart from this decoder, under the model’s native sampler, which commits a fixed number of positions per step without caching. The closest, FAIR-Calib (Huang et al., 2026), reweights calibration by a position prior from a deliberately policy-agnostic probe; in our reproduction under Fast-dLLM, this prior is nearly uniform on LLaDA and does not follow where the decoder forces commitments (Figure 1a). Yet under threshold decoding, the model’s confidence, passed through the decoding rule, determines both how many forward passes a response takes and which decisions become irreversible. Observation 1: irreversible errors enter through forced commitments. A token is written either because its confidence exceeds the threshold ( threshold commitment ) or because no position in the active block qualifies and the decoder must still write the most confident one ( forced commitment ); either way, it is never revised. Forced commitments make up only 16–29% of commitments yet carry 89–97% of the permanent flips , commitments that quantization changes and that survive into the returned output (Figure 1b, Section 3.2). 

Observation 2: quantization levies a parallelism tax. Quantization deflates confidence while largely preserving what the model would write, so fewer positions clear the threshold at each step and each response takes more forward passes. We call this extra cost the parallelism tax ; it reaches 13% on LLaDA and, as we show, can be recovered without retraining (Figure 1c, Section 3.3). We therefore align both calibration and decoding with the deployed decoder. D ECAL (DE coder-aligned Commitment-risk c AL ibration) weights layer-wise calibration by a commitment-risk prior estimated from full-precision trajectories of the deployed decoder. A commit temperature restores the confidence scale at the threshold test; since it preserves the argmax, it changes when positions are committed, never which token a position receives in a given state. The two act on different decisions: DECAL makes the fragile forced commitments less likely to flip, whereas the temperature releases only commitments just below the threshold, which rarely flip (Section H). Our contributions are as follows: • To our knowledge, we are the first to systematically analyze PTQ for dLLMs under the cached, threshold-based parallel decoder used in deployment, identifying forced commitments, including a placement failure absent from AR decoding (writing the wrong position), as the entry point of irreversible errors, and characterizing the parallelism tax that quantization levies. • We propose D ECAL , which scores each generation position by its flip probability, through the written token or the chosen position, times its reach into the returned output. Collected without any cache, the prior transfers to cached deployment, and its definition applies to 2Preprint both instruction-tuned and base models. D ECAL attains the best average accuracy among W4A4 methods on both models, with and without cache, and, under cached decoding, a lower run-to-run variance than FAIR-Calib on every task. • We introduce an argmax-preserving commit temperature, set by matching full-precision forward passes, that recovers the full tax of QuaRot on LLaDA without a detectable accuracy change and offers a controllable accuracy–efficiency trade-off beyond it. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Diffusion language models. Masked diffusion models, typically bidirectional Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017), generate text by iteratively denoising a fully masked sequence (Austin et al., 2021a; Lou et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024). LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025) and Dream (Ye et al., 2025) scale them to billions of parameters. Because vanilla sampling recomputes full bidirectional attention at every step, a line of work accelerates dLLM inference with approximate KV caching (Ma et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026) and adaptive parallel decoding (Israel et al., 2025); Fast-dLLM (Wu et al., 2026b) combines caching with threshold decoding and is the decoder we study. Polestar (Lee et al., 2026) uses token representation drift both to refresh stale cache entries and to commit tokens below the confidence threshold when their drift shows a sharp event; under its drift-guided commitment, forced commitments still carry most quantization-induced flips, the parallelism tax persists, and both the prior of D ECAL and the commit temperature keep their benefits (Section J). 

Post-training quantization of LLMs. Reconstruction-based PTQ fits quantization parameters layer by layer or block by block on a small calibration set (Nagel et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021). For LLMs, weight-only methods use second-order updates (Frantar et al., 2023) or protect salient channels using activation statistics (Lin et al., 2024), while weight–activation methods must handle activation outliers (Dettmers et al., 2022), by smoothing (Xiao et al., 2023), rotation (Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025), or learned transformations and clipping (Shao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025). 

Quantization of diffusion models and dLLMs. For image diffusion, calibration must cover the activations of all timesteps (Shang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). For dLLMs, Lin et al. (2025) show that AR PTQ methods transfer but W4A4 remains hard because of activation outliers. DLLMQuant (Xu et al., 2026) and Quant-dLLM (Zhang et al., 2026) calibrate across timesteps and mask ratios, STaR-Quant (Yan et al., 2026) treats masked and unmasked token states separately, and FAIR-Calib (Huang et al., 2026) weights calibration positions by a prior probed from a full-precision teacher that commits one random position per step, with neither blocks nor a threshold. None is calibrated or evaluated under the cached threshold decoder used in deployment. 

Temperature and confidence. Temperature scaling calibrates predicted probabilities post hoc (Guo et al., 2017; Minderer et al., 2021); we use it to restore the scale the commit threshold reads.

We measure W4A4 LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-v0-Base-7B on GSM8K with Fast-dLLM (dual cache, τ = 0 .9). 3.2 OBSERVATION 1: I RREVERSIBLE ERRORS ENTER THROUGH FORCED COMMITMENTS 

The threshold filters decisions by margin. A threshold commitment is made only when the top token leads its runner-up by a sizable logit margin, which a quantization perturbation can overturn only if it exceeds that margin: the threshold admits only robust decisions. The forced rule suspends this filter exactly when the model is uncertain, writing the most confident position at whatever margin it has; since nothing is revised, forced commitments are the channel through which fragile decisions become permanent. Accordingly, the flip rate falls steeply with the confidence at commitment and is up to three orders of magnitude higher below τ , a range only forced commitments reach (Figure 2a). With FlatQuant, forced commitments are 16.5% of all commitments on LLaDA and 29.2% on Dream, yet carry 97.1% and 88.8% of the permanent token flips (Figure 1b; 99.5% and 96.7% counting placement flips, Section G): per commitment, 6.0% and 9.8% of them flip, against 0.04% and 0.13% of threshold commitments. 

How forced commitments fail, and why only here. A forced commitment can flip its token, as in AR decoding, but also its position: when the most confident positions are nearly tied, quantization can change which one is written, a placement flip that AR decoding, with its fixed order, cannot make. Either error is permanent and becomes context for every later pass, so its damage grows with how long it is read before the returned output is complete. By contrast, a fixed-step sampler commits its k most confident positions whatever their margins, spreading fragile commitments over the whole schedule, and a random-commit probe, as in FAIR-Calib, does not see them at all (Figure 1a). Under threshold decoding, they are concentrated, can be located on full-precision trajectories before quantization, and differ in how far their errors reach; the prior of Section 4.1 measures both. 3.3 OBSERVATION 2: Q UANTIZATION LEVIES A PARALLELISM TAX

Anything that shrinks these gaps without changing the top token delays commitments while leaving every decision intact, and quantization does exactly this. It adds nearly independent noise to the logits, which barely changes their spread but pulls the top logit toward the rest and lifts the strongest of the many competitors, so the gaps near the peak shrink most: on LLaDA with QuaRot, the top-2 gap shrinks by 26% while the standard deviation of the logits is unchanged (Section B), and across models and quantizers the shrinkage reaches 30% at confident positions (Figure 2b). 

From compressed gaps to extra passes. Positions near τ then fall below it: fewer positions clear the threshold at each step, more steps end in a forced commitment, and each response needs more forward passes, up to 13% more on LLaDA with QuaRot (Figure 1c; both tasks in Section A). We call this cost the parallelism tax . It compounds Observation 1, since the deflation that slows decoding also routes more decisions through the forced channel. 

Deflation, not lost capability. The shadow of Section 3.1 shows that the tax is a matter of scale. The width loss it measures step by step matches an independent end-to-end measurement (13.4% against 14.6% on 400 questions; Section A), so the extra passes come from fewer commitments per step, not from diverging trajectories; and where the shadow would commit but the quantized model withholds, the two predict the same token in 97.9% of cases, against 68.3% over all masked positions. Quantization perturbs predictions broadly but, where decisions are made, only lowers confidence, so rescaling confidence recovers the tax without touching written tokens (Section 4.2).

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 SETUP 

Models, decoding, and tasks. We quantize LLaDA-8B-Instruct (Nie et al., 2025) and Dream-v0-Base-7B (Ye et al., 2025) and decode with Fast-dLLM (Wu et al., 2026b) using dual cache, 

τ = 0 .9, block length 32, and 256 generated tokens; we also report threshold decoding without cache. Following Fast-dLLM, we evaluate GSM8K (5-shot, flexible-extract exact match; Cobbe et al., 2021), HumanEval (0-shot, pass@1; Chen et al., 2021), MBPP (3-shot, pass@1; Austin et al., 2021b), and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with the 4-shot Minerva prompt (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), 200 problems per subject, and an answer-equivalence check. These match Fast-dLLM’s official evaluation; we report accuracy rather than perplexity, which scores teacher-forced text and never runs the decoder. 

Methods. All methods quantize weights and activations to 4 bits (W4A4). QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) represents rotation-based PTQ, and FlatQuant (Sun et al., 2025) uses its default calibration with uniform weights. FAIR-Calib (Huang et al., 2026) and D ECAL share FlatQuant’s reconstruction on 128 WikiText-2 sequences (Merity et al., 2017) of length Tc = 1 ,024 whose last K = 256 positions form the window, and differ only in the weights ω of (5): the probed prior for FAIR-Calib (reproduced in our pipeline) and the commitment-risk prior (4) for D ECAL , collected without cache from 5-shot GSM8K training prompts (about 640 per model; FAIR-Calib keeps its default 512, beyond which its own ablation shows no gain). This one-time cost is 2.8 GPU-hours on Dream (153 forward passes per prompt; LLaDA needs about half as many), fewer passes in total than FAIR-Calib’s 512 probes of 256 steps each. 5.3 ABLATION : C OMPONENTS OF THE PRIOR 

Table 3 isolates the two factors of (4). Every risk-aware prior improves on uniform weights, by 1.1–1.2 average points on LLaDA and 1.4–1.8 on Dream: where the calibration budget goes matters. The shuffled control, with the same weights at random positions, gains nothing on LLaDA and least on Dream, so the gain comes from where the weights are placed, not from non-uniform weighting as such. How the factors share it depends on the model and the task, as follows from what each measures: 

π marks locally fragile commitments, a commitments whose errors propagate through later reads. 

Task: local versus propagating errors. GSM8K solutions are short, so correctness hinges on a few decisive tokens. MATH solutions are long derivations in which an early error propagates into everything written after it. On Dream, the factors separate: π alone carries the GSM8K gain (+1.8 points, against none for a alone), whereas a alone gives the largest MATH gain (+2.8 against +1.7). 

Model: why the separation appears on Dream. An instruction-tuned model stops on its own, so its commitments, fragile or not, lie inside the returned answer: on LLaDA the factors largely agree, and either alone recovers most of the gain (+0.9 to +1.3 points). A base model writes past its answer, so part of its fragile commitments falls in a discarded continuation while reach concentrates on the answer: on Dream the factors single out different positions, and the task decides which matters. Table 3 does not separate the product from the stronger single factor, which lies within 0.1 average points of it on both models. ## 6 CONCLUSION 

We studied W4A4 quantization of dLLMs under the decoder they are served with. Under cached threshold decoding, quantization errors become irreversible chiefly through forced commitments, and quantization levies a parallelism tax by deflating confidence rather than changing predictions. D ECAL 

weights calibration by commitment risk, and a commit temperature calibrated on forward passes alone recovers the tax without retraining or labels. On LLaDA and Dream, D ECAL attains the best average accuracy among W4A4 methods with lower run-to-run variance than FAIR-Calib, and the temperature restores full-precision decoding cost. The prior is tied to the deployed block length and threshold, and we have studied one decoder family at one bit width; extending commitment-aware calibration to other decoders and lower bit widths is a natural next step. Table B.2 shows compression weakening monotonically as the scale widens: the gap between the peak and its strongest competitor shrinks to 0.74, while the slope over model-independent tokens is close to one and the standard deviation of the whole logit vector is unchanged. Quantization adds nearly independent noise that offsets the shrinkage in the bulk of the vocabulary, but thins exactly the few gaps on which the maximum softmax probability depends. 

Implication for the temperature. Dividing the logits by T scales every gap by 1/T , so a tempera-ture can undo this compression. Because the compression depends on scale and on the confidence stratum, no single number in these tables is the right temperature, and we calibrate it on the commit rate instead (Section 4.2).

## F PROOF OF PROPOSITION 1 AND THE PREMISE OF THE REACH 

Proof of Proposition 1. (i) The argmax is invariant to positive scaling, so dividing the logits by T > 0 leaves every written token unchanged. (ii) Replacing ∆v by ∆v /T in (6) shows that 

pT grows as T decreases, so the threshold set only grows, and a non-empty one commits at least as many positions as a forced commitment; with two candidates, p < τ ≤ pT is equivalent to 

T m τ ≤ ∆ < m τ . (iii) The softmax is shift-invariant, so zq = κz + b1 gives softmax( zq/κ ) = softmax( z + b1/κ ) = softmax( z).

Premise of the reach. The square root in (3) is the root-mean-square accumulation of approx-imately independent perturbations across the passes that read a committed token. We verified the independence premise: without the shared per-layer bias, cross-position error terms are 3.6% of the diagonal ones. The choice of the concave map matters little: log(1+ ·) in place of the square root gives a prior with correlation 0.9975.

## H HOW DECAL AND THE COMMIT TEMPERATURE DIVIDE THE WORK 

Quantization perturbs the logits, and the threshold sorts the affected decisions by margin. Fragile de-cisions with small margins are forced even at full precision and can flip, which costs accuracy (Obser-vation 1); D ECAL calibrates the model to perturb them less. ## I CHECKS OF THE FLIP -P ROBABILITY MODEL 

Both the risk model (2) and the reach (3) serve only to rank positions, since (4) keeps nothing but their order. We checked each part of (2) against the flips recorded for Figure 1b (W4A4 FlatQuant, first 400 GSM8K test questions; 102,400 commitments on LLaDA and 99,200 on Dream). 20 Preprint 

Smaller margins flip more often. Ranked by the full-precision margin from which π is computed, a commitment whose token flips is rated riskier than one whose token does not in 98.7% of such pairs on LLaDA and 93.9% on Dream (AUROC 0.987 and 0.939; chance is 0.5). 

The perturbation has no preferred direction. A zero-mean perturbation turns a near tie into a coin toss. Among commitments with a margin below 0.1, 47% flip on LLaDA and 51% on Dream. 

The Gaussian curve matches the observed flip rates. With its single scale fitted to the data, the Gaussian curve differs from the observed flip rate by 0.1 percentage points on average across margins on LLaDA and 0.8 on Dream. The default scale s = ˜ m, fixed before the quantizer is known, overestimates the absolute probabilities; this matters little, since (4) keeps only the order of positions (last check below). 

Treating token and placement flips as independent is a close approximation. Both depend on the same confidences and are slightly correlated (about 0.1), but they rarely occur together: the predicted share of forced commitments that fail in either way is 37.8% against 39.4% observed on LLaDA, and 39.8% against 39.2% on Dream. 

The prior does not depend on these modeling choices. Rebuilding the prior with the Gaussian curves replaced by flip rates measured directly from the data orders the 256 window positions almost exactly as ours (Spearman correlation 0.994 on LLaDA and 0.997 on Dream). 

The reach. Its square root rests on approximately independent perturbations across later reads, a premise verified in Section F, where a logarithm in its place also gives a nearly identical prior. Reach alone improves on uniform weights by 1.1 (LLaDA) and 1.4 (Dream) average points, and the shuffled control of Table 3 shows that such gains require the weights to sit at the right positions.
