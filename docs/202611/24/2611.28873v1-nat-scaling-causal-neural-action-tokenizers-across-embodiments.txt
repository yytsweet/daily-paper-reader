# NAT: Scaling Causal Neural Action Tokenizers Across Embodiments 

Ahad Jawaid, Alexander Swerdlow, and Yu Xiang 

Abstract —Action tokenizers let robot policies treat control as autoregressive next-token prediction. As these policies are trained on larger and more diverse datasets, they will need a universal tokenizer that can encode actions from many embodiments and data sources, including ones it has not seen. We study action tokenization at scale, training on 49k hours of action data from real-world robots, handheld grippers, simulation, and humans. We introduce Neural Action Tokenizer (NAT), a causal tokenizer whose decoder lets actions be executed as their tokens are gener-ated. Across four simulation benchmarks, NAT outperforms FAST and prior neural action tokenizers when tokenizing unseen robot embodiments, and in vision-language-action models it outperforms FAST+ across four benchmarks and on a real robot when acting through its tokens. NAT tokens also close the performance gap to a flow-matching action head on some benchmarks while lowering the inference latency for first actions. Our experiments also find that robot data may not be necessary to learn an effective action space: a NAT trained only on human hand motion performs close to tokenizers trained on robot data. 

Index Terms —Robot learning, action tokenization, autoregres-sive policies, vision-language-action models, robot manipulation 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large language models [1] have benefited from a simple scalable training recipe: convert text into a shared vocabulary of tokens, then train a single autoregressive model on a large cor-pus of tokens. A similar recipe may help scale robotic learning. Robot data, however, comes from many embodiments, often with different action spaces, control frequencies, and ranges of motion, and is collected in different ways: teleoperation on real robots, handheld grippers such as UMI [2], simulation, and recordings of human hands. A foundation robot policy that uses this recipe will likely need a tokenizer that can encode large and diverse sets of actions, ideally including those from embodiments it has not seen. The action tokenizer therefore plays an important role in training autoregressive robot policies, and its design can sub-stantially affect policy performance, training efficiency, and inference speed [3]. Early autoregressive policies discretized each action dimension into uniform bins [4], [5]. Binning can be applied to most robots, but it tends to produce long token sequences that slow down inference. FAST [3] compresses action chunks with a frequency-domain transform and byte pair encoding, yielding shorter sequences and faster training, and the released FAST+ [3] is trained on 1M robot trajectories such that it can work across embodiments. A growing line of work instead learns the tokenizer: a neural encoder, a quantizer [6], [7], and a decoder that reconstructs the action

These tokenizers can represent actions with fewer tokens than FAST [3], but most are trained on comparatively small datasets, so how they behave on the scale and diversity of data a foundation policy would require remains largely unexplored. In this work, we study action tokenization at scale. We assemble a corpus of 49k hours of action data from 156 datasets spanning real-world robots, universal manipulation interfaces (UMIs) [2], simulated robots, and human hand motion, and use it to train and evaluate action tokenizers. We also introduce the Neural Action Tokenizer (NAT) 1, a causal neural tokenizer that encodes action chunks into short token sequences using finite scalar quantization (FSQ) [7] at multiple temporal scales. Its causal decoder supports streaming: actions can be decoded as their tokens are generated, so the robot can begin executing while the policy is still predicting the rest of the chunk, and the policy can condition its next chunk on the actions still executing. We then pretrain vision-language-action models (VLAs) with NAT to test whether these results carry over to large pretrained policies, and compare acting through NAT tokens with acting through a flow-matching action head [13], [14]. > •

We show that NAT tokens can help pretrain vision-language-action models: further training a pretrained vision-language model on 1.8k hours of robot data through NAT tokens, before fine-tuning, raises mean success on four simulation benchmarks over fine-tuning the vision-language model directly.

A. Autoregressive Robot Policies 

Robot foundation models are commonly built by fine-tuning a pretrained vision-language model on robot data [14], [16], [17], [18]. Since these backbones are autoregressive transform-ers [19], actions can be tokenized and predicted with next-token prediction; early work showed that even simple binning transfers web-scale knowledge to robot control [4], [5]. Other models, such as π0 [14] and GR00T [17], instead decode actions with a flow-matching or diffusion head which showed improvements in inference latency. FAST [3] showed that mid-training on discrete action tokens improves training efficiency and policy performance, and later work adopts this for models with a continuous action expert [16], [20], [18]. 

B. Action Tokenizers 

Binning each action dimension [4], [5] is simple but produces long token sequences that can make real-time inference imprac-tical. FAST [3] uses handcrafted frequency-based compression to produce much shorter sequences, improving training effi-ciency and policy performance, and its variant FAST+ [3], fitted on 1M real robot action sequences, tokenizes new action spaces zero-shot. Learned tokenizers compress actions further with vector-quantized autoencoders [8], and have been extended to multiple embodiments [9], aligned with language [12], ordered so that partial token sequences can be decoded [10], and scaled to 2B action frames with vision-language alignment [21]. Most neural tokenizers have been trained on comparatively smaller datasets, so it remains largely unexplored how learned tokenizers behave when trained on large, heterogeneous action data spanning real robots, UMIs [2], simulation, and humans. 

C. Real-Time Execution 

Action chunking [22], [23] lets a policy predict several future actions per inference, but the robot must wait for each new chunk, and switching between independently sampled chunks can cause discontinuities. Many VLAs predict chunks with a diffusion or flow-matching action head [14], [17], which generates every action of a chunk in parallel over a fixed number of denoising steps; knowledge insulation [20] trains such a head beside discrete action tokens, and consistency distillation [24] reduces the number of denoising steps. Other work hides inference latency during execution: asynchronous inference [25] predicts the next chunk while the current one runs, bidirectional decoding [26] selects among sampled chunks for coherence with the previous one, and real-time chunking [27] inpaints the actions still executing into a flow policy’s sampling so that consecutive chunks join smoothly. Autoregressive policies, in contrast, usually decode a chunk only after all of its tokens are generated [3]. NAT’s time-ordered tokens and causal decoder give them both properties: actions are executed as their tokens are generated, and executing actions are fixed as a token prefix. III. P RELIMINARIES 

Action chunking. We consider policies that, given an obser-vation o (camera images, proprioception, and a task), predict a chunk of H future actions a1: H ∈ RH×D, where D is the action dimension. During execution, the robot executes the first s ≤ H actions of each chunk before querying the policy again. We refer to s as the execution horizon [27]. 

Action tokenization. Autoregressive policies that predict discrete tokens, the setting we study, require continuous action chunks to first be converted into a token sequence. A tokenizer 

T maps an action chunk to a sequence of L tokens, T (a1: H ) = 

c1: L with ci ∈ V , i = 1 , . . . , L , where V is the tokenizer’s vocabulary, and a detokenizer T −1 maps a token sequence back to an action chunk, T −1(c1: L) = ˆ a1: H . The policy only predicts in the token space, while the robot executes the continuous action reconstructed by T −1.Existing tokenizers differ in how they construct this mapping. 

Binning [4], [5] normalizes each action dimension to [−1, 1] 

and assigns each value to one of N uniform bins, giving one token per dimension per timestep ( L = HD ). FAST [3] applies the discrete cosine transform [28] to each action dimension, rounds the resulting coefficients, and compresses them with byte-pair encoding [29]. Neural tokenizers [8], [9], [10], [11], [12] instead learn the mapping: an encoder compresses the chunk into a short sequence of latent vectors, a quantizer [6], [7] maps each latent to an entry of a discrete codebook, and a decoder reconstructs the chunk. 

Streaming. For most tokenizers, the detokenizer T −1 needs the complete token sequence before it can decode any action, so the robot must wait for the policy to generate all L tokens. We call a tokenizer streaming if every prefix of the token sequence already determines a prefix of the action chunk, and those actions do not change as further tokens are generated. With a streaming tokenizer, the robot can begin executing a chunk while the policy is still generating it. IV. N EURAL ACTION TOKENIZER 

We introduce the Neural Action Tokenizer (NAT), a neural tokenizer whose decoder enables streaming. 

A. Architecture 

NAT builds on the Neural Action Codec (NAC) [11], an action tokenizer adapted from neural audio codecs [30], [31]. We start from the variant of NAC trained without adversarial losses and build on it with three changes, each guided by an ablation (Section V-D). First, we replace NAC’s multi-scale residual vector quantization (RVQ) with a multi-scale variant of finite scalar quantization (FSQ) [7], which substantially improved downstream policy performance. Second, we replace NAC’s inverse short-time Fourier transform (ISTFT) output head with a linear layer, which performed comparably with a simpler design. Third, we make the encoder and decoder causal, which is what enables streaming. This caused only a small change in policy performance relative to a non-causal NAT. V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

Action corpus. The corpus has 49k hours (5.5B action frames) from 156 datasets: real-robot teleoperation, simulation, handheld grippers (UMI), and human hands (Figure 4). Each dataset is normalized per dimension to [−1, 1] ; chunks span 

H = 32 steps in groups of 7 dimensions (Appendix A). 

Zero-shot corpus. The corpus excludes the evaluation robots (Franka Panda and Sawyer), so the tokenizers are not trained on them, except in Tables IV and XIII. Unless stated otherwise, tokenizers are trained on a fixed 1% subset. 

Tokenizers. We compare NAT with FAST [3], OAT [10], ActionCodec [12], and NAC [11]. All use the same data, a 51,200-token vocabulary, and 28 tokens per chunk (FAST aver-ages 29.8 tokens); the neural tokenizers have 63M parameters. 

Policies. Each frozen tokenizer drives the same autore-gressive policy (Section IV-C; 162M or 280M parameters, Table XI), which predicts 32-step chunks and executes 16. A diffusion policy [23] with the same backbone serves as a non-autoregressive reference (Appendix B).

D. Which Architectural Choices Matter? 

Table IV ablates NAT’s changes to NAC [11] and the number of scales. Here and in Table XIII only, the 1% subset comes from an earlier corpus that includes the evaluation robots. 

Scales and quantization. Success rises from 12.9% with one scale to 75.8% with four, with no gain from eight. Replacing FSQ with NAC’s RVQ costs 8.8 points. We suspect two causes, not isolated here: RVQ’s learned codebooks are prone to collapse, leaving codes unused [7]; and FSQ’s fixed grid makes codes that share most channel levels decode to similar latents, so a policy’s confusions between them cost less, while RVQ’s codes have no such structure. 

Output head and causality. The ISTFT and linear heads perform similarly (75.6% vs. 75.8%), so we use the linear head. A non-causal NAT scores 1.7 points higher, within the 95% intervals. Appendix D gives further sweeps.

F. How Much Does a Flow Action Head Help? 

Many VLAs add a flow-matching action head with the aim of reducing latency and discretization error, so we evaluate the per-formance of one on top of our VLA. Following the MolmoAct2 recipe [18], we add a flow-matching action head [13] trained with knowledge insulation [20]: the head attends to the language model’s activations, but its gradients do not reach the language model, so the backbone keeps training on action tokens. Each pretrained model is first mid-trained with the head and then fine-tuned with tokens and head together (Appendix F). 

Latency. FAST+ uses 6 to 73 tokens per chunk (38.8 on average) and decodes only after the whole chunk. NAT uses 14, and its first 4 decode to 8 actions, which take 267 ms to execute at 30 Hz; the rest of the chunk, and the next one (Section IV-C), are generated while the robot moves. Table VI times this on NAT VLA (50 samples). It also times the same decoding steps on the language models of larger VLAs, as a latency analysis only. NAT VLA has its first actions after 19.0 ms, 5.1 × sooner than FAST+ VLA and 2.2 × sooner than its own flow expert. On these 3–5B models, generating NAT’s first 4 tokens would take 45–101 ms, comparable to or faster than their action heads, and a full chunk 85–226 ms, under the 267 ms the first actions take to execute. 

Success and cost. We fine-tune each backbone with the flow head on the four benchmarks of Table V. These runs add the mid-training stage and use MolmoAct2’s post-training learning rates, so they differ from the token runs in more than the action head. Through the expert, NAT VLA reaches 73.6% mean success and FAST+ VLA 71.4% (Table V); the intervals overlap except on RoboMimic. The expert raises NAT’s mean by 12.2 points, mostly on Meta-World and RoboCasa, and FAST+’s by 34.0. On LIBERO, NAT tokens and the expert are within the 95% intervals (90.8% vs. 89.2%). The expert adds 77.7M parameters, a mid-training stage, and a denoising process at inference, while NAT’s first actions arrive sooner than a chunk from our flow head (19.0 vs. 42.0 ms) and, in our latency analysis, than the heads of GR00T N1.7 and MolmoAct2 (Table VI), and its tokens keep the streaming and real-time chunking of Section IV-C. VI. D ISCUSSION 

Our results support four takeaways. First, a single neural action tokenizer can serve robots it has never seen, outper-forming FAST and prior neural tokenizers without training on the evaluation robots. Second, an action tokenizer may not need robot data: human hand motion alone yields a tokenizer close to one trained on all sources, so large-scale human data could be used to train action tokenizers. Third, NAT tokens are an effective action representation for vision-language-action models, both for robot pretraining and for acting, where they outperform FAST+. Fourth, action tokens can be faster than flow action heads, and on some domains acting through tokens alone closes the performance gap to a flow head, though a gap remains on others. 

Limitations and future work. Acting through NAT tokens still trails a flow-matching head on some benchmarks; further tuning and design choices, such as the token budget, decoding, 8

and fine-tuning recipe, may close this gap fully. NAT encodes every chunk in a fixed number of tokens; a future study could set the number of tokens per chunk dynamically. NAT is also trained on a single chunk length, so tokenizing chunks of varying length remains to be explored. Our data-scaling experiment scales the tokenizer’s training data while keeping the policy’s LIBERO data fixed; a future study could explore the benefits of scaling the tokenizer corpus together with the policy’s training corpus.
