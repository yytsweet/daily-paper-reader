Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demon-
strated strong capabilities in a wide range of robotic tasks.
However, adversarial attacks in VLA models have shown
strong transferability across robotic tasks, posing signifi-
cant threats to robotic systems. Despite their different attack
strategies, we observe that these transferable attacks share
a common consequence: they disrupt the VLA model’s per-
ception towards the robot itself.
Motivated by this ob-
servation, we propose a proprioception-grounded defense
framework that leverages trusted proprioceptive informa-
tion to detect and mitigate transferable adversarial attacks.
Specifically, we utilize the robot’s proprioceptive states to
establish a trusted spatial reference, and compare it with
the robot’s perceived location inferred from visual features.
Discrepancies between these two sources provide a reliable
signal for detecting adversarial attacks. Extensive exper-
iments demonstrate effective detection of transferable ad-
versarial patches and improved closed-loop performance.
Real-robot experiments further demonstrate the effective-
ness of our defense on real-world robotic tasks.
1. Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1, 9, 11] leverage
pretrained vision-language models to learn general-purpose
robot policies, and have demonstrated strong capabilities in
a wide range of robotic tasks. These models rely on cam-
era observations to perceive their surroundings and extract
visual features to guide action generation. However, prior
studies have shown that vision models are usually vulnera-
ble to adversarial perturbations [2, 8, 34]. Such vulnerabil-
ities pose significant safety risks to VLA models, as adver-
sarial perturbations can corrupt visual representations and
consequently lead to incorrect robot actions.
More dangerously, recent studies have demonstrated that
many adversarial attacks against VLA models can trans-

trated in Figure 1 (a), an adversarial patch can simultane-

are unknown to the attacker. In practice, attackers can de-
velop such transferable patches using publicly available pre-
trained VLA checkpoints and datasets. If a VLA model is
fine-tuned from one of these checkpoints, it is also likely to
be vulnerable to the same attack, even if the attacker has
no access to its fine-tuned parameters or private training
data. Thus, this transferability could pose significant safety
threats to real-world VLA deployment, as attackers can tar-
get a wide range of downstream tasks without developing
task-specific attacks.
To better understand why adversarial attacks can trans-
fer across different robotic tasks, we first conduct a visual-
ization analysis to examine the VLA model’s visual rep-
resentations under attacks. Specifically, we analyze rep-
resentative transferable attacks in Section 2.2 and identify
two common mechanisms. (1) Some attacks broadly dis-
rupt visual features, including the object and robot-related
features to mislead the model’s perception across different
scenes and tasks [7]. This mechanism is similar to uni-
versal adversarial attacks observed in general vision mod-
els [10, 32, 33].
(2) Other attacks target the perception
towards the robot itself [16, 26, 28]. Through explicitly
or implicitly mimicking visual features associated with the
robot, these attacks mislead the model’s perception of the
robot. Since accurate perception of the robot is important
across different tasks, such attacks can also achieve cross-
task transferability.
Despite their different mechanisms, we found that both
types of attacks share a common consequence: they dis-
tort the visual representation of the robot itself. This
observation reveals an important opportunity for defense:
While adversarial patches can mislead a VLA model’s vi-
sual perception of the robot, they cannot alter the robot’s
actual physical state. In fact, robots continuously measure
their own states through proprioceptive sensors, which pro-
vide information such as joint positions and orientations in-
dependently of camera observations. We can leverage this

Therefore, we propose a proprioception-grounded de-
fense framework that detects adversarial attacks by check-
ing the consistency between proprioceptive information and
visual representations. First, we use the robot’s proprio-
ceptive state, together with its kinematic model and cam-
era calibration, to determine where the robot should appear
in the camera scene. We then analyze the VLA model’s
visual features to estimate where the model perceives the
robot. As shown in Figure 1 (b), these two estimates are
consistent under benign conditions but diverge when attacks
distort the robot’s visual representation. By measuring this
discrepancy, our framework can detect adversarial attacks
without requiring any additional training or expensive in-
ference. Upon detection, our framework also leverages this
discrepancy to locate and resist the adversarial attacks.
We conduct extensive experiments on OpenVLA [11]
and π0 [1] across LIBERO [13] task suites and various ad-
versarial attacks. Results demonstrate that our framework
can accurately detect various transferable attacks, outper-
forming existing detection baselines. Beyond detection, our
framework effectively mitigates adversarial patches and sig-
nificantly improves task success under attack. To further
examine the robustness of our detection, we conduct two
adaptive attacks by preserving robot-related visual features
and minimizing the visual-proprio inconsistency score. We
find that both strategies reduce attack effectiveness, sup-
porting the reliability of the defense design. Real-robot ex-
periments further demonstrate the effectiveness of our de-
fense on real-world robotic tasks.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify a shared mechanism of transferable adver-
sarial attacks in VLA: these attacks compromise robot-
related visual representations. This observation motivates
leveraging trusted robot proprioception to verify the reli-
ability of visual perception.
• We develop a principled, lightweight, and attack-agnostic
defense framework that leverages visual-proprioceptive
consistency checking to detect, localize, and mitigate ad-
versarial attacks. Our approach requires no adversarial
examples or additional model training and can be inte-
grated into existing VLA models without modifying their
parameters. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first
work to leverage robot proprioceptive information for
adversarial defense in VLA models.
• Extensive experiments across two VLA models and a
broad range of attacks validate the effectiveness and gen-
eralizability of our defense. Evaluations under benign vi-
sual variations and adaptive attacks, together with real-
robot experiments, further validate its reliability.

2.2. Threat model and attack mechanism analysis
Threat Model. We focus on adversarial patch attacks [7,
16, 26, 28] that can transfer across robotic tasks. As illus-
trated in Figure 1 (a), a single adversarial patch can compro-
mise multiple robotic tasks, even when these tasks are un-
known to the attacker. Such attacks do not require access to
target-task data or the victim model’s fine-tuned parameters.
Instead, the attacker optimizes an adversarial patch using a
publicly accessible VLA model and source-task data D col-
lected from diverse robotic tasks. The same patch can then
be directly transferred to victim models fine-tuned from the
public VLA checkpoint on unseen downstream tasks. Such
transferability poses a significant threat to real-world VLA
deployment, as a single adversarial patch can compromise
a wide range of robotic applications.
There are several strategies to generate these patches. As
a representative example, the work [26] introduces the Un-
targeted Action Discrepancy Attack (UADA), which aims
to mislead the VLA model into generating incorrect robot
actions. Specifically, UADA optimizes an adversarial patch
to maximize the deviation between the predicted action
aadv and the correct action a:

3. Methodology
Analysis in Section 2.2 reveals that transferable adversar-
ial patches disrupt or spoof robot-related visual representa-
tions. This inspires us to introduce Visual-Proprio Con-
sistency (VPC) Detection to identify adversarial attacks by
measuring the discrepancy between the robot’s visual per-
ception and its proprioceptive state. Upon detection, we
further propose a gradient-based localization and mitigation
strategy to identify and suppress suspicious visual regions.

3.2. Visual-Proprioceptive Consistency Detection
We next aim to determine where the VLA model perceives
the robot in the image. To this end, we examine the visual
features extracted by the VLA’s vision encoder to determine
which image tokens exhibit robot-related features
Robot Feature Distribution. To determine whether a
visual token represents the robot, a straightforward way is to
measure its similarity to a reference robot feature. However,
the robot’s visual features can vary across different config-
urations, camera viewpoints, and visual conditions. A sin-
gle reference feature may not adequately represent the robot
across different observations. Therefore, we model robot-
related visual features using a Gaussian distribution. Specif-
ically, given a set of clean observations from the training
data, we extract visual features using the VLA model’s vi-
sion encoder. For each observation, we collect robot-related
features from tokens with mt,k = 1, according to Eq. 3.
We then compute their mean µR and covariance ΣR, which
characterize the typical robot features and their variations.
We refer to the resulting Gaussian distribution N(µR, ΣR)
as the robot feature distribution (R-distribution).
During model inference, we need to locate the robot
from visual features. To achieve our goal, we aim to deter-
mine whether a visual token is associated with the robot by
measuring its compatibility with the R-distribution. Specif-
ically, we use the squared Mahalanobis distance to measure
how well each token’s visual feature fits the R-distribution:
DR(zt,k) = (zt,k −µR)⊤Σ−1

Distinguishing Robot from Background. However, the
semantic information captured by visual features can differ
substantially across vision encoders and feature layers. For
example, features from deeper layers may become increas-
ingly similar across various locations, making it difficult to
distinguish robot tokens using Eq. 4 alone. To reduce the
dependence on specific encoders and layers, we introduce
a background feature distribution (B-distribution) as an ad-
ditional reference. By contrasting each token’s distance to
the two distributions, we adopt a discriminative matching
strategy that evaluates its relative compatibility with robot
and background features.
We construct the background

3.3. Consistency-Guided Patch Localization and
Mitigation
Once an adversarial attack is detected, our goal is to sup-
press the patch’s influence while preserving task-relevant
visual information. To achieve this, we first identify sus-
picious image regions and then selectively suppress their
influence through attention masking. Specifically, we lever-
age the differentiability of DPV and use gradient-based at-
tribution to identify regions that contribute most to the in-
consistency. To reduce the noise from the gradients and
obtain a more accurate estimation, we adopt a Smooth-
Grad [22] based method, which averages gradients over
multiple noisy perturbations of the input. Given an input
image It, we compute the attribution map as

4. Experiments
We evaluate our defense across different VLA architectures,
manipulation tasks, and adversarial attacks. We first com-
pare VPC with existing vision-only defenses to highlight
the benefits of incorporating robot proprioception. We then
assess its ability to restore task performance through closed-
loop evaluations in simulation and the real world. Finally,
we evaluate robustness under natural visual variations and
adaptive attacks.
Models and benchmarks.
We evaluate our method
on two representative open-source VLA models, Open-
VLA [11] and π0 [1], primarily in the LIBERO [13] sim-
ulation environment across four task suites: Spatial, Object,
Goal, and Long. We further validate our defense on a real-
world robotic platform equipped with a WidowX AI robotic
arm and an Intel RealSense D455f camera (in Section 4.3).
Considered Attacks. We evaluate three adversarial patch
attacks, VLA-Hijack [7], UPA-RFAS [16], and EDPA [28],
on both models. We additionally evaluate four patch vari-
ants: UADA, UMA, UPA, TMA from the work [26] on
OpenVLA. These attacks are designed for OpenVLA’s dis-
cretized action predictions and cannot be directly applied
to π0, which generates continuous action chunks through
flow matching. In our study, we optimize these attacks only
in LIBERO-Spatial task suite. As shown in Table 1, they
could also transfer to other task suites to cause task success
rate drop.2 Beyond these adversarial patches, we introduce
a phantom-robot attack that adds an additional robotic arm

Baseline Defenses. We select five representative vision-
only defenses that rely solely on visual information, al-
lowing us to evaluate the benefits of incorporating robot
proprioception. Deep kNN [23] detects out-of-distribution
inputs using feature-space distances, while Feature Squeez-
ing [29] identifies adversarial examples through prediction
inconsistencies under input transformations. ARMOR [14]
exploits anomalous attention patterns in ViTs to locate and
mask adversarial patches. Jedi [24] and LGS [19] iden-
tify suspicious regions using image entropy and local gradi-
ents, respectively, and mitigate their influence through im-
age restoration or smoothing.
Results.
For each LIBERO task suite, we evaluate 280
clean observations and 280 adversarial observations for
each attack (40 episodes in each suite and 7 frames in each
episode). We use AUROC to assess the ability to distinguish
clean from attacked observations. Table 2 reports the mean
and minimum AUROC across the four task suites for each
attack, reflecting overall detection performance and per-
formance in the most challenging suite, respectively. Our
method achieves the highest mean and minimum AUROC
on OpenVLA, while remaining highly effective on π0.
For the baselines, Deep kNN achieves high AUROC
on π0 under adversarial patch attacks, but its performance
drops substantially on OpenVLA. As shown in Figure 4,
patches optimized for π0 often exhibit visually irregular pat-
terns, potentially producing distinctive out-of-distribution
features that Deep kNN can readily detect.
In contrast,
patches targeting OpenVLA appear smoother and more vi-
sually natural, making them potentially harder to distin-
guish from benign features. This limitation becomes more
evident under the phantom-robot attack, which introduces
an additional robotic arm with a natural appearance into
the observation. Similarly, Jedi, LGS, and ARMOR rely
on specific visual cues, such as anomalous local entropy,
gradients, or attention concentration, which may not consis-
tently appear across different attacks. Unlike these vision-
only defenses, VPC detects inconsistencies between visual
representations and trusted robot proprioception, making it
less dependent on attack-specific visual characteristics.

4.2. Closed-Loop Defense Performance
We next evaluate whether detection-guided intervention im-
proves closed-loop task execution under attack. Using the
LIBERO benchmark, we evaluate 100 episodes per suite
with adversarial attacks detected by our VPC detector. Ta-
ble 3 shows the closed-loop task success rate under vari-
ous attacks with and without our defense method to show
the defense’s effectiveness. We also report the performance
for clean observations to validate that our method does
not undermine the performance for benign observations.
Results show that our method substantially improves task

4.3. Real-World Experiments
To further validate the practical effectiveness of our defense,
we deploy our framework with OpenVLA on a real-world
robotic platform consisting of a WidowX AI robotic arm
and an Intel RealSense D455f camera.
The robot is in-

bowl”. Under clean conditions, the robot succeeds in 19 out
of 20 trials. We then introduce a representative UPA [26] at-
tack, as illustrated in Figure 5. Under this attack, the robot
succeeds in only 5 out of 20 trials without defense. With
our defense enabled, all 20 attacked trials are successfully
detected, and our defense enables the robot to successfully
complete 16 of them. These results demonstrate the effec-
tiveness of our defense in mitigating adversarial patches and
restoring task performance in real-world scenarios.

5. Related Work
Adversarial Attacks and Defenses on VLA models.
Re-
cent studies have demonstrated the vulnerability of VLA
models to adversarial attacks. RobotAttack [26] introduces
adversarial patches that manipulate predicted actions and
disrupt robotic trajectories. Moving beyond action-space
attacks, EDPA [28] and UPA-RFAS [16] disrupt visual rep-
resentations and cross-modal alignment to achieve effective
and transferable attacks. VLA-Hijack [7] further exploits
the visual self-representation of robots to improve transfer-
ability. Beyond 2D patches, Tex3D [3] optimizes adversar-
ial textures on 3D object surfaces to disrupt a robot across
varying viewpoints, Universal Adversarial Object [30] in-
troduces a textured physical object to disrupt robotic con-
trol, and Phantom Menace [18] investigates physical sensor
attacks, including light projection and laser interference.
Compared with adversarial attacks, defenses for VLA
models remain relatively underexplored. SARF [35] im-
proves robustness by fine-tuning the visual encoder to rec-
tify adversarially disrupted attention.
However, its re-
liance on robust fine-tuning with adversarial inputs and
model-specific attention mechanisms may limit its adapt-
ability to unseen attacks and different VLA architectures.
CertVLA [15] provides certified robustness through re-
peated masking and action consistency verification, but in-
curs substantial computational overhead due to multiple
VLA inference passes. In contrast, our method is based
on visual-proprio consistency, which provides a lightweight
model-agnostic defense framework.
This enables our
framework to generalize well without additional training.
Proprioception in VLA Models.
Recent studies have ex-
plored proprioception as a source of physical priors to en-
hance visual understanding in VLA models. One line of re-
search focuses on grounding visual representations in robot
states. ROSA [27] leverages robot state estimation to im-
prove vision-action alignment and data efficiency, while
GeoProp [36] and GeoAlign [4] incorporate proprioceptive
information to enhance spatial grounding. Another line of
research focuses on improving inference efficiency by lever-
aging proprioception to guide visual information process-
ing. ThinkProprio [25] uses robot states to select action-
relevant visual tokens, whereas SQAP-VLA [5] preserves
robot-related tokens during model compression.
Mean-
while, ReViP [12] and GAP [17] investigate modality im-
balance between vision and proprioception to improve pol-
icy learning and generalization.
These studies primarily
leverage proprioception to improve policy performance or
computational efficiency, while our work is the first one to
explore its potential for adversarial defense.
6. Conclusion and Limitation
In this paper, we investigate the mechanisms of transferable
adversarial attacks for VLAs. Based on this analysis, we
build an attack-agnostic defense framework that leverages
proprioception as a trusted reference to detect inconsisten-
cies in visual robot localization.
Our framework primarily targets transferable adversar-
ial patches that disrupt robot-related visual representations
and cannot defend against all possible attacks.
For ex-
ample, task-specific attacks may manipulate object percep-
tion without affecting the robot’s visual representation. De-


fending against such attacks remains challenging even in
conventional computer vision [20, 37].
However, task-
specific attacks may require detailed knowledge of target
tasks and objects, potentially limiting their transferabil-
ity across different tasks. We hence focus on transferable
attacks as an initial step toward more robust VLA mod-
els.

D.1. Threshold Calibration
For practical deployment, a detection threshold is required
to determine whether an observation is under attack. To
maintain the attack-agnostic nature of our method, we se-
lect this threshold without using any adversarial attack
data. Moreover, to improve system robustness, we aim to
minimize false alarms caused by benign visual perturba-
tions. Specifically, we augment clean training images with
randomly generated noise patches and select a threshold
that keeps the false-positive rate on these non-adversarial
patches below 5%. Using this fixed threshold, we report the
true-positive rate (TPR) on adversarial observations and the

D.4. Camera Shift Detection
Beyond adversarial defense, our visual-proprio consistency
principle can be extended to detect other anomalies in
robotic systems, such as unexpected camera displacement,
temporal misalignment caused by camera latency. For VLA
deployment, it is very common for the camera viewpoint
to be changed due to accidents such as collisions, and this
anomaly may affect the performance of VLA models. This
camera shifts cause input image shifts while the calibra-
tion used for mask generation remains unchanged. There-
fore, there will be an inconsistency between the visual loca-
tion and proprioceptive state, which can be detected by our
method. In order to test the camera calibration detection
capability, we leverage LIBERO-Plus benchmark, and syn-
thesis viewpoint rotations from LIBERO-Spatial suite. As
shown in Figure 10, we could see that after camera view-
point shift, there is a gap between the visual location and
mask rendered from staled camera calibration. Table 7 fur-
ther shows the detection capability under different degrees
of camera viewpoint change.
