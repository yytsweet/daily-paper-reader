Abstract
Tactile feedback benefits contact-rich manipulation, yet de-
ploying tactile sensors introduces additional hardware and
maintenance requirements. Tactile experience can still pro-
vide effective supervision for policies that operate without
tactile sensors at deployment. Tactile imagination meth-
ods such as TacImag remove this hardware dependency
by predicting tactile observations, but retain a reconstruc-
tion stage whose errors can propagate into action gen-
eration. We present TacitTouch, a distillation framework
that transfers tactile-guided behavior directly into a vision-
only policy. The core shift is from “learn to imagine what
touch would feel like” to “learn to behave as if you could
feel touch”: the student directly learns tactile-guided ac-
tion patterns without reconstructing tactile observations. A
frozen visuotactile teacher supervises the student’s denois-
ing predictions and intermediate action representations at
the same noisy action and flow time. Training also uses
demonstrated-action supervision. Deployment retains only
the visual policy, with no tactile encoder, expert, or gener-
ator. Across four simulated UniVTAC tasks and four real-
world tasks, TacitTouch achieves absolute improvements of
16.0% and 22.5% in mean success rate over vision-only
control, respectively. The absolute gaps to the teacher us-
ing measured tactile input are only 2.0% and 3.75%, re-
spectively. In robustness tests, it also outperforms TacImag,
with absolute gains of up to 20.0% in mean success rate.

This use of interaction experience to guide action gen-
eration parallels how humans draw on prior manipulation
experience: recognizing familiar objects allows people to
scale lifting forces before weight feedback becomes avail-
able [10, 15].
Fingertip forces can also adapt to an ob-
ject’s actual weight while subjective weight judgments re-
main biased [13]. The distinction between motor adaptation
and perceptual experience offers an intuitive motivation for
transferring experience through behavior.
Figure 1 captures this behavioral focus: learn to imag-
ine what touch would feel like versus learn to behave as if
you could feel touch. TacitTouch places tactile guidance
in the training objective and retains the student’s standard
visual control path at deployment, without a tactile sensor,
encoder, expert, or generator. Compared with tactile imag-
ination, this removes both the cost of tactile reconstruction
and the dependence of action generation on its predicted
output.
On four contact-rich UniVTAC tasks [6], TacitTouch
reaches 77.5% mean success, compared with 61.5% for
vision-only control and 79.5% for the live-touch teacher.
On four real-world tasks, mean success increases from
50.00% to 72.50% over vision-only control. Deployment
timing shows that TacitTouch reduces policy-call latency by
25.8% relative to TacImag. Its mean success is also higher
than that baseline under image-brightness and camera-pose
perturbations.
Our contribution is a tactile-to-visual distillation frame-
work that transfers a visuotactile teacher’s behavior and ac-
tion representations into a vision-only policy through joint
denoising velocity and action-feature supervision. Tactile
experience thereby directly shapes the student’s behavior
learning. The framework retains the student’s standard vi-
sual control path without tactile sensing, reconstruction, or
additional tactile processing at deployment. Simulation and
real-world experiments demonstrate its benefits for contact-
rich manipulation and higher success under visual perturba-
tions.

tation into a visual student, allowing tactile experience to
guide policy learning while preserving the original visual
policy’s deployment architecture. Figure 2 shows the train-
ing and deployment pipeline.

3.3. Training and Deployment
We use frozen, task-trained SimpleTouch [38] as the
teacher,
initialize
the
student
from
pretrained
π0.5
weights [2], and jointly train its visual-language back-
bone and action expert. Offline training uses synchronized
demonstrations without environment rollouts; teacher and
student share augmented images, action representations,
and normalization.
Main simulation training uses eight-GPU FSDP [49],
global batch size 64, and 20,000 AdamW [25] updates. We
evaluate the final checkpoint; learning-rate schedules and
further settings appear in Appendix A.1.
At deployment, the student uses current images, instruc-
tions, and proprioception to generate a 50-action chunk
from Gaussian noise through ten Euler steps (Eq. 3). The
base policy’s inverse normalization and action adapter pro-
duce control commands; task settings determine how many
actions execute before the next observation. No teacher or
tactile processing is used.
4. Experiments
We evaluate manipulation performance, visual robustness,
and deployment cost, then use ablations and analysis to ex-
amine the effects and scope of tactile-guided transfer.

Tasks and Platforms.
The main simulation experiments

Real-world experiments use a

4.3. Visual Robustness
Visual perturbations are also studied in dedicated VLA
benchmarks [11]. We evaluate fixed checkpoints against
unperturbed Clean. Light halves camera RGB values be-
fore model preprocessing in simulation and the real world,
leaving scene lighting and tactile preprocessing unchanged.
Simulation-only Camera uniformly shifts head-camera yaw
within ±30◦and, when used, wrist-camera yaw within
±15◦. Methods share environment seeds and per-episode
perturbations, including views that cause occlusion.
TacitTouch outperforms TacImag under brightness per-
turbations on both platforms. Its simulation mean remains
77.5%, compared with 64.5% for TacImag (Table 2). Its
real-world mean is 66.25%, compared with 46.25% for
TacImag (Table 3). On the real robot, TacitTouch exceeds
both vision-only control and TacImag on all four tasks and
shows a smaller decrease from Clean.
SimpleTouch re-
tains the highest mean on both platforms, while TacImag re-

a Lift Bottle case with matched seeds and perturbations:
TacitTouch completes the lift, while TacImag fails. Trajec-
tory frames retain their original recorded brightness because
dimming is applied in the policy-input path.
In TacImag’s two-stage inference path, tactile-prediction
errors can propagate into subsequent action generation;
TacitTouch generates actions directly without this interme-
diate prediction, providing a plausible explanation for the
robustness gains.

method.

TacImag (Table 2). The largest task-level margin appears

versus 21% for TacImag.

After stable warmup, we measure 100 calls per method and
task, synchronizing the GPU before and after each call. The
timed interval includes preprocessing, model computation,
and CPU action output, excluding checkpoint loading, input
decoding, simulation, and communication. The complete
protocol is given in Appendix A.5.
TacitTouch achieves 0.337 seconds per call, reducing
mean latency by 25.8% relative to TacImag and 9.5% rel-
ative to SimpleTouch (Table 4). The deployed model con-
tains 3.35B parameters and retains only the visual-language
backbone and action expert.
Timing uses GT0 student
checkpoints (λGT = 0, without demonstrated-action super-
vision), while the main performance results use GT1 check-
points (λGT = 1, with this supervision). Both share the
same inference architecture, so the measurements charac-
terize their common deployment cost.

4.6. Analysis
4.6.1. Task-Relevant Visual Attention
Figure 6 compares π0.5, TacImag, and TacitTouch with
matched wrist images, instructions, and initial action noise;
each retains its native state input and receives no measured
touch.
TacitTouch emphasizes ball-surface texture for discrim-
ination in Ball Sorting and the receiving socket for align-
ment in Insert USB. In Wipe Board, attention highlights
residual writing beside the eraser, enlarged in the input in-
set, indicating where further cleaning is needed. In Chip
Moving, it emphasizes the gripper fingers adjacent to the
chip, highlighting contact geometry relevant to grasp sta-
bility. Because the visual input is matched across meth-
ods, the comparison concerns how each policy weights the
same scene rather than differences in camera framing. The
highlighted regions span object appearance, the interaction
target, and the gripper-object interface. These patterns are
consistent with the transfer of tactile-guided behavior into
task-relevant visual weighting, and the attention maps pro-
vide qualitative evidence of where the policies attend.
4.6.2. Scope of Tactile Transfer
The benefit of measured touch does not transfer uniformly
across tasks (Table 6).
On Pull-out Key, Insert Hole,
and Grasp Classify, tactile input substantially improves the
teacher, while the visual student remains close to the vision-
only baseline. For example, measured touch raises Insert
Hole success from 25% to 85%, but the student remains
at 25%. These tasks separate effective tactile control from
successful vision-only transfer: a capable teacher does not
ensure that a visual student recovers its advantage under the
evaluated training setup.
On Insert HDMI, all three methods remain at 3–4% suc-
cess, indicating that adding measured touch alone does not
resolve this task under the evaluated setup. Here, there is lit-
tle measured-touch benefit for the student to recover. These
results do not distinguish insufficient observation informa-
tion from training-objective or optimization effects.
5. Conclusion
We present TacitTouch, which transfers tactile-guided be-
havior into a vision-only policy through joint distillation
of a frozen visuotactile teacher’s denoising predictions
and intermediate action representations, combined with
demonstrated-action supervision. This allows the student to
retain its original visual control path without tactile sens-
ing or reconstruction at deployment.
Across four UniV-
TAC simulation tasks and four real-world tasks, the student

achieves absolute gains of 16.0% and 22.5% in mean suc-
cess over the vision-only baseline, respectively, approach-

Further

achieves higher mean success under visual perturbations
and its deployment architecture reduces policy-call latency
by 25.8%.

A.1.2. Proprioception, Action Representation, and Data

A.2. Baseline Implementations and Inputs
A.2.1. π0.5 and SimpleTouch
In Table 1, π0.5 uses images, language, and proprioception.
SimpleTouch adds a tactile branch to the same backbone. A
frozen T3 encoder extracts features from the current tactile
observation, and a separate tactile expert processes them.
TacitTouch uses SimpleTouch as the teacher and deploys
the visual student alone. Table S2 lists the tactile inputs and
modules used at inference.
Real-world π0.5 and SimpleTouch start from the same
pretrained π0.5 checkpoint, use the real-world optimiza-
tion settings in Sec. A.1, and use the checkpoint at update
20,000. SimpleTouch randomly initializes the tactile expert
and keeps the T3 encoder frozen during training.
Real-world SimpleTouch conditions on current touch
and predicts T3 features from future measured touch as an
auxiliary objective. It samples a target every five steps over
a 50-step horizon and pools the spatial features to 6 × 6.
It normalizes the targets by a reference root mean square
(RMS) from the frozen T3 encoder. Let γ and β denote the
per-channel scale and bias in the last LayerNorm. Under the
assumption of zero mean and unit variance in each channel
before the affine transform, each output channel has second
moment γ2 + β2. The reference RMS is

where c indexes the channels. The auxiliary loss weight
is fixed at 0.01 throughout training, with no warm-up or
scheduling. At the end of a trajectory, we repeat the last
target.

A.2.3. Mixed-Action Training for HapticVLA
Our HapticVLA baseline follows the student-stage action
mixing in the official Crab implementation [1]. A frozen,
task-trained SimpleTouch teacher precomputes a 50-action
chunk for each demonstration observation using ten flow-
matching steps. For each action step in the chunk, we aver-
age the demonstration target and teacher prediction elemen-
twise in the eight action dimensions, Amix = 0.5AGT +
0.5Ateacher, then zero-pad to 32 dimensions.
The student inherits the teacher’s shared vision-language
backbone and action weights, with the tactile modules re-
moved. We use the precomputed mixed actions as super-
vision, restart the optimizer and scheduler, and train for
20,000 updates with a global batch of 64. The learning rate
warms up to 5 × 10−5 over 1,000 updates and then decays
along a cosine schedule to 5 × 10−6.

For export, we construct a regular 15 Hz grid with an in-

the latest available frames from the three cameras (wrist,

the wait for each command to one interval. We allow the

A.3.4. Real-World Action Execution
All four tasks send the seven absolute joint-position tar-
gets obtained in Sec. A.1.2 as references to their low-level
arm controllers.
Ball Sorting passes these targets to the
Franka SDK’s joint-position interface. Wipe Board uses
the follower-arm controller from FACTR, a bilateral force-
feedback teleoperation system. It tracks the targets with
a joint-space proportional–derivative torque law based on
scaled position error and measured joint velocity.
Insert USB and Chip Moving reuse the controller from
our Pico teleoperation setup, which records demonstrations
using a PICO headset and a tracked handheld controller.
The controller computes the target end-effector pose by for-
ward kinematics and uses the SDK’s desired joint state to
compute pose and posture errors. It maps proportional pose
feedback to joint velocities through a damped Jacobian in-
verse and adds a projected joint-posture correction. Ruckig
smooths the resulting joint-velocity reference before execu-
tion. The gripper uses the Move interface to execute the
total opening specified by the eighth action dimension.
