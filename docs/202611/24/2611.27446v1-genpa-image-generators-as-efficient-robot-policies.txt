ABSTRACT
Pretrained visual generation models have shown great promise in robotic manip-
ulation. However, a representative line of work still treats future visual predic-
tion as the central link between visual generative pretraining and action learning,
retaining it as a training objective even when future scenes are no longer gener-
ated at inference time. This paradigm organizes policy learning around the proxy
task of predicting future visual states. We argue that the instruction-following,
spatial understanding, and visual imagination capabilities acquired through im-
age generation pretraining can support control without future visual prediction:
image generation models themselves can be directly adapted into powerful ac-
tion learners. Building on this perspective, we propose GenPA, which fine-tunes
an image generation backbone with a lightweight 19M-parameter action regres-
sion adapter and reuses its native generative objective for perception co-training.
The native image-editing interface accommodates different visual targets without
task-specific perception heads, enabling comparisons of current-scene perception,
future prediction, and their combinations within a common policy architecture.
At deployment, GenPA requires only a single backbone forward pass and action
regression, without visual generation or iterative action denoising. Without addi-
tional large-scale robot-data pretraining, GenPA demonstrates strong control per-
formance and robustness to perturbations across simulation benchmarks (99.10%
success on LIBERO and 83.79% on LIBERO-Plus) and real-world manipulation
tasks. Under compiled inference, GenPA takes 20.9 ms per action chunk on a sin-
gle H100 GPU, achieving 1.7–3.4× speedups over strong VLA and WAM base-
lines. Our latency analysis further highlights architectural advantages of visual
generation backbones for efficient robot control. Ablations across visual tasks
and their combinations identify current-depth supervision as a simple, effective
training recipe. Together, these results show that image generators can be directly
adapted into strong, efficient robot policies without requiring future visual predic-
tion.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in image generation pretraining have yielded strong instruction-following and vi-
sual understanding capabilities. Beyond synthesizing realistic images, these models can identify
objects referred to in language, follow spatial constraints, and visualize instructed changes to a
scene (Xiao et al., 2025; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). As illustrated in Fig-
ure 1(a), given a robot manipulation instruction and a corresponding editing request, an image gen-
eration system can depict the scene after task completion or sketch end-effector motion paths on
the current image. These behaviors suggest that the spatial understanding and visual imagination
capabilities acquired through generative pretraining may provide valuable priors for robot action
learning.

2024) or decoding actions from predicted visual transitions through inverse dynamics (Li et al.,
2026). Several methods jointly predict future observations and actions within a shared generative
model (Shen et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026b). Recent approaches further reduce
or bypass future visual generation at deployment while retaining future prediction as a co-training
objective (Yuan et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026a). In the broader context of predic-
tive policy learning, future supervision has also been extended to depth, 3D pointmaps, and semantic
features (Zhang et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026). Despite differences in architecture and deployment,
these methods continue to organize policy learning around the proxy task of predicting the future.
We argue that the control value of image generation pretraining need not be realized through the in-
termediate objective of future visual prediction: image generation models themselves can be directly
adapted into powerful action learners. Prior work demonstrates the value of features from frozen im-
age generators for control (Shin et al., 2026). We instead adapt the backbone itself through action
learning, with a lightweight adapter mapping its features to actions. Meanwhile, direct action learn-
ing need not discard the generator’s visual output capabilities. Research on generative perception
shows that depth estimation, surface normal prediction, and segmentation can be learned as image
generation tasks (Gabeur et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026). Together with evidence that auxiliary depth
and spatial supervision benefit policy learning (Li et al., 2025b; Tu et al., 2026), these findings mo-
tivate reusing the generator’s native interface for current-scene perception co-training. Representing
different targets through this common interface also enables a systematic investigation of which vi-
sual supervision benefits control, including comparisons with future prediction and combinations of
visual tasks.
Building on this perspective, we introduce GenPA (Generative Perception for Action), an end-to-
end policy that fine-tunes the pretrained image generation backbone FLUX.2 klein Base 4B (Black
Forest Labs, 2026) with a 19M-parameter action regression adapter by default. During training,
action and perception objectives jointly update the backbone, with perception supervision reusing
its native image-editing interface and generative objective. Deployment requires only a single back-
bone forward pass and action regression, without visual generation or iterative action denoising.
Figures 1(b) and 2 provide an overview and the detailed architecture, respectively.
Without additional large-scale robot-data pretraining, GenPA achieves strong control performance
on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0. On real-world contact-rich manipulation, GenPA
2


outperforms π0.5, Fast-WAM, and ImageWAM while demonstrating strong robustness to visual per-
turbations. Under compiled inference on a single H100 GPU, GenPA takes 20.9 ms per action chunk,
achieving 1.7–3.4× speedups over the evaluated VLA and WAM baselines. Our ablations further
show that direct action regression achieves better closed-loop control performance with lower in-
ference latency than a flow-based action head in our main evaluations. Compared with direct depth
regression, native flow-based depth supervision improves control robustness at a higher training cost.
Our contributions are twofold. (i) We introduce GenPA, demonstrating that an image generation
backbone can be directly adapted into a strong, efficient robot policy without a future visual pre-
diction objective. Its native visual co-training interface supports diverse supervision targets without
task-specific perception heads, enabling systematic comparisons of visual tasks and their combina-
tions within a common policy architecture. (ii) Our controlled studies show that strong closed-loop
control can be achieved with direct action regression, without iterative action denoising, and iden-
tify current-depth supervision as a simple, effective training recipe. Our latency analysis further
reveals an architectural advantage of visual generation backbones for robot control: lightweight ob-
servation encoding and independent language encoding align computation with the different update
frequencies of observations and instructions.

3
EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether a pretrained image generator can be directly adapted into a strong, efficient
robot policy without a future visual prediction objective. Simulation and real-world experiments
assess control performance and robustness, while latency profiling and visual-supervision ablations
examine the sources of inference cost and the effects of perception supervision.
3.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We train separate multitask policies on LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 without additional large-scale
robot-data pretraining. Unless otherwise stated, GenPA uses current-depth supervision during train-
ing. Training and evaluation details are provided in Appendices A and C.
LIBERO.
We train jointly on the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites of LIBERO (Liu et al.,
2023a), comprising 40 tasks. We replay the 2,000 released demonstrations to construct visual super-
vision labels, retaining 1,808 successfully replayed demonstrations for training (Appendix B.2). The
LIBERO policy configuration uses 224×224 views with training-time image augmentation, whereas
the visual-target ablation configuration retains native 256 × 256 views without augmentation. Our
policies in the external benchmark comparisons and visual-target ablations use training seed 42.
Internal comparisons under the LIBERO policy configuration report mean ± sample standard devi-
ation across three training seeds. LIBERO results marked independent use the same statistics across
three evaluation seeds with new initial-state sets for fixed policies trained with seed 42. We evaluate
50 trials per task and report suite success rates and their arithmetic mean. The GenPA score in Ta-
ble 1 is from checkpoint selection; selection and independent-evaluation protocols are specified in
Appendix C.1.
LIBERO-Plus.
We evaluate LIBERO-Plus using the same checkpoint reported on standard
LIBERO, without fine-tuning or separate checkpoint selection. We run one trial on each of the
10,030 released instances across seven perturbation categories (Fei et al., 2026). Unless other-
wise specified, we use Clean-Instruction, which removes extraneous perturbation metadata (Ap-
pendix C.2). Overall and category success rates pool outcomes over all instances and within each
category, respectively.
RoboTwin 2.0.
We train one policy jointly across 50 bimanual tasks using 2,500 clean and 25,000
randomized demonstrations with depth annotations (Chen et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026). We use a
99%/1% episode-level training/validation split. We evaluate 100 trials per task under each of the
Clean and Randomized conditions and report mean task success for each condition and their arith-
metic mean. For RoboTwin, we use a wider action adapter for coordinated bimanual manipulation.
Baselines.
Our comparisons cover representative VLA and WAM policies (Kim et al., 2025; Phys-
ical Intelligence et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Luo et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026b; Yuan et al.,
2026a; Chen et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026b; AMAP CV Lab, 2026; Zhang et al.,
2026a; Bi et al., 2025). Tables indicate additional robot-data pretraining. LIBERO and RoboTwin
baseline scores come from published reports. For LIBERO-Plus, we re-evaluate six released base-
lines under Clean-Instruction using their native inference configurations.

Why visual generation backbones suit efficient control.
A more revealing difference lies in the
computation required for each new observation. Visual encoding is substantially more expensive
in the two evaluated VLM-based policies than VAE encoding in policies built on visual generation
backbones. This reflects different divisions of computation: VLMs first extract high-level features
with a vision encoder and then perform fusion and reasoning in a multimodal backbone; latent
visual generators use a lightweight VAE to compress observations into reconstructable latents, per-
form conditional visual modeling in the visual generation backbone, and encode language indepen-
dently (Rombach et al., 2022). Comparing backbone sizes alone therefore overlooks the different
visual-encoding costs these architectures incur at each observation update. The latter organization
matches the inputs of robotic manipulation: observations change continuously, whereas instructions
typically remain fixed within a task. Language encoding can thus be amortized across policy calls,
while each new observation passes through a lightweight VAE and the visual generation backbone.
GenPA combines this organization with a lightweight regression adapter, producing actions through
a single backbone forward pass and direct action readout. These results suggest that efficient pol-
icy design should not only simplify action generation but also align module boundaries with input
update frequencies; visual generation pretraining provides a favorable architectural basis for this
organization.

established through closed-loop evaluation. Current-depth supervision remains a simple, effective
default.
Why is depth supervision effective?
Depth improves success over None in all seven perturba-
tion categories, whereas other geometric targets do not yield equally broad gains. We hypothesize
that depth benefits from combining control-relevant geometry with a simple supervision format. It
provides metric distance constraints on visible surfaces, encouraging the backbone to learn spatial
structure relevant to reaching, grasping, and placement. This information is expressed as a dense
scalar field aligned with the current observation, whose definition does not depend on task roles or
execution stages. In contrast, robot-frame pointmaps additionally require learning a mapping from
the current view to the robot frame; normals primarily constrain surface orientation rather than met-
ric distance; and segmentation emphasizes categories and boundaries. These differences suggest
that the value of auxiliary supervision depends not only on its information content, but also on what
it asks the backbone to learn and how these constraints support control.

RELATED WORK
Visual generative pretraining for robot control.
Pretrained image and video generators provide
visual subgoals for goal-conditioned policies (Black et al., 2024) or predicted transitions for inverse
9


dynamics (Li et al., 2026). Other methods jointly generate future observations and actions (Shen
et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026b), or retain future prediction for co-training while en-
abling action-only inference (Yuan et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026a). The closely related ImageWAM
combines image-editing pretraining, future-image co-training, and a flow-based action expert, sup-
porting inference without image generation while retaining iterative action decoding (Zhang et al.,
2026a). Beyond future prediction, generators can represent actions through joint-localization tar-
gets, pixel-motion fields, or multiview action images (Shridhar et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2026;
Zhen et al., 2026). ORCA instead extracts task-adaptive features from a frozen diffusion model
through learned conditioning (Shin et al., 2026). Whereas Fast-WAM and ImageWAM retain future-
prediction co-training, GenPA fine-tunes the image generation backbone for direct action prediction
through a lightweight regression adapter, coupled with current-scene perception co-training.
Perceptual supervision for policy adaptation.
Auxiliary perception objectives have been stud-
ied across policy architectures. They range from future visual prediction (Wu et al., 2024; Tian
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) to current-scene depth supervision (Li et al., 2025b; Yuan et al.,
2025) and localization, segmentation, or spatial-relation tasks (Tu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025a).
Geometric pretraining and supervision further support policy adaptation (Song et al., 2026; Peng
et al., 2026), while representation analyses link state decodability to control performance (Dong
et al., 2026). LingBot-VLA 2.0 distills depth and video representations at both current and future
timesteps (Wu et al., 2026a). Generative perception research demonstrates that visual generators can
learn dense geometry (Ke et al., 2024; He et al., 2025) and diverse instruction-conditioned percep-
tion tasks (Gabeur et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). GenPA uses the generator’s
native image-editing interface for perception co-training without task-specific perception heads. Per-
ceptual objectives shape the shared backbone during training; predicted annotations are not inputs
to the deployed policy. Our ablations compare current-scene and future targets, as well as target
combinations, within a common policy architecture to assess their utility for closed-loop control.
We further compare native depth supervision with a direct depth-regression head to evaluate their
control performance and training cost.
Action prediction and inference efficiency.
Action chunking is established in visuomotor imita-
tion (Zhao et al., 2023), with actions produced through diffusion or flow matching (Chi et al., 2023;
Black et al., 2025), autoregressive token decoding (Pertsch et al., 2025), or parallel regression (Kim
et al., 2025). Lightweight action adapters (Wang et al., 2026b), fewer generative decoding steps (Guo
et al., 2026), and decoupling action-module depth from the visual backbone (Ma et al., 2026) offer
different routes to efficient inference. GenPA combines lightweight regression with an adapted im-
age generation backbone and compares regression and flow-based action readout under a common
training framework to examine whether strong closed-loop control requires iterative action denois-
ing. Beyond action decoding, our component-level analysis examines visual encoding and backbone
computation, relating architectural differences to the recurring costs of changing observations and
the reusable computation associated with fixed instructions.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented GenPA, which directly adapts a pretrained image generation model into a robot policy
through lightweight action regression and native perception co-training, without requiring future
visual prediction as an intermediate training objective. At deployment, GenPA requires only a single
backbone forward pass and action regression. GenPA combines strong control performance and
perturbation robustness in simulation and real-world manipulation with low measured inference
latency. Ablations of visual supervision show that depth supervision on current observations offers
a simple, effective training recipe, while adding targets does not always improve control. Our latency
analysis further suggests that efficient policy design should not only simplify action decoding but
also match computation to input update frequencies. Visual generation models provide a favorable
architectural basis for this organization: a lightweight VAE encodes changing observations, while
independently encoded language features can be reused across observations.

GenPA is initialized from FLUX.2 klein Base 4B (Black Forest Labs, 2026). Table 8 summarizes
the architecture and training status of each component. Following the native FLUX.2 conditioning
pipeline, we concatenate the frozen Qwen3-4B encoder’s hidden states from layers 9, 18, and 27 at
each text position to produce 7,680-dimensional conditioning features.
The frozen VAE encodes observations and perception targets into 32-channel latent feature maps.

A.2
ACTION ADAPTERS
Regression action adapter.
The default adapter layer-normalizes the final reference-token states
and projects them from 3,072 to 512 dimensions. We add two-dimensional sinusoidal position fea-
tures derived from the image-token coordinates and apply a second layer normalization to form the
17


visual memory. Proprioception is encoded by LayerNorm–Linear–GELU–Linear, with both linear
outputs of width 512, and added to each of 16 learned action queries in the simulation configu-
rations. Four pre-normalized decoder blocks apply bidirectional self-attention among the queries,
cross-attention to the visual memory, and a feed-forward network. Each attention layer has eight
heads; the feed-forward width is 2,048, with GELU activation and zero dropout. A final LayerNorm
and linear projection predict the complete action chunk in parallel. The adapter reads the backbone
output without writing states back into the backbone.
For RoboTwin 2.0, we retain the four-layer, eight-head architecture and 16 action queries, but in-
crease the adapter width to 1,024 and the feed-forward width to 4,096. The visual-memory projec-
tion, proprioception encoder, learned queries, and output projection use this wider representation.
Flow-based action adapter.
The flow-based adapter matches the default regression adapter in
backbone, Action Prefix, visual-memory projection, decoder depth, hidden width, and attention-
head count. Each noised seven-dimensional action is linearly projected to width 512, replacing the
learned action queries. Proprioception is encoded by the same MLP as in the regression adapter,
followed by LayerNorm, and appended as one token to the visual memory. Self-attention uses one-
dimensional RoPE with base 10,000 over the 16 action positions. Flow time is represented by a
256-dimensional sinusoidal embedding of 1000τ, followed by a two-layer width-512 MLP with
SiLU. Each decoder block uses the embedding to modulate its three pre-normalized sublayers; the
modulation projections are zero initialized. A final LayerNorm and linear projection predict the
seven-dimensional action velocity.
For a normalized demonstration chunk At, we sample one time τ ∼U(0, 1) per chunk and Gaussian
noise ϵ of the same shape. Following the flow convention in Equation 1, the noised action chunk is
At,τ = (1 −τ)At + τϵ.
(6)
The adapter predicts the velocity ϵ −At and minimizes mean-squared error over valid action ele-
ments within each chunk, averaged across the batch. Key padding masks prevent attention to padded
action steps, which are also excluded from the loss. At inference, eight uniform Euler steps inte-
grate from τ = 1 to zero. The VAE and DiT execute once per observation, and the decoder’s
cross-attention keys and values are cached throughout the action loop. The action-flow time is in-
dependent of the perception-flow time; the backbone retains the fixed condition τA = 0 during the
action forward pass.

A.3
INPUT AND OUTPUT INTERFACES
Text conditioning.
The text-conditioning sequence contains 128 positions in both forward passes.
In the action forward pass, 16 learned Action Prefix tokens occupy the first positions, leaving up
to 112 positions for instruction embeddings. In the perception forward pass, no Action Prefix is
inserted, so all 128 positions are available for the perception prompt. Unused positions are right
padded and masked.
Visual inputs and perception outputs.
For an image canvas of height HI and width WI, the VAE
produces a latent tensor in R32×HI/8×WI/8. Packing 2 × 2 neighborhoods gives (HI/16)(WI/16)
image tokens of dimension 128. Reference tokens encode the current observations. Target tokens
have the same layout but are instantiated only in the perception forward pass. The perception output
is a velocity tensor aligned with these target tokens.

The simulation action output is a chunk in R16×da, where da is

For RoboTwin 2.0, the policy predicts 12 absolute target joint angles and two continuous gripper
commands. Proprioception contains the corresponding 14-dimensional current state. Both joint and

A.5
TRAINING AND OPTIMIZATION
We train all GenPA policies reported in this paper with RADAR, an optimizer from the RAD fam-
ily (Lyu et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2026). We set (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95), enable decoupled weight
decay with coefficient 0.01, and use γ = 0, l = 0, δ = 1, and ζ = 10−16.
During native perception co-training, action and perception losses are backpropagated separately,
with gradients accumulated before each optimizer update. Configurations that sample multiple per-
ception targets are specified in Appendix B.4. We train in BF16 and clip the gradient norm at 1.0.
Seed settings are specified in Appendix C.1. Learning rates warm up linearly over the first 5% of
updates and then follow cosine decay to an absolute minimum of 10−6 for each parameter group.
The action-interface learning rate applies to both the Action Prefix and adapter. The training config-
uration uses eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs with 80 GB of memory each.

AUXILIARY DEPTH-REGRESSION HEAD
For the direct-depth comparison in Section 3.5, we use a randomly initialized DPT-style de-
coder (Ranftl et al., 2021) with 11.064M parameters, branch widths of [128, 256, 512, 512], and
a fusion width of 128. All four branches read the same final reference-token features. The two cam-

add two-dimensional sinusoidal positional embeddings after branch projection and final upsampling,
and use an unconstrained linear output.
The depth head and action adapter share a single backbone forward pass without target-image to-
kens. We jointly optimize the action loss and a pixel-wise depth MSE loss with coefficient 0.1, using

Task-conditioned grounding (G).
G encodes three independent binary roles in R/G/B: the op-
eration object, goal or anchor, and active articulated part. A pixel can occupy more than one role.
Roles are assigned to task-relevant instances using task predicates and the demonstration’s execution
22


stage. The active stage follows the order in which predicates become persistently satisfied in that
demonstration. The prerequisite drawer-opening stage requires three consecutive satisfied frames to
be marked complete. Grounding is defined only before full task completion and only for a camera
with at least 64 visible role pixels.
Future RGB (F) and future depth (FD).
For current timestep j in a filtered demonstration with
L retained timesteps, the target index is jf = min(j + 16, L −1). F is the two-view RGB image
at jf; FD is the depth image from the same future state, encoded and validated exactly as LIBERO
D. Both use the current RGB canvas as their reference. When j + 16 exceeds the final retained
timestep, we use the final frame. The offset counts retained action steps after no-op filtering, not
necessarily 16 original simulator frames. FD is a single future-depth task; D+F combines current
depth with future RGB.

SIMULATION EVALUATION AND BASELINE DETAILS
C.1
STANDARD LIBERO
The repeated-training comparisons use seeds 42, 142, and 242 for None, current depth, future RGB,
the flow-based action adapter, and DPT regression under the LIBERO policy configuration. Other
GenPA training runs retain the original seed 42.
Evaluation protocol.
We evaluate each of the 40 tasks for 50 trials, using the benchmark’s re-
leased initial states in their provided order and the simulator’s task-success signal. Episodes allow
up to 400 action steps for Spatial, Object, and Goal and 700 for Long, following 30 initial no-op
steps. The policy predicts 16 actions per call and executes the first 12 before replanning, with binary
gripper postprocessing and no temporal action ensemble. Unless otherwise stated, GenPA bench-
mark success rates are measured using uncompiled inference.
Checkpoint selection.
We apply one selection rule to all our LIBERO-trained policies, including
both action heads and all perception-supervision configurations. The candidate set consists of ten
checkpoints at 20k, 22k, 24k, 26k, 28k, 30k, 32k, 34k, 36k, and 36.75k action updates. We select
the checkpoint with the most successes across the 2,000 standard LIBERO trials, breaking ties in
favor of the earlier checkpoint. The same checkpoint is used for LIBERO-Plus, without further
25


selection or fine-tuning. We denote the selected checkpoint as Top, the default for reported results,
and the fixed 36,750-step checkpoint as Final. Unless marked independent, our standard LIBERO
results use the same initial states as checkpoint selection. Complete checkpoint scans are provided
in Appendix C.3.
Independent LIBERO evaluation.
Since standard LIBERO scores are used for checkpoint selec-
tion, we re-evaluate the Top checkpoints from training seed 42 using three evaluation seeds (1042,
1142, and 1242). Each seed provides 50 new initial states per task, yielding 6,000 episodes per pol-
icy. The states satisfy the original task constraints and are disjoint across sets and from those used
for checkpoint selection. All policies use the same three sets.

D.2
MEASUREMENT PROTOCOL
We measure batch-one policy calls on an NVIDIA H100 80GB HBM3 (700 W power limit) and
an NVIDIA L40S (350 W power limit). Both systems use PyTorch 2.7.1 and CUDA 11.8. Mod-
els use BF16 for their main computations while retaining implementation-specific higher-precision
operations. Each configuration is benchmarked in three independent runs, each in a fresh process
with exclusive GPU access and four CPU inference threads. After 50 warmup calls, each process
measures the same 200 observations from 40 LIBERO tasks (five observations per task). We report
the mean and the sample standard deviation of the three process means. Latencies are per native
action chunk, whose horizon differs across policies. Uncompiled denotes PyTorch eager execu-
tion; compiled denotes execution with torch.compile and TorchInductor, with model-specific
compilation scope described below.
For the main comparison, synchronized wall-clock timing begins with decoded CPU RGB images,
raw proprioception, and either a raw instruction or cached text features, as specified below. It ends
with the CPU action chunk after native postprocessing. Image preprocessing, CPU–GPU transfers,
model execution, and action denormalization are included. Image-file decoding, model loading,
compilation, and environment or robot communication are excluded. Only observation-independent
text features may be reused across calls; visual features and observation-conditioned representations
are recomputed for every observation. These offline measurements do not measure robot request–
response time or physical control frequency.
The total labels in Figure 3 and the L40S totals below come from complete calls without internal
instrumentation. The colored component intervals in that figure come from a separate profiling pass
with the same inputs and warmup procedure. They are not rescaled to match the independently
timed totals.

EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We evaluate task execution under three visual conditions. In all conditions, the initial right-arm pose
and the source port on the left hub vary randomly across trials, while the destination remains the
first port on the right hub. At reset, the end-effector position is perturbed by ∆x ∈[−0.05, 0] m and
34


∆y, ∆z ∈[−0.05, 0.05] m, while its orientation remains fixed. Each policy is evaluated for 50 trials
per condition, totaling 150 trials.
• Standard: the nominal scene, with the initial-condition variations described above.
• Background: visual distractors, such as a game controller and tape, are added to the
workspace.
• Lighting: room lights are switched off, and the scene is illuminated by the recording
phone’s LED.
A trial succeeds only if the robot unplugs the connector, inserts it into the target port, and releases the
gripper within 50 seconds without dropping the connector. This timeout is used to terminate stalled
trials; in our evaluations, it did not interrupt any trial still progressing toward task completion. We
report success rates for each condition and their arithmetic mean in Table 4. Figure 6 shows example
executions under each condition.
