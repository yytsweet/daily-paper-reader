Abstract Abstract 

LLM quantization reduces storage but often degrades predictive fidelity at low bit rates. We propose a training-free, CDF-free, Gaussian-CLT-motivated progressive residual-sign quantization framework that uses randomized orthogonal mixing to promote Gaussian-like residual distributions and improve sign-based reconstruction of Transformer weights. Each stage independently normalizes residuals and analytically calibrates reconstruction, progressively recovering quantization errors. The framework enables flexible storage–fidelity trade-offs without learned codebooks, empirical CDFs, or trellis optimization. We evaluate GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B across attention and MLP projections. At five residual stages, joint quantization preserves near-baseline predictive fidelity, achieving perplexities of 32.16 and 12.73, compared with full-precision values of 29.89 and 11.07, respectively. Ablations further demonstrate that orthogonal mixing substantially improves reconstruction over identity-based sign coding, while activation-aware scaling improves output fidelity and reduced-precision statistics lower storage overhead with negligible perplexity degradation. Comparisons with conventional quantizers establish the method’s progressive fidelity recovery and characterize its storage–quality and runtime trade-offs. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonally mixed residual-sign coding for flexible, training-free Transformer weight compression. 

Keywords: large language models, post-training quantization, orthogonal mixing, progressive residual coding. 

## 1 Introduction 

Transformer models Vaswani et al. (2017), including GPT-2 and the LLaMA family Radford et al. (2019); Touvron et al. (2023a,b), impose substantial weight-storage, transfer, and inference-memory costs. Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces these costs without retraining. Established methods include outlier-aware LLM.int8() Dettmers et al. (2022), second-order GPTQ Frantar et al. (2023), activation-aware SmoothQuant and AWQ Xiao et al. (2023); Lin et al. (2024), and rotation-based QuaRot and SpinQuant Ashkboos et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2025). Yet scalar quantization does not directly exploit relationships among coordinates of a weight vector. 1arXiv:2611.31089v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 Vector quantization (VQ) captures such structure through joint encoding and has a long history in information theory and signal processing where given a matrix W ∈ Rd×n of n vectors in dimension d, is mapped to a discrete, finite subset of representative vectors called a codebook. Then, using just the codebook approximately reconstruct the original matrix. Recently, VQ has been central to AI infrastructure with techniques that employ additive codebooks Egiazarian et al. (2024), data-aware codebooks and error compensation van Baalen et al. (2024), structured lattice codebooks Tseng et al. (2024a), or trellis coding Tseng et al. (2024b). These methods motivate a question: can vector weights be compressed progressively, with explicit control over reconstruction fidelity, without learned codebooks, trellis search, or distribution-dependent Cumulative Distribution Function(CDF) computations? This question matters because a fixed low-bit representation may offer insufficient fidelity, while storing additional correction stages could recover accuracy at an added storage cost. In this paper, we introduce a codebook-free, CDF-free, information theoretically near-optimal, 

Progressive Residual-sign Vector Codec where each stage centers and RMS-normalizes a weight or residual vector, applies seeded orthogonal mixing, and encodes the transformed coordinates by their signs. Compact statistics and a fitted least-squares gain support reconstruction; blockwise distortion selects among deterministic transform candidates. The codec then encodes the remaining error as successive sign-coded corrections, accepting a stage only when it decreases blockwise RMS distortion. Any accepted prefix can be decoded, making residual-stage depth an explicit storage–fidelity control rather than requiring a separately trained quantizer for each operating point. Gaussian central-limit behavior motivates mixing but is neither assumed for correctness nor evaluated through CDF or inverse-CDF operations. Activation calibration does not update model weights; no gradients, distillation, or recovery fine-tuning are required. We evaluate GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B under attention-only, MLP-only, and joint attention– MLP quantization, measuring reconstruction error, perplexity, output fidelity, serialized storage, and runtime across residual depths and codec ablations. Our contributions are: (i) a CDF-free, sign-based vector codec with compact reconstruction statistics; (ii) distortion-aware transform selection and monotonic residual refinement; and (iii) a model-level characterization of progressive storage–fidelity trade-offs without retraining. Our results show that deeper residual refinement substantially improves model fidelity, although it increases storage and reconstruction cost. Under joint attention–MLP quantization at five residual stages, perplexity reaches 32 .16 on GPT-2 Large and 12 .73 on Llama-3-8B, compared with full-precision baselines of 29 .89 and 11 .07, respectively. Ablations identify activation-aware scaling and transform selection as useful contributors to fidelity. These findings demonstrate the potential of training-free progressive residual-sign quantization to balance compression efficiency and model fidelity through adjustable residual-stage depth. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Aggressive weight compression requires preserving useful model information within a limited storage budget, including the auxiliary information needed for reconstruction. The choice of representation is therefore central: it determines what information is retained, how weights are reconstructed, and how additional bits improve the approximation. Our work investigates a progressively refinable representation based on orthogonally mixed sign vectors. Its distinguishing contribution is the design of each residual stage, which combines distortion-based selection with reconstruction statistics and transform metadata. Researchers have proposed different PTQ techniques for compressing Transformer weight matrices 

W ∈ Rdout ×din without retraining. GPTQ uses approximate second-order reconstruction information 2Frantar et al. (2023); AWQ protects activation-salient channels Lin et al. (2024); SmoothQuant shifts quantization difficulty between weights and activations Xiao et al. (2023); and LLM.int8() treats outliers separately Dettmers et al. (2022). In spite of their significant contribution these methods don’t explore VQ. Whiles the scalar quantizers encode coefficients, vector quantizers encode blocks. AQLM uses additive codebooks Egiazarian et al. (2024), and GPTVQ uses data-aware vector coding van Baalen et al. (2024). QuIP# combines incoherence processing with structured lattice codebooks Tseng et al. (2024a), while QTIP uses trellis-coded quantization to access high effective vector dimensions without an exponential codebook Tseng et al. (2024b). Our codec instead stores transformed signs, reconstruction statistics, and transform metadata, providing a block representation that requires neither centroid lookup nor trellis optimization. Orthogonal transforms preserve Euclidean norm while redistributing coordinate magnitudes. QuaRot and SpinQuant use rotations to improve quantization robustness Ashkboos et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2025). TurboQuant combines random rotations with distribution-matched scalar quantization and, for inner-product estimation, a one-bit residual correction Zandieh et al. (2026). We directly sign-code orthogonally mixed vectors and recursively encode their residuals. Our encoder is CDF-free :it requires neither empirical CDF estimation nor inverse-CDF tables. Residual quantization constructs ̂ x(r) = ∑rk=1 ̂ e(k), with e(k) = e(k−1) −̂ e(k) and e(0) = x. RRQ applies low-bit scalar quantizers to a base and its residuals, producing multi-precision LLM weights from one checkpoint Luo et al. (2026). Our contribution is the residual-stage codec : each correction is a distortion-selected, orthogonally mixed sign vector reconstructed from ( μ, σ, γ ) and transform metadata. Thus, the distinction lies in the stage representation, rather than residual refinement itself: additional stages refine the approximation through sign-vector geometry rather than increased scalar precision. LoRA and QLoRA adapt model weights through optimization Hu et al. (2022); Dettmers et al. (2023). Our quantizer requires neither model retraining nor recovery-adapter training; calibration examples supply only activation statistics. This avoids an additional training phase while retaining distortion-based selection during encoding. Random orthogonal mixing forms weighted sums of normalized block elements. A central-limit argument suggests approximately Gaussian marginals under suitable regularity conditions, but does not guarantee Gaussianity for arbitrary weights. Gaussianity motivates mixing; the codec itself requires neither Gaussian probability evaluation nor empirical CDF mappings.

## 5 Experimental Methodology 

We evaluate GPT-2 Large ( gpt2-large ) and Llama-3-8B ( meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B ) under attention, MLP, and attention+MLP quantization, excluding embeddings and the language-model head. Each scope uses r ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 5}, yielding 2 × 3 × 4 = 24 primary conditions. Calibration, test, and held-out subsets are constructed from WikiText-2 raw ( wikitext-2-raw-v1 ). Original splits are combined, normalized, filtered, deduplicated, and deterministically shuffled (seed 42); the first 512 samples form the calibration pool, the next 256 the test set, and the final 256 the held-out set. Calibration only estimates activation statistics; runs use at most 32 calibration samples, and manuscript metrics use the held-out set. The supplied Llama-3-8B evaluation JSON identifies data/evaluation.txt , a limit of 256 evaluation samples, a maximum sequence length of 256, and 36,153 evaluated tokens. The JSON alone does not independently verify the provenance of the evaluation file. The full-precision model is the reference. For Llama-3-8B, the reported checkpoint sizes are measured serialized overlay sizes; they do not include all unchanged full-precision parameters. Llama-3-8B runtime measurements use model-sharded execution and should not be interpreted as optimized compressed-kernel performance. Parameter metrics include MSE, RMSE, MAE, relative error, cosine similarity, PSNR, and SNR; functional metrics include logit MSE/cosine similarity and next-token perplexity under matched tokenization and samples. Storage metrics include selected-weight payload, compression ratio, bits per parameter, and serialized checkpoint size. Because checkpoints can be overlays, their size is distinguished from self-contained deployment storage. We also record quantization/evaluation runtime and GPU memory where available. 6.3 Cross-Model Analysis 

GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B exhibit the same broad trend: increasing residual depth reduces parameter distortion and improves language-model fidelity for attention-only, MLP-only, and joint quantization. However, their baseline perplexities and quantized parameter counts differ, so absolute perplexities and raw checkpoint sizes should not be interpreted as matching comparisons. GPT-2 Large has a full-precision perplexity of 29 .89, and Llama-3-8B has 11 .07. At r = 5, GPT-2 Large perplexity is 31 .44, 31 .15, and 32 .16 for attention, MLP, and joint scopes, corresponding to approximately 5 .2%, 4 .2%, and 7 .6% above baseline. For Llama-3-8B the corresponding perplexities are 11 .90, 12 .55, and 12 .73, or 7 .5%, 13 .3%, and 15 .0% above baseline. Attention-only quantization is the least disruptive scope at r = 1 for both models, but differences among scopes remain visible at r = 5, particularly for Llama-3-8B. The larger Llama-3-8B quantized scopes also incur substantially larger serialized overlays and longer streaming reconstruction times. These figures characterize the measured prototype, not a normalized compression advantage or hardware speedup. The results support residual depth as a useful fidelity control while underscoring the need for scope-aware and implementation-aware evaluation. 

6.4 Orthogonal-Mixing Comparison 

A separate joint-scope benchmark evaluates FP16 reconstructed dense weights on 4,095 scored tokens; its results are not directly comparable to the scope-specific streaming runs. Figure 3 shows that orthogonal mixing lowers GPT-2 Large perplexity and selected-weight MSE at all tested depths. On Llama-3-8B, MSE improves at every depth and perplexity at r = 2 , 3, 5; the omitted r = 1 perplexity is worse with orthogonal mixing (2 .42 × 10 6 versus 9 .03 × 10 5). Estimated payloads are nearly matched between the two sign variants. 

Scalar and vector baselines. The orthogonal sign codec does not dominate conventional quantizers in perplexity per selected-weight payload. At r = 5, GPT-2 Large sign coding yields PPL 39 .73 at 6 .618 estimated BPW, whereas scalar 4-bit coding at r = 1 yields 37 .59 at 3 .658 BPW and residual VQ ( K = 256, r = 5) yields 36 .98 at 4 .995 BPW. Thus, the benchmark supports orthogonal mixing and progressive sign refinement, not a universally better rate–distortion frontier. The full comparison and measurement caveats appear in Appendix B. 

## 7 Ablation Studies 

Both models use joint attention+MLP quantization at r = 5 with n = m = 256, 32-bit statistics, 128 block rows, block batch size 4, K = 4, activation-aware scaling (AAS), 32 calibration samples, monotonic stage acceptance, and seed 42 unless otherwise stated. Detailed measurements appear in Appendix A. For GPT-2 Large, AAS reduces logit MSE from 0 .3001 to 0 .2543 and perplexity increase from 9 .79% to 7 .63%, despite higher parameter MSE. Four transform candidates outperform one; calibration-size and block-row effects are modest. Using 16-bit rather than 32-bit statistics increases compression from 4 .77 × to 5 .53 × with negligible fidelity change. Vector dimension controls the observed storage–fidelity trade-off. For Llama-3-8B, AAS reduces logit MSE from 1 .0983 to 0 .6920 and PPL from 13 .9445 to 12 .5854, although parameter MSE rises from 3 .379 × 10 −6 to 6 .867 × 10 −6. Sixteen-bit statistics reduce the overlay from 8347 .85 to 7220 .71 MiB with comparable PPL. Increasing K from 1 to 4 reduces PPL from 12 .6102 to 12 .5854 but increases encoding time from about 100 .3 to 175 .6 s. Other tested settings have smaller or model-dependent effects. No Llama-3-8B vector-dimension or early-stop-rate ablation is available. These results favor evaluating functional fidelity alongside weight error. 

## 8 Discussion 

Coding, depth, and scope. Our proposed framework combines distortion-selected orthogonal mixing, analytical sign reconstruction, and progressive residual refinement without requiring learned codebooks or CDF estimation. Increasing r trades storage for fidelity: from r = 1 to r = 5, joint-scope PPL drops from 6768 .31 to 32 .16 (GPT-2 Large) and 1 .21 × 10 6 to 12 .73 (Llama-3-8B), versus full-precision 29 .89 and 11 .07. Attention-only coding is less disruptive at r = 1; all scopes approach baseline at r = 5 (Section 6). 

Weight error and output fidelity. Weight MSE does not reliably predict functional quality. At 

r = 5, Llama-3-8B attention-only and joint coding yield MSEs of 2 .247 × 10 −5 and 6 .867 × 10 −6 but PPLs of 11 .90 and 12 .73, respectively; their MSEs cover different parameter sets. GPT-2 Large ablations similarly show AAS benefits despite higher weight error. On Llama-3-8B, AAS raises weight MSE from 3 .379 × 10 −6 to 6 .867 × 10 −6 but lowers logit MSE from 1 .0983 to 0 .6920 and PPL from 13 .9445 to 12 .5854. 

Calibration and operating points. Both models improve with depth but differ in low-depth sensitivity. Llama-3-8B uses AAS β = 0 .5 and four transform candidates. One-factor ablations cover AAS, candidate and calibration counts, statistics precision, block rows, and batch size, but not interactions, vector dimensions, or seed generalization. Lower r and larger vectors favor storage; higher r and smaller vectors favor fidelity. Across both models, r = 3 restores substantial quality and r = 5 approaches baseline; 16-bit statistics further reduce storage. Increasing depth or scope raises storage and reconstruction time. Streaming remains slower than full precision, while overlay size excludes self-contained deployment costs. Cross-scope rates therefore require selected-weight BPW or compression ratios. 14 Baselines and Gaussian motivation. Appendix B compares identity/orthogonal sign coding, scalar quantization, and residual VQ at estimated selected-weight rates. Mixing often improves sign coding, although scalar quantization or VQ can achieve lower PPL at lower BPW; codebook-free coding guarantees neither inference speedup nor universal rate–distortion superiority. Finally, the CLT motivates mixing without assuming Gaussianity: reconstruction uses least-squares-fitted gains, not Gaussian CDF evaluation. 

## 9 Limitations 

AAS relies on limited calibration data, with domain-shift robustness untested. Global residual depth ignores layer-specific sensitivity. Quantization scopes differ in parameter count, making overlay sizes incomparable without normalization and distinct from self-contained deployment storage. Streaming weight reconstruction is slower than full precision, while multi-GPU scaling depends on hardware and transfer overhead. Evaluation is limited to GPT-2 Large and Llama-3-8B, primarily using perplexity and short-horizon fidelity; downstream, long-context, instruction-following, and generative robustness remain untested. Although Llama-3-8B includes scope/depth sweeps and component ablations, vector-dimension, early-stop, repeated-seed, and matched-rate baseline analyses are absent. One-factor ablations omit hyperparameter interactions, and broad hardware-matched PTQ comparisons re-main unavailable. Finally, Gaussian-CLT behavior is a motivation rather than a verified result; orthogonality alone guarantees neither Gaussianity nor independence.
