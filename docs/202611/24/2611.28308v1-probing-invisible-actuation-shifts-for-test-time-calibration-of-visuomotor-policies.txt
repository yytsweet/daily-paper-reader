## Abstract 

Visuomotor policies such as vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world action models (WAMs) are increasingly deployed on robots whose cameras and controllers differ from those used to collect their training data. Such deploy-ment introduces two kinds of shifts. Visual shifts, such as a moved camera, change the observed images; actuation shifts, such as a retuned controller, latency or an added payload, change only how commanded actions translate into robot motion. Invisible to the policy, actuation shifts sharply degrade its success, even under domain random-ization. In this work, we propose PEA, a test-time cali-bration method that identifies actuation shifts through ac-tive probing before task execution. Specifically, before each episode, the robot executes a short probe that moves one arm axis at a time with known commands, so that gain, de-lay and drift leave separable traces. A closed-form iden-tifier estimates them from the response, and PEA compen-sates every subsequent command so that the robot executes the motion the policy intends. The identifier requires no policy training and thus applies to any frozen visuomotor policy. Since visual shifts remain visible to the policy, PEA further adapts the frozen policy with a removable low-rank

## 1. Introduction 

Visuomotor policies, which map camera observations and language instructions to robot actions, now achieve near-saturated success on standard manipulation benchmarks such as LIBERO [34]. This family includes vision-language-action models (VLAs) [4, 21, 22], which fine-tune pretrained vision-language models to predict actions, and world action models (WAMs) [23, 29, 62, 73], which jointly generate future frames and actions. When such a policy is deployed on a different robot or at a different site, two as-pects of the system can change: what the robot observes and how it executes commands. A visual shift , such as a dis-placed camera, as shown in Figure 1(a), alters the observed images. An actuation shift , such as a retuned controller, a control delay or an unmodeled payload, alters only the mapping from commanded actions to the resulting motion; as Figure 1(b) shows, the initial observation is unchanged. An actuation shift is thus invisible to a policy that condi-1arXiv:2611.28308v1 [cs.RO] 20 Nov 2026 tions only on its observations, and the resulting degradation is severe. As shown in Figure 1(c), with the camera un-changed, tripling the controller stiffness reduces the success of the WAM Cosmos-Policy [23] on LIBERO-Spatial from 98% to 15%, and a 30 N end-effector load reduces it to 9%. Domain randomization [43, 56] does not remove this lim-itation, since the observations still contain no information about the current actuation; on shifts absent from training, it even underperforms the released model. Compensating an actuation shift thus requires evidence of the current dynamics. Most adaptive methods infer the dynamics during execution, from the history or execution feedback of the current episode [9, 18, 24, 27, 33, 69] or from a previous failed trial [66]; this evidence arrives only after the policy has acted under the shift and stems from task motion not designed to reveal it. Other methods move the arm randomly before the task, briefly [58] or for about 500 steps [26]; random excitation mixes the effects of axes, de-lays and loads, so that short probes are hard to read and long calibrations degrade nominal performance. In both cases, the evidence is typically read by learned modules that gen-eralize only to the dynamics seen in training. Our key insight is that, since an actuation shift manifests only through motion, the robot should move before it acts, and move in a way that makes the shift easy to identify. We therefore propose PEA (Probe, Estimate, Act), a test-time calibration method for frozen visuomotor policies. Before each episode, the robot executes a short probe in free space that excites one arm axis at a time with zero-sum com-mands. This design separates the unknowns: each axis’s gain appears in its own response, a control delay shifts the response in time, and a constant external force leaves a net displacement. From this response, a closed-form identifier, assembled from classical components [1, 36, 55] and cali-brated once offline, estimates the per-axis gain, control de-lay and drift. PEA then compensates every subsequent com-mand, so that the robot executes the motion the policy in-tends. This identifier requires no policy training and leaves the policy weights unchanged, so it applies to any policy with the same action interface. For visual shifts, which the policy can observe, PEA further trains a lightweight low-rank adapter (LoRA) [15] on the frozen weights, which lifts success under camera changes while largely preserv-ing the policy’s nominal performance. We evaluate PEA on LIBERO with a WAM, Cosmos-Policy, and a VLA, OpenVLA-OFT [22], under coupled camera and gain shifts and under stiffness, payload and latency shifts absent from training. Without any policy training, the identifier of PEA raises the success on LIBERO-Spatial under these unseen shifts from 61.0% to 89.7% on Cosmos-Policy and from 63.5% to 89.0% on OpenVLA-OFT. With the adapter, PEA further raises the success of Cosmos-Policy on LIBERO-Spatial under coupled shifts from 42.8% to 93.0%. PEA is thus policy-agnostic, needs only 30 steps and extends be-yond training dynamics. Our contributions are threefold: • We distinguish visual and actuation shifts by whether a single frame reveals them, and show that actuation shifts, alone or coupled with camera shifts, severely degrade both WAMs and VLAs. • We propose PEA, a test-time calibration method that ad-dresses both shifts: a 30-step probe read in closed form compensates actuation shifts without policy training, and a lightweight adapter handles visual shifts. • Extensive experiments on LIBERO with a WAM and a VLA demonstrate that PEA substantially improves the ro-bustness of both policies to visual and actuation shifts, including dynamics never seen during training. 

## 2. Related Work 

Robustness of VLAs and WAMs. Most robustness benchmarks for VLAs and WAMs perturb what a policy ob-serves or reads [10, 53, 60, 72], or inject action noise [11]. WAMs [7, 16, 23, 29, 62, 73] are reported to be more ro-bust than VLAs to such visual perturbations [68], and ded-icated remedies target camera changes [14, 19, 30, 42]. More generally, a frozen policy can be adapted through prompt tokens [28, 32], feature-wise modulation [44] or low-rank updates [15], possibly generated by a hypernet-work [2, 6, 12, 20, 41, 46, 61]; PEA uses such an adapter for visual shifts. Far fewer works vary the robot itself: LIBERO-RIG [8] changes actuator gain and control rate, where Cosmos-Policy is among the most affected models, and joint-level faults [18] and execution errors [27] also degrade VLAs. We distinguish shifts by whether a single frame reveals them, extend actuation shifts to stiffness, pay-load and latency, couple them with camera shifts, and cor-rect them rather than only measure them. 

Adaptation to dynamics shifts. Adaptive policies update their weights or prompts at test time [17, 25, 31, 35, 64, 65, 67, 71], infer the dynamics from the interaction his-tory [24, 33, 37, 39, 50, 52, 54, 63, 66], or are trained on randomized domains [43, 56], and thus cover only the dy-namics seen in training. Closest to our problem, execution-aware policies [9, 18, 27] and online tracking compensa-tion [69] exploit the gap between commanded and executed motion during the episode. Closest in the evidence used, ICWM [58] keeps clips of random pre-task motion in a VLA’s context, and SPACE [26] fits an affine command adapter on about 500 random calibration steps. Grounded action transformation [13] likewise rewrites the actions of a fixed policy, and real-time chunking [3] compensates infer-ence delay. System identification and active perception. Identify-ing a robot’s dynamics from designed excitation is a long-standing topic in control: optimized excitation makes the parameters of manipulator models identifiable [55], least-squares and kernel methods estimate them from input-output data [36, 48], and certainty-equivalence control acts on the estimates as if they were exact [1]; our identifier as-sembles these components. In robot learning, environment-probing policies [70], active exploration for system identi-fication [38] and learned in-context exploration [49] choose actions that reveal physical parameters, and interactive per-ception more broadly acts in order to perceive [5]. Hand-eye calibration [57] also moves the robot in view of the camera, but to recover geometry rather than dynamics. Whereas these works learn the exploration or the estimator, or calibrate a robot once, PEA calibrates a frozen, image-conditioned and chunked VLA or WAM within a budget of 30 steps per episode, a setting in which we find that the probe’s design largely determines the identification error. 

## 3. Preliminaries 

3.1. Visuomotor policies 

A pretrained policy π maps an observation ot (camera im-ages and proprioception) and a language instruction ℓ to a chunk of H actions (at, . . . , a t+H−1) = π(ot, ℓ ), which is executed open loop before the next observation is taken. Each action at ∈ R7 consists of six end-effector translation and rotation deltas at, 1, . . . , a t, 6 for an operational-space controller (OSC) and a gripper command at, 7. We study two such policies: the WAM Cosmos-Policy [23], built on the Cosmos-Predict2-2B video model [40] with T5 [51] in-struction embeddings, and the VLA OpenVLA-OFT [22]. 

3.2. Visual and actuation shifts 

We separate the system the policy acts in into an observa-tion map , which renders the scene into ot through camera parameters ψ, and an execution map , which turns a com-mand at into the end-effector motion ∆xt through actuation parameters θ such as controller gains, stiffness, latency and external forces. A visual shift changes ψ; an actuation shift 

changes θ. The two differ in what the policy can perceive. A visual shift alters every frame, including the first. An actuation shift leaves the initial observation o0 unchanged, because o0 depends on θ only through motion that has not happened yet; it becomes observable only once the robot moves. A policy that acts from o0 therefore commits to its first action chunk blind to the actuation that executes it. 

3.3. Problem formulation 

Let (ψ0, θ 0) denote the camera and actuation parameters un-der which π was trained. At test time, each episode runs un-der unknown parameters (ψ, θ ) that are constant within the episode and may differ from (ψ0, θ 0) in ψ, in θ, or in both. Our goal is to maximize the task success of π under such shifts, subject to three deployment constraints. (C1) Frozen policy : the pretrained weights of π remain unchanged; only an adapter attached to them may be trained. (C2) Bounded interaction : before each episode, the robot may execute at most K=30 steps in free space. (C3) No test-time tuning :no component is tuned on test shifts; offline training uses only episodes from a training family of shifts, demonstrated by a privileged Teacher (Secs. 4.5 and 5.1). 34. Methodology 

In this section, we introduce PEA, a test-time calibra-tion method that identifies actuation shifts with a short probe and compensates every command accordingly, while a lightweight adapter on the frozen policy handles visual shifts. Specifically, as shown in Figure 2, the actuation cal-ibration proceeds in three steps. First, before each episode, the robot executes a short, fixed probe in free space and records its own motion. Then, a closed-form identifier es-timates the per-axis gain, control delay and drift from this motion. Finally, PEA compensates every command of the policy for the identified actuation, so that the robot acts as the policy intends. In addition, a LoRA adapter conditioned on the same probe adapts the frozen policy to visual shifts.

## 5. Experiments 

We evaluate whether PEA corrects actuation shifts with-out policy training, whether this extends to dynamics ab-sent from training, and whether its adapter handles coupled visual and actuation shifts across policies; we then analyze the design choices behind these results. 

5.1. Experimental setup 

Benchmark and shifts. We evaluate on the LIBERO [34] benchmark with test shifts fixed per configuration, in three groups. Seen types couple per-axis action gains, the grip-per force limit and a camera rotation, drawn from the train-ing family ( gi ∼ U [0 .7, 2.0] , azimuth within ±40 ◦, ele-vation within 25 ◦). Unseen types add a 1.5× zoom-out, object masses ×100 or a narrower field of view. New dy-namics each change one factor that training never varies: OSC stiffness ×0.3 or ×3, a 15 or 30 N downward force on the end effector, or a 1- or 2-step command latency. Ta-ble S1 lists all test configurations, and the appendix further reports seen-type couplings with added latency or payload, shifts that violate Eq. (1) such as cross-axis mixing, and the hardware-setup benchmark LIBERO-RIG [8]. 

Policies and baselines. We adopt the WAM Cosmos-Policy [23] and the released four-suite checkpoint of the VLA OpenVLA-OFT [22] as the visuomotor policies. Adapters are trained once per suite pair (LIBERO-Spatial with LIBERO-Goal, LIBERO-Object with LIBERO-Long) on successful Teacher episodes whose initial states are dis-joint from the test states. Table 1 compares two groups. The first keeps the released policy frozen and places an es-timator in front of it: besides PEA-ID and PEA, Gain-ID reads only the gain, as each axis’s mean probe response rel-ative to nominal; our re-implementations of the evidence of ICWM [58] (a random 30-step probe) and SPACE [26] (500 calibration steps with online LMS) are marked “-style”; and a prefix-token adapter replaces our LoRA. The second serves as a reference that fine-tunes the policy: the base is fine-tuned on intended labels without probe input (FT) and paired with these estimators or an ARX identifier with RLS [36], and PEA-FT trains our adapter jointly with the base. SPACE-style and ARX also calibrate for 500 steps rather than 30. 

Evaluation metric. We report the task success rate (%). Each test configuration is evaluated on 100 episodes per suite (10 tasks × 10 initial states), and each table column averages the configurations of one shift type. Unless noted, results are for Cosmos-Policy; Sec. A gives training details and statistical tests.

Comparison with baselines. Among systems that keep the released weights, PEA-ID outperforms the ICWM-style random probe of the same length (60.5% on LIBERO-Spatial seen types), which excites all axes at once and, as analyzed in Sec. 5.5, identifies the gains less accurately. The SPACE-style calibration runs 500 steps yet reaches only 44.0% on LIBERO-Spatial seen types and lowers nominal success to 74%. On LIBERO-Spatial new dynamics, both baselines also stay below PEA-ID (71.8% and 66.5% vs. 89.7%). Fine-tuning the base behind an estimator does not replace the identifier either: FT + Gain-ID matches or ex-ceeds PEA on seen and unseen types, which contain no la-tency or force, but collapses to 36.8% on LIBERO-Spatial new dynamics, where reading the gain alone fails, and FT + ARX stays below PEA on every column.

5.4. Real-robot experiments 

We deploy PEA on an AgileX PiPER arm with a π0.5 pol-icy [47] fine-tuned on teleoperated demonstrations of plac-ing one cube beside another. With the camera fixed, a 200 g payload on the end effector acts as an invisible actuation shift; we run the full PEA, which probes the arm before each episode, compensates every command and adapts π0.5

with its LoRA adapter while the base weights stay frozen. Over 10 trials per condition, π0.5 succeeds 4 times under the payload and 8 times with PEA; at this sample size, the difference is indicative rather than significant (Fisher’s ex-act test, p=0 .17 ). As Figure 5 shows, the payload lowers the carried cube so that it strikes the target, which PEA pre-vents; Sec. H gives the full setup.

The last two rows of Table 3 zero one probe input of the trained adapter at test time. Without the probe frames, PEA is unchanged on all three shift types (94.5%, 88.3% and 89.5% vs. 93.0%, 87.8% and 90.3%). Probe design sets the identification error. Figure 6 isolates the probe: four 30-step probes are read by the same least-squares identifier on 120 held-out configura-tions. Our axis-wise probe yields 1.8% mean gain error, against 12.9%, 14.3% and 14.8% for Hadamard, ICWM-style and full-rank random probes, which excite all axes at once. Exciting one axis at a time keeps each response free of the others, as Proposition 1 assumes, and lets a per-axis calibration absorb the amplitude nonlinearity that Eq. (1) omits. This carries over to the task: in Table 1, swapping in the equally long ICWM-style probe lowers LIBERO-Spatial seen types from 77.3% (Gain-ID) to 60.5%. 

5.6. Limitations 

PEA compensates the actuation shifts that model (1) de-scribes, namely per-axis gain, delay and drift. Shifts outside this model, such as cross-axis coupling or loads that change within an episode, lie beyond its scope (Sec. G.2); detecting such a mismatch before correcting is a natural extension. Our real-robot study covers one task and one payload, and a broader hardware evaluation is left for future work. 

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented PEA, a test-time calibration method for visuo-motor policies under actuation shifts, which leave the obser-vations unchanged and are therefore invisible to the policy. Before each episode, PEA executes a 30-step probe that ex-cites one arm axis at a time, estimates the per-axis gain, con-trol delay and drift from the response in closed form, and compensates every subsequent command. A LoRA adapter on the frozen policy complements this identifier for visual shifts. On LIBERO with a WAM and a VLA, the identi-fier alone, without policy training, recovers most of the suc-cess lost to actuation shifts, including unseen ones, and the adapter further restores success under coupled shifts. More broadly, when a deployment change cannot be seen, a robot should briefly interact before acting and read that interac-tion with an explicit model. 8Acknowledgements

## B. Implementation details 

Adapter. A probe encoder (26.4M parameters) maps the probe to a code z. A numeric branch (LayerNorm, linear layer, GELU) embeds the per-step 15-d feature 

[at, 100∆ pt, 10 rotvec(∆ Rt), 100∆ qt] (q: finger posi-tions) of the 30 probe steps. An image branch stacks the first of 31 agent-view frames ( 64 ×64 ) with the 30 differ-ences to it and applies four stride-2 convolutions, so it sees how the known probe motion appears from the current cam-era; an MLP fuses the two 1024-d features. The adapted layers of Eq. (5) are the self-attention q, k, v and output pro-jections and both MLP layers of the 28 DiT blocks (168 layers). A zero-initialized linear head generates the spec-trum s(z) ∈ R168 ×14 , so training starts exactly at the re-leased model; the factors Al, B l learn directions shared by all episodes, and each episode’s adaptation is 2,352 num-bers. The adapter has 43.3M parameters, 16.9M besides the encoder. After training, all 715 DiT tensors are bit-identical to the released checkpoint.

Probe protocol. The probe runs after the environment is reset to the episode’s initial state and before the first policy step, with the episode’s shift active, including latency and payload. Identifier. The templates Ti, the calibration ϕi and the drift statistics μi, σ i are computed once from the 800 train-ing probes. Training contains no latency, so the test latencies of 1 and 2 steps lie outside the data the identifier was built from. 

Baseline estimators. Gain-ID divides each axis’s mean signed probe response ri by its mean ¯ri over nominal train-ing probes and maps the ratio to a gain with a per-axis quadratic calibration fitted like ϕi. The ICWM-style probe keeps K=30 steps but consists of five 6-step segments, each along a random unit direction in the 6-D arm action space scaled by 0.5, with the gripper open; the direction seed de-pends on suite, task and initial state. Its gain per axis is the least-squares slope of the axis’s motion on its command, dropping the first two steps of each segment, followed by the same calibration; this reading was the best of the vari-ants we tried on the training probes. Learned-only reads the scripted probe through prefix tokens, with the encoder trained at the base learning rate. 

OpenVLA-OFT. We use the released four-suite check-point [22] with the upstream LIBERO evaluation settings. The identifier and the hold rule are applied exactly as for Cosmos-Policy. Its weights are never updated; the adapter adapts the 224 linear layers of its Llama backbone and is trained per suite pair. 

## C. Baseline details 

C.1. SPACE-style adapter 

To see why the SPACE-style rows lose nominal success, we composed the fitted adapter W with the nominal command-to-displacement map A; under nominal dynamics, the ideal product is the identity. For the inverse regression of SPACE (command on displacement), the diagonal of W A is 0.75– 0.90: displacements lag behind the commands, which atten-uates the regression slope, so the adapter under-commands. Off-diagonal terms reach 0.5 and mix axes that the con-troller keeps separate. Fitting the forward map (displace-ment on command) and inverting it brings the diagonal to 0.95–1.09, but the cross terms remain. SPACE retrains its policy to output displacements, whereas our policies output commands, so these numbers characterize the adapter in our command-space setting, not SPACE as published.

D.3. Superposition and amplitude nonlinearity 

Moving one axis at a time wins because a per-axis cali-bration can then absorb the amplitude nonlinearity of the response. For 40 configurations (gains log-uniform in 

[0 .7, 2] ), we ran each probe, each of its six single-axis com-ponents alone and the probe with all arm commands set to zero, all from the same state. Regressing the response on the measured component responses, which removes any error of the nominal model, gives per-axis gain errors of 9.7% for the Hadamard probe and 11.5% for ours before calibration; our errors concentrate on rx and ry (25 and 37%, transla-tions at most 1%). The controller responds nonlinearly to the amplitude: doubling the command of our probe from 0.5 to 1.0 scales the translation responses by 1.97–2.12 but the rotation responses by only 1.27–1.70. With one axis at a time, each axis’s nonlinearity depends only on its own command, so the per-axis calibration absorbs it (1.8% after calibration, Table S3); with all axes moving, it depends on the other axes’ commands (12.9% for the Hadamard probe).

## F. Why a LoRA adapter: the gain-compensation test 

This section compares the interfaces through which the probe can enter the policy and thereby motivates the LoRA adapter of Sec. 4.5. 

Representational argument. A LoRA with a probe-generated spectrum contains gain compensation as an ex-act special case, whereas prefix tokens and FiLM do not. Compensating gains g means mapping every arm action 

a = W h of a linear read-out to diag(1 /g ) a, whatever the hidden state h. In the family of Eq. (5), a rank-6 update does this exactly: with A the six arm rows of W , B the matching selection and si(z) = 1 /g i − 1, W (z)h = diag(1 /g ) W h 

for all h, and each spectrum entry is a scalar function of one axis’s gain, which the probe determines. Prefix to-kens give an attention output (1 − λ) oc + λ o p, with oc

the output over the content, op a convex combination of the prefix values and λ the attention mass on the prefix [45], so the residual stream moves along directions fixed by z.FiLM [44], h 7 → diag(1 + γ(z)) h + β(z), scales fixed fea-ture coordinates, and W diag(1 + γ) = diag(1 /g ) W has no solution unless the arm read-out aligns with them. Both must approximate the scaling through later nonlinear layers. Cosmos-Policy decodes its action from denoised latents, so the construction is not exact there; the argument concerns what each interface can represent.

## G. Additional results 

G.1. New dynamics 

Figure S4 shows the six new dynamics for the released model and the identifier variants, and Figure S5 gives ev-ery system of Table 1 per configuration. Reading only the gain (Gain-ID) fails under latency, because each push’s re-sponse lands on probe steps of the opposite sign; reading the delay restores these configurations, and the drift term lifts the payloads. Among the frozen baselines, the ICWM-style random probe fails under the 30 N payload (1% and 19%), which its gain reading does not account for. 

G.2. Combined shifts, extrapolation and model mis-match 

Table S7 adds latency, a payload or both to seen-type cou-plings. The identifier and the adapter compose: PEA-ID handles the actuation part, and the adapter lifts the config-urations with the largest camera changes (c2 and c4 build on s4 and s3). Table S8 pushes the seen types beyond the training range (x1–x4); since the identifier reads the gains from the probe rather than from training data, PEA extends well beyond the training gains. Four further shifts violate model (1): m1 rotates the x/y translation commands by 30 ◦

(cross-axis mixing), m2 executes half of each command one step and half two steps late, m3 removes 0.15 from every arm command’s magnitude (dead zone), and m4 ramps a downward force from 0 to 30 N during the task. The identi-fier reads each as the nearest shift of its model, e.g ., m3 as gains of about 0.7. This helps under the dead zone, is neu-tral under the fractional delay and the force ramp, and hurts under cross-axis mixing, which no per-axis model captures.

G.6. Evidence and estimator ablations 

Table S11 varies, on one model family and dataset, which evidence the policy receives and how it is read. With an estimator on the FT base, the scripted probe read by the gain-only estimator outperforms the random probe, the 500-step calibration and the ARX identification, and a learned probe encoder adds little on top of it. Stacking the gain di-vision on a policy that was trained on compensated labels compensates the gain twice and loses 15–20 points on seen types, which is why the adapter trains on intended labels (Sec. 4.5). Without an estimator, in-episode history, an ear-lier episode and a random probe all stay near DR-only, and only the scripted probe helps. 

G.7. LIBERO-RIG 

On the four overlapping axes of LIBERO-RIG [8] (actua-tor gain, field of view, camera rotation and camera position, levels 3 and 5), PEA raises the mean success of Cosmos-Policy from 81.0% to 89.3% on LIBERO-Spatial and from 77.0% to 80.6% on LIBERO-Goal, and Gain-ID reaches 85.1% and 78.6% (Figure S12). The gain axis improves most; our Original values are within 11 points of those re-ported by LIBERO-RIG on every configuration. 

## H. Real-robot experiments 

Setup. We use an AgileX PiPER arm (six joints and a parallel gripper) with a fixed top-down camera and a wrist camera. The policy is π0.5 [47], fine-tuned on teleoperated demonstrations of placing one cube beside another. The ac-tuation shift is a 200 g payload attached to the end effector; camera and scene are unchanged, so the shift is invisible in the first frame. We run the full PEA: it probes the arm before each episode, compensates every command and adapts π0.5

with its LoRA adapter; the base weights stay frozen. The adapter is trained on episodes that we collected ourselves on this arm, separate from the test trials. 

Results. Over 10 trials per condition, π0.5 succeeds in 4 of 10 trials under the payload and PEA in 8 of 10 (95% Wilson intervals 17–69% and 49–94%; Fisher’s exact test, two-sided p=0 .17 ). Figure 5 in the main paper shows one rollout per condition. In these rollouts, the end effector car-ries the cube at a mean height of 19.4 cm without payload, 17.0 cm with the payload and 19.0 cm with the payload and PEA: the payload lowers the cube so that it strikes the tar-get, and PEA restores the nominal height.
