Abstract
Vision–language–action foundation models learn gen-
eral manipulation priors, but object mass and local
slip are difficult to infer from vision alone. Chang-
ing loads require contact-driven grasp adjustments
that demonstrations collected under fixed conditions
may not adequately cover. We present VTLA-RL
to study visuotactile grasping policies and their rein-
forcement learning (RL) post-training under varying
loads. Our main approach shares a visual encoder
between tactile-image histories and RGB inputs to
jointly generate and execute motion and bilateral
force targets in a unified action space. Mixed real
and simulated demonstrations first establish motion
and contact priors. Two-stage multi-task RL then
uses only simulated interaction to optimize the policy
through task-success returns without continued real-
demonstration supervision, and the trained policy
deploys directly to real robots.
We compare tac-
tile representations and RL post-training routes in
simulation and separately evaluate load variation
and placement perturbations on hardware. The full
method achieves higher grasping success than the su-
pervised initialization, while RL post-training gains
differ across approaches, providing empirical evidence
for choosing representations and training approaches
for this task.

Introduction
Moving a beverage carton from a table to a target
area requires semantic understanding, geometric lo-
calization, and contact control. Vision can identify
the target and plan motion, but has difficulty distin-
guishing visually identical containers with different
internal masses before contact. Load changes alter
the contact conditions required for stable grasping,
and an initial grasp may slip during lifting or trans-
port. To complete transport under varying loads, the
policy must adjust its grasp pose, motion, and grip
force based on the current contact state.
Vision–language–action (VLA) models provide
transferable action priors; touch reveals contact de-
formation, slip tendencies, and force changes. Adding
touch does not directly ensure that a policy can use
this information to adjust grasps under changing
loads.
Imitation learning mainly inherits demon-
strated motion and force habits, while demonstra-
tions collected under fixed conditions may not cover
new contact states. Simulated interaction offers a
resettable training environment for expanding this
experience and reduces the risks of drops, collisions,
and material damage during hardware exploration.
We present VTLA-RL (Fig. 1) to study visuotactile
grasp adaptation under varying loads and the effects
of tactile representations and reinforcement learning
(RL) post-training routes. Without additional tactile
pretraining, our main approach shares a pretrained
visual encoder between tactile-image histories and
RGB inputs, and uses one action expert to jointly
generate motion and bilateral force targets. Super-
vised fine-tuning (SFT) on mixed real and simulated
demonstrations establishes motion and contact priors.
Two-stage multi-task RL in simulation then intro-
duces placement and mass randomization in sequence
to optimize the unified motion–force policy through
task-success returns. The policy deploys directly to
real robots through a hybrid force–position controller.
Starting from real and simulated demonstrations at
fixed placements and masses, simulation RL expands
interaction experience through placement and mass
randomization. We evaluate grasping on hardware
under placement perturbations and varying loads.
Comparisons and ablations show higher success than
the supervised initialization, with improved position
generalization and load adaptation. Hardware results
also show that the current tactile-array approach
performs better at SFT initialization, whereas the
image-based approach achieves larger post-training
gains.
Our contributions are twofold:
• A visuotactile policy for load-adaptive
grasping. We combine tactile history, a shared
visual encoder, and a unified motion–force action
interface with mixed-demonstration initialization
and simulation RL to improve grasping without
test-object mass labels. Post-training requires no

Related work
2.1
Tactile
representations
and
contact-aware foundation policies
Sparsh learns tactile features across sensors through
self-supervision [10]; AnyTouch and AnyTouch 2 learn
general representations of static/dynamic and force-
related observations, respectively [7, 8]. TaF-VLA
grounds tactile features in physical forces through
tactile–force alignment [12]. These approaches im-
prove data coverage, sensor adaptation, and physical
representation, but representation quality alone does
not determine how much grip force a policy chooses
in successful manipulation.
VTLA, Tactile-VLA, and VLA-Touch extend vi-
sual policies through preference learning, physical
knowledge, and dual-level feedback [2, 11, 36]. Om-
niVTLA handles heterogeneous tactile inputs [5];
ForceVLA and ForceVLA2 emphasize force feedback,
action generation, and closed-loop control [18, 34];
AT-VLA studies the timing and location of tactile
injection [16]. DreamTacVLA and OmniVTA con-
nect tactile prediction to contact actions [32, 42].
FTP-1 advances multi-source sensor pretraining [35];
N0-VTLA further uses large-scale tactile data and
ALTER advantage-conditioned offline policy improve-
ment [20]. ViTaL combines local visuotactile skills
with residual reinforcement learning [41]; TouchGuide
uses touch to guide actions at inference [38]; ViTaR
selects and scales bounded residuals over a frozen
VLA [29]; TouchWorld combines predicted tactile
goals with high-frequency residual correction [43].
HapticVLA uses safety-aware reward-weighted flow
matching to penalize excessive grip force and distills
tactile knowledge for deployment without tactile sen-
sors [9]. VTLA-RL uses a Tabero-style hybrid force–
position interface [30], jointly generates motion and
bilateral force targets, and optimizes them through
simulation RL. With tactile feedback retained at de-
ployment, it focuses on adapting motion and force to
changing loads without object-mass labels.
2.2
Tactile simulation and evaluation
TACTO, Taxim, and FOTS generate optical tactile
observations [24, 28, 40]; TacSL incorporates tac-
tile images and contact-force distributions into GPU
learning [1]; TacEx integrates soft-body contact and
optical models through a modular framework [21].
DiffTactile supports differentiable contact and pa-
rameter calibration [25], Taccel targets high-fidelity
contact with GPU parallelism [17], TaCauchy pro-
vides continuum stress-based tactile simulation [39],
and TactileGenesis studies multiple tactile abstrac-
tions and their effects on policy learning [6]. Rich con-
tact models reduce data cost, but introduce material
parameters, numerical solving, and sensor-statistics
matching.
UniVTAC unifies multisensor simulation, tactile
representation, and manipulation benchmarks [3];
Tacmap reduces the domain gap through geomet-
ric contact representations [26]. SoftVTBench sepa-
rates task completion from object deformation and
2


uses evaluator-only finite-element states to construct
deformation-aware success, showing that contact qual-
ity requires independent evaluation [14]. VTLA-RL
aligns optical tactile observations and motion–force
control interfaces across simulation and hardware,
then evaluates adaptation and transfer through grasp-
ing success under varying loads and placement per-
turbations.
2.3
Reinforcement learning for VLA
policies
PiRL (πRL) [4] uses proximal policy optimization
(PPO) to fine-tune the action expert of flow-based
VLA policies. RL Token (RLT) [31] learns a com-
pact readout representation, then freezes the VLA
and representation while training a lightweight actor–
critic. SimpleVLA-RL [15] optimizes action-token
VLA policies through RL, combining parallel trajec-
tory sampling and exploration strategies to improve
task performance. DSRL freezes the generative policy,
treats the initial noise as a learnable action, and op-
timizes the noise input through RL [27]. VTLA-RL’s
RL framework builds on PiRL and RLinf [33] and fol-
lows Hebero [37] for success-rate-weighted multi-task
joint optimization.
TacCoRL [19] injects normal-taxel histories into
the VLA context and action expert through a dedi-
cated encoder and contact gating, combining mixed-
demonstration initialization with simulation PPO to
improve recovery and completion in contact-rich tasks.
Its main RL approach retains real-demonstration
supervision. VTLA-RL focuses on grasping under
changing loads, primarily uses tactile-image histo-
ries with the shared RGB encoder, and executes
jointly generated motion and bilateral force targets
through a Tabero-style hybrid force–position inter-
face [30]. After mixed-demonstration initialization,
RL post-training uses only simulated interaction
and task-success returns, without continued real-
demonstration supervision.
We evaluate grasping
adaptation under varying loads and placement per-
turbations.
3
Method
3.1
VTLA
We use a π0.5 backbone [13] with two 224 × 224 RGB
views. TacImage concatenates eight tactile frames per
finger, including the current frame, crops and down-
samples to the RGB resolution, and shares the pre-
trained RGB vision transformer (ViT) without a sep-
arate tactile encoder. TacField shares TacCoRL’s [19]
idea of encoding tactile-array histories as tokens; our
causal temporal convolutional network (TCN) maps a
reference frame and eight history frames of 2D marker
arrays to one prefix token.
Each effective action has 13 dimensions:
end-
effector position (3), rotation vector (3), gripper (1),
and contact-force targets (3 per finger: one normal
and two tangential components, in newtons in its
sensor frame). One flow action expert jointly gen-
erates motion and force targets, unnormalized for
execution by a Tabero-style hybrid force–position
controller [30]. Force targets receive demonstration
supervision during initialization and remain action
variables optimized by subsequent simulation RL.
SFT initialization follows π0.5’s flow-matching
training [13], jointly supervising motion and bilateral
force targets in a unified action space to establish
motion and contact priors. Normalized force coordi-
nates receive separate loss weights to balance training
scales; the appendix specifies the loss, weights, and
trainable parameters. Figure 2 shows the architec-
ture.
3.2
Sim-Real alignment
Real and simulated scenes use corresponding geom-
etry, cameras, and robots. Teleoperation supplies
real demonstrations, and Tabero-style replay supplies
simulated data. Three-dimensional Gaussian splat-
ting (3DGS) reconstruction reduces visual differences.
Figure 3 shows visual and tactile alignment during
grasping.
We combine Taxim [24] and FOTS [40] in one
sensor interface, with simulation assets built and ad-
justed for Xense sensors. Both use a shared contact
representation from depth beneath the tactile pad.
Taxim maps local surface geometry to tactile RGB im-
ages; FOTS combines contact deformation, centroid
changes, and relative sensor–object yaw to produce
image-coordinate marker displacements. These com-
plementary observations of the same contact state
consistently support tactile-image and array-history
inputs.
Cross-domain calibration covers tactile observa-
tions and force control. We use real tactile calibra-
tion data to adjust the force-observation mapping.
Across domains, we align image resolution, channel
order, preprocessing, update timing, marker index-
ing, reference state, coordinates, history windows,
and displacement scale. End-effector poses, gripper
commands, and bilateral force targets share coordi-
nate frames, units, and corresponding hybrid force–
position execution interfaces, giving the actions in
Sec. 3.1 consistent physical meanings across domains.
We mix simulated and real demonstrations at a
fixed 1:1 ratio using the SFT setup in Sec. 3.1 to
initialize motion–contact priors. Both subsequent
RL stages use only simulation rollouts and task-
success returns, without real-demonstration super-
vision (Sec. 3.3).
The policy deploys directly to
real robots through the same observation/control
interfaces, without additional fine-tuning, online real-
robot RL, or test-object mass/friction labels.

3.3
Multi-stage multi-task joint train-
ing
Building on RLinf and PiRL [4, 33], we start from
the mixed-data SFT checkpoint and use PPO [23]
with generalized advantage estimation (GAE) [22]
to update action-expert low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
parameters θ and value-head parameters ϕ.
The
remaining backbone and tactile encoder stay frozen.
Multi-stage training.
Stage 1 optimizes grasping
and placement under randomized positions. Stage 2
resumes from this checkpoint and further randomizes
object mass and friction, expanding interaction expe-
rience under varying loads and optimizing grasping
behavior through task-success returns. Both stages
share the policy, value head, and action interface,
using sparse task-success reward: 1 on success and 0
otherwise.
Joint multi-task optimization.
Tasks and their
language instructions share one policy and value head.
Following Hebero [37], we adjust update contributions
using task success. Let si be the exponential moving
average (EMA) of task i’s completed-episode success
rate, with decay 0.9, and ¯s its equally weighted mean
across tasks. The raw task weight is
ωi = ωmin + (ωmax −ωmin)σ(κ(¯s −si)) .
(1)
Here σ(x) = 1/(1 + e−x), ωmin = 0.5, ωmax = 2.0,
and κ = 10; lower-success tasks receive larger weights.
Raw weights are updated after each rollout and fixed
during that round’s optimization.
Tasks with no
completed episodes in the current round retain their
previous success-rate EMA; all task weights remain
one until every task is initialized. Let B contain a
global optimizer batch’s valid action-chunk samples.
Each sample receives its task’s raw weight, and these
weights are normalized within the batch so that the
sample weights ˆωk have mean one.
Model optimization workflow.
Each round syn-
chronizes training and inference parameters and col-
lects up to 300 primitive steps per environment (30
fixed-length chunks). After aggregating success rates
and updating task weights, we compute and fix GAE
advantages and value targets from chunk rewards
and old value estimates, then traverse valid samples
in batches for one PPO epoch. Resets for the next
round are prefetched during updating.

3.4
Policy deployment
Our custom hybrid force–position gripper reaches ar-
bitrary openings at 20 Hz. A low-level controller fol-
lowing Tabero and Tactile-VLA tracks active targets
using measured tactile force between policy updates.
Synchronous and asynchronous execution both use
the first 10 actions of each prediction at a 20-Hz
base rate. Four interpolation points between targets
yield 100-Hz commands. Model inference and arm
execution run asynchronously, with tactile feedback
correction at 20 Hz.
New chunks blend with existing fused targets us-
ing weights increasing linearly from 0.2 to 0.8 over
three base steps; old chunks receive complementary
weights. Rotations interpolate in local rotation-vector
coordinates; other coordinates, including the grip-
per target, interpolate linearly. Recomputing fusion
from original overlapping chunks after each admission
avoids weight accumulation. We call this continuous-
inference system with 20-Hz tactile feedback FAST
and enable it by default.

Hardware and data sources.
Training uses eight
RTX PRO 6000D GPUs. Stage 0 performs SFT; the
supervision data combine teleoperated real demon-
strations with simulator replays of the same demon-
strations, with 30 real trajectories and 30 simulated
replay trajectories per task. RL post-training uses
128 parallel simulated environments, evenly split be-
tween the two tasks. Each of the two training stages
runs for 200 epochs. Real evaluation uses an xArm
with a tactile hybrid force–position gripper. Both real
and simulated evaluation use the same RTX 4090.
Success criterion.
Simulation success requires the
object to remain inside the target container’s goal
region for eight consecutive control steps, subject to
planar and height error requirements; the specific
parameters are provided in the appendix. Episodes
terminate after 300 control steps. Contact force does
not participate in success. The real-robot criterion is
essentially the same as in simulation.

Weight-adaptive grasping under
varying loads

initialized with the same π0.5 pretrained parameters.
Gentle and Firm undergo SFT following Tabero using
demonstrations with different force commands, where

image representation of Sec. 3.1. Tables 1 and 2 re-

matches or exceeds Firm in some tiers, showing that
stronger force commands do not always yield higher

4.2
Method comparison
We compare three RL post-training routes under
the adaptive setting. VTLA-RL is the full method.
VTLA-RL(Img) uses TacImage and matches Adap-
tive in Sec. 4.1. VTLA-RL(Field) uses tactile-array
signals; its marker inputs and prefix-only TCN are
specified in Sec. 3.1. VTLA-RLT corresponds to the
RL Token route [31]: it freezes the VLA backbone and
encoder, compresses prefix features into RL tokens,
and trains a lightweight actor and value networks.
VTLA-DSRL freezes the generator, treats the initial
noise as a learnable action, and optimizes the noise
input through RL [27]. All methods use the same
observation access, initialization data, two-stage RL
configuration, reward, task weighting, execution rate,
and primitive interaction budget; Table 4 reports suc-
cess rates in simulation for each method on the two
tasks and three load tiers. Real-robot comparison
results are reported in Sec. 4.4.
Equally averaging the six task–tier conditions in Ta-
ble 4, VTLA-RL, VTLA-RLT, and VTLA-DSRL with
Img achieve approximately 96.7%, 94.0%, and 93.3%
success, respectively. VTLA-RL(Img) has the highest
overall mean and balanced performance across tasks.
VTLA-RLT(Img) performs more consistently than
VTLA-RL(Img) on Task A but trails it on Task B.
Differences are larger with Field: VTLA-RLT(Field)
performs well on Task A but poorly on Task B, while
VTLA-RL(Field) and VTLA-DSRL(Field) also de-
cline markedly at Task A’s T3 tier. These results
indicate that representation and post-training choices
should be evaluated in relation to the task and load
conditions in this setting. Section 4.3 further com-
pares RL post-training and execution, and Sec. 4.4
reports hardware results.

5
Conclusion
We presented VTLA-RL to study visuotactile grasp-
ing policies and their post-training under varying
loads. The method combines mixed-demonstration
initialization, a shared visual encoder, and a unified
motion–force policy, followed by RL post-training
through simulated interaction and direct real-robot
deployment.
Comparisons across tactile represen-
tations and RL methods in simulation and on real
hardware, together with component ablations, demon-
strate effective grasping under randomized place-
ments and varying object masses.
Limitations
The current method is evaluated only on small-scale
data and tests, and the hardware experiments provide
limited evidence for distinguishing small performance
differences. Future work will expand task diversity
and evaluation scale, reduce dependence on initializa-
tion, and improve feedback timeliness.

Gripper and Force Interface
The platform combines an xArm 7, a custom single-
actuator parallel gripper driven by a DM4310 motor,
two Xense optical tactile sensors, and external/wrist
RGB-D cameras. Gripper travel is 0–0.045 m, with
a tool-center-point (TCP) offset of 0.232 m along
link7’s z axis. Each pad measures 17.5 × 29.3 × 2
mm and provides a 400 × 700 tactile image with an
11 × 20 marker grid.
Force targets and measurements use aligned local
contact coordinates: x points up, y forward, and z
along the closing axis, with the two fingers’ z axes
pointing in the same direction. Real forces are esti-
mated by Xense from pad deformation. Simulated
forces come from contact sensors and are transformed
from world to fingertip coordinates; Taxim/FOTS
supply tactile observations.
After unnormalization, the first six action coordi-
nates add back the inference-time state to recover
absolute TCP position and rotation vector. Simu-
lation uses differential inverse kinematics; hardware
converts TCP targets to flange poses before sending
them through the robot SDK. The gripper coordi-
nate sets opening, and the final six coordinates specify
bilateral force targets.

E
Tactile Input Specifications
TacImage.
Eight frames per finger, including the
current frame, are collected at 20 Hz. Each image
is resized to 224 × 224 and then to a 56 × 56 tile,
without cropping or background subtraction. A 4 × 4
grid places left-finger frames in the left half and right-
finger frames in the right half, ordered from oldest
to newest in row-major order within each half. The
resulting 224×224 RGB image is normalized to [−1, 1]
and shares the camera SigLIP encoder. With patch
size 14, it contributes 256 visual tokens.
TacField.
The input contains one fixed reference
frame and eight recent frames of absolute marker
coordinates, shaped 9 × 440 × 2. The first 220 mark-
ers belong to the left finger and the remainder to
the right, following fixed grid indices. Coordinates
remain in pixels, without reference subtraction or
additional normalization. Both representations re-
peat the earliest frame when fewer than eight history
frames are available.
TacField uses two residual causal convolution
blocks with kernel size 3, dilation 1, hidden dimension
4096, and SiLU activation, followed by a projection
to 2048 dimensions.
It has approximately 73.2M
parameters and emits one token appended to the
image/language prefix. Only the final time-step out-
put is retained: its five-frame receptive field uses the
five most recent frames, so the reference and earliest
three history frames do not affect the output. The
encoder is trained during SFT and frozen during RL.
TacField shares TacCoRL’s history-to-token idea, but
uses marker coordinates and one prefix token, rather
than taxel readings with tokens injected into both
prefix and suffix.

The external camera is located at (0.6993, 0, 1.5702)

Taxim and FOTS share a tactile-pad height map
from an internal depth camera.

RGB images; FOTS combines the height map and

FAST Timing and Ablation
Definition
FAST runs observation collection, inference, and ex-
ecution concurrently. Tactile history updates at 20
Hz.
Predictions align to observation timestamps,
and elapsed actions are skipped. Four interpolation
points between base targets yield 100 Hz hardware
commands. The force correction is applied after inter-
polation and removed from the action written back
to the buffer, preventing accumulation.
Overlapping chunks are fused from oldest to newest.
A new chunk’s weight increases linearly from 0.2 to
0.8 over three base steps, with complementary weight
on the previous result.
Translation, gripper, and
force interpolate linearly; rotation interpolates in lo-
cal rotation-vector coordinates. Fusion is recomputed
from original chunks after each admission.
Asynchronous simulation retains all 50 predicted
actions (2.5 s) and requires admitted observation
timestamps to be at least 0.2 s apart.
Hardware
retains the first 10 actions (0.5 s), admits returned
predictions immediately, and permits one inference
in flight. Its first chunk starts at arrival time. When
waiting for a target, simulation pauses, with a 30 s
timeout; hardware holds its last command, suspends
force correction and history updates, and stops after
2 s. Fully expired hardware targets cause immediate
stopping and are not replayed.
For TacImage PiRL at round 400, 40,594 simulation
inference events give median/P95 wall-clock round-
trip times of 0.353/0.953 s.
The mean admitted-
observation interval is 0.213 s in simulation time,
approximately 4.7 Hz.
These shared-service mea-
surements distinguish wall-clock inference time from
simulation-time refresh rate.
The w/o FAST control uses synchronous inference
followed by ten open-loop actions. Simulation pauses
during inference and refreshes the policy every 0.5 s;
both execution modes retain the same 60 Hz force-
feedback controller. Hardware w/o FAST also uses
synchronous ten-action prefixes.
