# Abstract 

Deploying large vision-language-action (VLA) models on resource-constrained robots demands extreme compression, yet existing post-training quantization (PTQ) methods fre-quently collapse at ultra-low bit-widths ( ≤ 2 bits). We trace this failure to two mismatches in standard pipelines. First, calibrating on static offline demonstrations induces distri-bution shift : minor quantization perturbations drive the policy into unseen states where uncalibrated errors com-pound. Second, minimizing the layer-wise weight recon-struction error is a flawed proxy , as action generation de-pends only on the representations consumed by the action head. We present LoopQ , an on-policy ultra-low-bit quan-tization framework. LoopQ closes the loop by calibrat-ing on states from the quantized policy’s own rollouts. It then adds readout-aligned fine-tuning , which freezes the trellis codes and optimizes only lightweight lookup tables and scaling factors to align the backbone readout with the full-precision teacher. With all linear layers at 2 bits, LoopQ achieves a 60.4% success rate on LIBERO-Plus with GR00T N1.7, close to the 61.8% of full precision. Even at 1 bit per weight, π0.5 still completes 80.0% of LIBERO tasks. With 8-bit activations, the 2-bit π0.5 runs on integer kernels on an embedded NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor , with 

4.1 × smaller weights and 44% less memory. On a real Ag-ileX Piper arm, it stays within one success of full precision on each of three tasks. Code and checkpoints are available at § github.com/ForeverBlue816/LoopQ .

# 1. Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models [3–5, 17, 27] have become the standard recipe for generalist robot policies: a pretrained vision-language backbone, a flow-matching or diffusion transformer action head [9, 20], and several bil-lion parameters evaluated many times per second on on-board hardware. While language models of this scale are routinely compressed to 2 bits per weight with modest loss [10, 30, 31], VLAs resist such extreme compression. Existing VLA quantizers stop at 4 bits or keep the action head in higher precision [1, 36, 37]; applying the strongest 2-bit quantizers across an entire VLA drops perturbed suc-cess by 4 to 62 points. Figure 1a illustrates this failure: from the same initial state and noise, the full-precision pol-icy opens the drawer, whereas its 2-bit counterpart reaches for the upper drawer and stalls. Why does a recipe that works for language models break a policy? Because a policy acts in closed loop : its outputs determine its next inputs. This single fact invalidates two core assumptions behind standard post-training quantizers, explaining both why they fail and how to fix them. 

Quantization drift. Layer-wise quantizers minimize output error on static calibration data (human demonstra-tion frames), pushing the unavoidable ultra-low-bit error into input directions that those frames never excite. While a language model rarely encounters this hidden error be-cause its test and calibration distributions align, a closed-loop robot inevitably does: a 2-bit policy acts slightly differ-ently from full precision, enters an unseen state, triggers the error hidden there, and deviates further. We term this self-reinforcing divergence quantization drift . Figure 1b traces it in a real episode, where the 2-bit end-effector leaves the ref-erence path and never recovers. Across every VLA module, policy-induced states dominate the input distribution shift at deployment, accounting for 45% in the language model and up to 83% in the action head. 

Readout corruption. Because the action head inter-acts with the backbone solely through attention readouts over backbone tokens, action generation depends not on raw weights, but on what the head reads. Closed-loop calibration quan-tizes every layer on the states the quantized policy itself visits, so the quantizer can no longer hide error where the policy will go; it requires a few hundred rollouts and no training. Readout-aligned fine-tuning then updates only a 512-entry lookup table and a scale vector per layer around the frozen codes, aligning the action head’s readouts with the full-precision model. Neither step alters the storage for-mat, bit budget, or decoder. The result is a VLA quan-tized end to end, across both backbone and action head, that behaves like the original (Fig. 1d). Without any train-ing, closed-loop calibration alone outperforms the strongest post-training quantizer on LIBERO-Plus, reaching 73.4% vs. 72.6% on π0.5 and 57.5% vs. 55.9% on GR00T N1.7. Adding readout alignment brings the 2-bit policies to 74.9% and 60.4%, within 1.6 and 1.4 points of full precision, re-covering three quarters of the degradation incurred by the best existing 2-bit quantizer on GR00T. Nor does our ap-proach stop at 2 bits: at 1 bit per weight, π0.5 still com-pletes 80.0% of LIBERO, far surpassing the 48.0% reported for 2-bit backbone-only quantization [1]. Finally, the com-pressed policy is directly deployable: with 8-bit activations, the 2-bit π0.5 executes on integer kernels on an embedded NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor with 4.1× smaller weights and 44% less memory (Fig. 1e). On a real AgileX Piper arm, the 2-bit policy succeeds in 58 of 75 trials, against 59 for full precision. Our contributions are: • A closed-loop diagnosis. We show that ultra-low-bit VLAs fail due to two phenomena rooted in closed-loop execution: quantization drift , caused by calibration statis-tics that miss policy-induced states, and readout corrup-tion , a systematic displacement of the representations consumed by the action head under layer-wise objectives. • LoopQ. We propose closed-loop calibration to align cal-ibration statistics with the policy’s own state distribu-tion, outperforming the strongest post-training quantiz-ers on two VLAs without any training, followed by 2readout-aligned fine-tuning , which optimizes only per-layer lookup tables and scales while keeping all stored codes and the decoder untouched. • End-to-end VLAs at 2 bits and below. We present the first VLAs with both backbone and action head quan-tized entirely to 2 bits that stay within two points of full precision under perturbation, while remaining functional policies even at 1 bit. Furthermore, we deploy the 2-bit policy on a physical AgileX Piper arm, demonstrat-ing reliable closed-loop execution across three real-world manipulation tasks: precision stacking, two-stage lidded-bin lemon placement, and multi-object grasping in clutter, each within one success of full precision. 

# 2. Related Work 

Vision-language-action models. RT-2 [5] and Open-VLA [17] cast actions as tokens of a vision-language model; 

π0 and π0.5 [4, 27] and the GR00T family [3] attach a flow-matching action head that reads the backbone through atten-tion and denoises an action chunk in a few steps. We study the latter family, where the interface between backbone and head is an attention readout. 

Low-bit quantization of large models. GPTQ [12] and AWQ [19] quantize layer by layer using second-order statis-tics of the layer inputs, following the optimal-brain line of work [15]. At two bits, scalar quantizers collapse and vec-tor formats take over: QuIP and QuIP# [6, 30] combine in-coherence processing with lattice codebooks, AQLM [10] and VPTQ [22] learn additive and vector codebooks, and QTIP [31] uses trellis-coded quantization [25] to approach the rate–distortion limit with a decoder that needs only a small lookup table. Rotations reshape weight and acti-vation statistics before quantization [2, 7, 23], and fine-tuning or distillation repairs the continuous parameters af-terwards [8, 10, 24, 30]. We adopt the trellis-coded format and contribute what is specific to policies: the calibration statistics and the fine-tuning objective. 

Quantizing VLAs. QVLA [36], QuantVLA [37], Ac-tQuant [1], and DA-PTQ [35] adapt bit allocation, scaling, or calibration to action sensitivity, mostly at four bits or with parts of the action pathway kept in higher precision. BitVLA [32] instead train the policy with quantization in the loop. VLAQuantBench [34] shows that a single action-interface layer can dominate the loss. These works calibrate on demonstrations; we quantize every linear layer to two bits and identify the closed loop as the root cause of failure. 

Calibration data and distribution shift. Post-training quantization is sensitive to its calibration set [33]. In imita-tion learning, DAgger [29] corrects the mismatch between demonstrations and learner-induced states by querying an expert and retraining. Closed-loop calibration needs nei-ther labels nor training: it re-estimates layer statistics on the states the quantized policy itself visits.

3.2. Quantization drift under closed-loop execution 

Equation (2) weights the reconstruction error by Hessian calibration Hℓ. Because errors along low-energy directions of Hℓ incur negligible penalty, an ultra-low-bit quantizer in-evitably hides its unavoidable distortion in those subspaces. For y = w1x1 +w2x2, the output error is ∆w1x1 +∆ w2x2:if x2 ≈ 0 on the demonstration set D, ∆w2 is unconstrained and w2 is quantized coarsely. This is harmless only when 

x2 is near zero. Because a policy’s actions alter the future scene, small quantization perturbations steer a 2-bit policy into states where x2̸ = 0 , exposing the hidden error ∆w2x2,amplifying the deviation, and closing a self-reinforcing loop [28]. To quantify this effect, we decompose the second layer-wise moment of the deployment input shift relative to calibration frames across three sources: photometric vari-ations, geometric variations, and states visited by the 2-bit policy itself. Across the entire VLA, policy-induced states dominate the distribution shift, accounting for 45% in the language model, 51% in the vision encoder, 48–57% in the action head’s attention layers, and up to 83% in its remain-ing layers (Fig. 2a). To isolate this mismatch, we change only the calibra-tion statistics so that they include on-policy states (Sec. 4) and keep everything else fixed (Fig. 2b). The output er-ror on on-policy states then falls in 427 of the 432 layers, most strongly in the action head (median 14%, up to 50%) and least in the backbone (median 3%). 3.3. Readout corruption in the action head 

Calibration statistics determine where weight error is placed, and the second failure cause lies in the layer-wise objective itself, which leaves the readout of the action head unconstrained [13, 16]. To measure the effect, we feed the same episode, with identical inputs, to the full-precision 

π0.5 and to its 2-bit versions and record the attention readout of the action expert at each of the 19 policy calls (Fig. 2c). Quantization to 2 bits displaces the readout by a nearly con-stant offset at every policy call. The plot axes are the two leading directions of both models’ errors: the base error lies along the first, and LoopQ removes it, leaving a residual half as large along the second. LoopQ is closer to full precision at all 19 calls (0.51 of the base distance on average). The trellis format and closed-loop calibration bring it to 0.63, and readout alignment accounts for the rest. 

# 4. Method 

LoopQ keeps the quantization format fixed and changes two things around it: the statistics that guide the quantizer and the objective that repairs its output (Fig. 3). 

4.1. Base format: trellis-coded quantization 

We quantize every linear layer of the prefix and the ac-tion head with trellis-coded quantization [25] in the bitshift formulation of QTIP [31]. Each weight matrix undergoes a two-sided randomized Hadamard transform and is parti-tioned into 256-weight tiles. Each tile is encoded as a 512-bit path through a 216 state trellis: the decoder slides a 16-bit window over the bitstream in 4-bit strides, hashes the window to index a 512-entry lookup table Tℓ, and outputs a weight pair scaled by a per-row factor sℓ. Viterbi search with block-wise error feedback selects the path minimiz-ing Eq. (2). Because adjacent windows overlap, all weights within a tile are optimized jointly, allowing a compact 512-pair table to approach the 2-bit rate–distortion limit [14]. 4Format: trellis-coded weights Step 1: closed-loop calibration Step 2: readout-aligned fine-tuning

Readout-level alignment : constraining the projected at-tention outputs WO o instead of raw prefix keys and values focuses the limited trainable capacity on the subspace ex-tracted by the action queries. Student-side queries : oq is computed with the quantized action head’s own queries, so that gradients flow through the head’s lookup tables; this is essential for GR00T, whose 1.1B-parameter head holds half of the quantized weights, whereas freezing the compact head of π0.5 is a special case, and β is set once to balance prefix and head gradient norms. Table-scale stability : each table entry is shared by thousands of weights, so standard codebook learning rates destabilize entire layers. We pair a small cosine-decayed learning rate with a root-mean-square re-normalization of each table after every step, absorbing the factor into sℓ. Fine-tuning sees only the 256 demonstra-tion observations used for calibration: 768 updates on 216 of them, with the checkpoint selected on the other 40. 

4.4. Deployed format and inference 

The stored model is a packed stream of 2-bit codes with one 512-entry table and one scale vector per layer, 2.02 bits per weight over all linear layers, unchanged by both steps. Each weight pair is decoded from a fixed 16-bit window of the stream, so all pairs decode independently, with one shift and one table lookup, inside the GPU kernel that uses them. With 8-bit tables and 8-bit activations taken in the rotated space, a layer becomes an integer matrix product followed by one scale per row. One file runs in two modes: decoding once at load gives the highest speed, and decoding on the fly keeps only the 2-bit stream in GPU memory. Both modes reproduce the reference arithmetic bit for bit (Sec. 5.4).

# 6. Conclusion 

A quantizer treats a policy as weights to approximate, but a policy is a closed loop: it chooses its own inputs and acts on what its action head reads. LoopQ lets calibration follow the quantized policy and the objective follow the readout, and two bits per weight then keep an entire VLA within two points of full precision under perturbation and within one success of it on a real arm, while one bit still yields a working policy. The lesson reaches beyond quantization: an embodied model should be compressed on the distribution it induces and for the signals it consumes.

# D. Inference kernels and operating points 

Kernel design. Each quantized layer computes y =

Sv H diag( sℓ) Q HS ux, where Q holds the decoded table values, H is the Hadamard transform, and Su, S v are the stored sign vectors. Our CUDA kernels execute this in four steps: rotating and quantizing the input to 8 bits per token, decoding all weight pairs in parallel from their 16-bit win-dows, computing an 8-bit integer product with 32-bit accu-mulation, and applying the token and row scales together with the output rotation. Projections reading the same in-put tensor (query, key, and value; the two MLP input pro-jections) share a single input rotation, eliminating 162 of the 414 input rotations exactly. The 8-bit tables are de-rived from the stored tables at load with a maximum relative weight error of only 5.9 × 10 −5.

Deployment on the RTX 6000 Ada (Fig. S2). The pol-icy runs in PyTorch 2.7 with CUDA 12.6 on a single RTX 6000 Ada. Operating points. Table S4 benchmarks all operating points of the stored 2-bit checkpoint. Both integer kernel modes beat full precision in latency while dramatically re-ducing GPU memory. Decoding at load (reported in Tab. 3) cuts chunk latency from 81.6 ms to 74.6 ms and memory by 44% to 3.72 GB. Decoding on the fly retains only the 2-bit stream on the GPU, slashing peak memory by 71% to 1.92 GB (29% of full precision) while still running faster than full precision (79.8 ms vs. 81.6 ms).
