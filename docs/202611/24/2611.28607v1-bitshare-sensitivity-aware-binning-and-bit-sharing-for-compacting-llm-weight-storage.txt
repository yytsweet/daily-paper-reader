# BitShare: Sensitivity-Aware Binning and Bit Sharing for Compacting LLM Weight Storage 

Qiuyu Ren, Sudipta Paria, Aritra Dasgupta, Swarup Bhunia Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering (ECE), University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611, USA 

{qiuyuren,sudiptaparia,aritradasgupta }@ufl.edu, swarup@ece.ufl.edu 

Abstract —Decoding with large language models at small batch sizes is limited by memory bandwidth, so the bits stored per weight largely set its cost. Group-wise integer quantization stores every weight of a group at the same precision, although the input channels of a group differ in sensitivity. We present B IT SHARE ,a codebook-free weight format that keeps the integer grid and the dequantizer of group-wise quantization but varies precision within each group. Sensitivity-aware binning places the channels of each group into three bins at fixed positions, and static bit sharing with optimal bit-value allocation lets the less sensitive weights share their lower bits, each stored once per group or once per pair of weights. A sharing-aware version of GPTQ chooses every stored value, with no fine-tuning and no codebook. BIT SHARE has two modes. B IT SHARE -U gives every group the same layout, so its decoder routes bits by fixed wiring, while BIT SHARE -N chooses a layout per group within the same bit budget. With calibration on the Pile and at the same storage, both modes have lower perplexity than GPTQ at 3 and 2 bits on WikiText-2 and C4. At 2 bits across Llama-2 and Llama-3 models, B IT SHARE -N lowers WikiText-2 perplexity by up to 70% against SliM-LLM and up to 35% against GPTAQ. At the 2-bit perplexity of SliM-LLM, BIT SHARE -U stores 13.6-19.4% fewer bits per weight, cutting DRAM weight traffic by the same fraction. A verified hardware decoder implementation synthesized in SkyWater 130 nm reconstructs B IT SHARE weights at the throughput of INT-n and SliM-LLM decoders. 

Index Terms —Large language models, weight quantization, mixed precision, bit sharing, binning, hardware decoder; 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Serving large language models is limited by their weights. At small batch sizes, every weight is read from memory for each generated token, and weight traffic far exceeds arith-metic [1], so fewer bits per weight mean faster decoding and larger models per device. Post-training quantization usually stores weights in group-wise integer formats, which split each row into groups of 128 input channels with a scale, a zero point and one integer code per weight. These formats are close to lossless at 4 bits but degrade at 3 and especially at 2 bits. At 2 bits, GPTQ with a tuned clipping range raises the WikiText-2 perplexity of Llama-3-8B and Qwen2.5-0.5B by factors of 6.4 and 9.9. Most low-bit methods keep this format and improve the encoder [2]–[5], so every weight of a group maintains the same precision, although the input channels of a group affect the layer output very unequally. Outlier methods [6]–[8] keep a few weights in higher precision but must store their posi-tions. SliM-LLM [9] varies precision between groups, which requires a width per group and lowers decoding throughput, while still treating all weights of a group alike. Codebook and trellis formats [10]–[12] achieve lower 2-bit perplexity but decode through lookups or a trellis and often need fine-tuning. Varying precision within a group is harder, as a per-weight width costs at least one bit per weight, half the budget of a 2-bit format. Work on bit-level structure suggests another route. Ac-celerators prune the low-order bits of a group to a shared constant [13], RRAM designs store identical bit columns once [14], and texture formats, test compression and bus coding tie several values to one stored bit chosen by the encoder (Section II). The low bits of a weight carry little of its value, and even less in the less sensitive channels of a group, so these weights can read their low bits from shared stored bits. Sharing constrains a weight rather than discarding a bit. A weight that shares its lowest bit can still take any code of that parity, a grid with twice the step whose offset is chosen per group-row. On the INT3 grid, this step is still 14% finer than that of INT2. We present B IT SHARE , a format that keeps the integer grid and the dequantizer of group-wise quantization but varies precision within every group (Figure 1). It works on a grid one bit finer than its rate: INT4 at 3 bits and INT3 at 2 bits, and it places the input channels of each group into three bins at fixed positions determined by the Hessian diagonal that GPTQ already computes. The high, middle and low bins store n, n−1

and n − 2 bits of each code. One stored flag per group-row supplies the lowest bit of every middle- and low-bin code, and one stored bit per pair of low-bin weights supplies their second bit. This wiring is the same for every layer and model, and a sharing-aware GPTQ chooses the shared values. The decoder needs no per-weight metadata or codebook. It routes bits into codes and runs the unchanged INT-n dequantizer. B IT SHARE -U gives every group the same layout, so the routing is fixed wiring. B IT SHARE -N picks a layout per group within each layer’s bit budget, incurring a 6-bit index per group and a larger decoder. Both store 3.164 and 2.156 bits per weight, 0.016 more than INT3 and INT2.

B ACKGROUND 

A. Weight-only quantization of LLMs a) Encoders on a fixed grid: Group-wise integer formats store a scale and a zero point per group of 128 input channels and one code per weight (Section III-A), so all weights of a group share one precision. GPTQ [2], derived from OBS [15] and OBQ [16], rounds one column at a time and compensates the error in the remaining columns through the inverse Hes-sian. GPTAQ [3] also matches each layer’s output to the full-precision model, OmniQuant [4] and AffineQuant [5] learn quantizer parameters by gradient descent and QuaRot [17] removes outliers with rotations. All of them keep the INT-n

storage format. 

b) Codebook and trellis formats: QuIP# [10], AQLM [11] and QTIP [12] reach lower 2-bit perplexity with lattice or additive codebooks or a trellis code, usually with Hadamard transforms and fine-tuning, and decode by lookup or from trellis states. In QTIP, neighboring weights share stored bits through overlapping trellis windows.

AWQ [1] found that keeping 1% of the channels in FP16, selected by activation magnitude, lowers the round-to-nearest INT3 perplexity of OPT-6.7B from 23.54 to 11.39, but rescales salient channels instead to keep one precision. Precision is uniform within each group.

D. Motivation for BIT SHARE 

Table I summarizes prior formats. Integer formats decode cheaply but give every weight in a group the same precision. Formats that vary precision pay with stored positions and a sparse path, or with a per-group width map that still leaves each group on one grid. Codebook and trellis formats give up the integer dequantizer, and bit-level techniques share only bits that happen to be equal after quantization. To our knowledge, no integer weight format for LLMs fixes a sharing structure in advance and lets a second-order encoder choose the shared values. B IT SHARE fills this gap. It varies precision within every group at fixed positions, without per-weight metadata, and pays for it with shared bits chosen by GPTQ, while keeping the INT-n dequantizer. Our scope is post-training scalar integer formats without gradient-based training, the setting of SliM-LLM’s comparison (Section IV-A). III. M ETHODOLOGY 

BIT SHARE stores every linear layer on the integer grid of standard group-wise quantization, but gives the weights of a group different numbers of stored bits. Sensitivity-aware binning places the input channels of each group into three bins. A weight in a less sensitive bin stores fewer bits of its code, and every bit it does not store is shared : it is tied by fixed wiring to one stored bit that several weights read. The sizes of a group’s bins form its layout . B IT SHARE has two modes, which differ only in how the groups get their layouts: B IT SHARE -U (uniform) gives every group of every layer the same layout, and B IT SHARE -N (non-uniform) gives each group a layout from a fixed menu, so that groups that need more bits take them from groups that need fewer at the same total. In both modes a sharing-aware version of GPTQ chooses every stored value.

The layout indices of B IT SHARE -N add about 10 −5.A group-row decodes from its own fields and its group’s layout (Ug , D g ), which in B IT SHARE -U is the same for every group and in B IT SHARE -N is looked up from the group’s 6-bit index in the menu, a table of 44 entries shared by every layer and model. The decoder reads sig , zig and fig , then the Ug n-bit, Mg (n−1) -bit and Dg (n−2) -bit fields and the Dg /2 pair bits, forms the codes by Equation 2 and dequantizes them with Equation 1, exactly as INT-n does. Forming the codes needs no arithmetic: a position’s bin follows from two comparisons with its group’s boundaries, which in B IT SHARE -U are the same in every group, so that the routing is fixed wiring. In B IT SHARE -N, group-rows of different layouts have different sizes, but every row of a layer has the same sequence of layouts, so a group’s offset within a row is a prefix sum over the layer’s layout indices, computed once per layer. The format has no per-weight or per-block metadata and no codebook or look-up table of weight values.

F. Ablations 

Table XI in Appendix C-D starts from GPTQ + clip on the INT2 grid and isolates the parts of the 2-bit format one at a time. Without the bins (U0 D0), perplexity is within 0.6% of BIT SHARE -U on Llama-2 but 8-65% higher on Llama-3-8B and the Qwen models, where a few channels per group need the full INT3 grid of the high bin. Taking bit b0 from the parity of the zero point instead of the flag saves 1/128 bit per weight but raises perplexity by 5-30%. A layout per group with equal bits in every group lowers perplexity against B IT SHARE -U by 2.6% on average, though not on every model, and moving bits between groups by Equation 5, as B IT SHARE -N does, lowers it on every model, by 3.6% on average. The appendix also gives the details and shows the layouts that B IT SHARE -N chooses (Figure 7). 

G. Encoding and decoding cost a) Encoding: BIT SHARE -N chooses the layouts from the weights and the diagonal of the Hessian that GPTQ accumulates anyway, and both modes find the codes in one GPTQ pass. On one 48 GB GPU, quantizing Llama-2-70B or Llama-3-70B takes 1.8-1.9 hours with GPTQ + clip, 2.2-2.3 hours with B IT SHARE -U and 3.1 hours with B IT SHARE -N, at either width. SliM-LLM scores every candidate allocation of a linear layer by the KL divergence of its outputs on the calibration set. Its code spends 32-38 minutes per decoder layer on this search alone, or 43-51 hours per model and width, also on one GPU. 

b) Decoding: Assuming weights are streamed once per token at batch size one, bits per weight constrain bandwidth-limited decoding throughput. Both modes use 3.164/2.156 bits per weight in group records, approximately 0.016 above INT3/INT2 and SliM-LLM. At SliM-LLM’s 2-bit perplex-ity on WikiText-2, however, B IT SHARE -U reads 13.6-19.4% fewer bits (Table V), and Appendix D-E turns these savings into a bound on the speedup. Neither mode has per-weight metadata or a codebook. In B IT SHARE -U every group has the same layout, so each field sits at a fixed offset and no layout table is needed. B IT SHARE -N reads a 6-bit layout index per group and selects each field at a layout-dependent offset. Section V implements its decoder in hardware. SliM-LLM, whose groups also differ in size, reports 20-27% lower GPU throughput than GPTQ at 2 bits with its own kernels, on three models and an A800 GPU (Table 4 of [9]). V. H ARDWARE IMPLEMENTATION 

We implemented the decoder of B IT SHARE in synthesizable SystemVerilog code, verified it and compared its resource overhead with INT-n and SliM-LLM under similar constraints. Figure 4 shows the data path from the compressed stream to FP16 weights (Figure 4(a)) and the reconstruction from stored and shared bits (Figure 4(b)) along with the layout-independent output schedule of G/L outputs per group-row (Figure 4(c)). 

A. Microarchitecture 

In the decoder implementation, group-rows are stored back to back without padding, and one 6-bit layout index per group, shared by all rows of a layer, gives each group-row its layout 

(Ug , D g ) and size Bg . A bit aligner cuts each group-row from the packed stream, and the group decoder rebuilds the n-bit codes by Equation 2 and dequantizes them to binary16 by Equation 1, exactly as for INT-n. It emits L weights per cycle and takes G/L cycles per group-row for every layout, so for 

G/L ≥ 2 it sustains min( L, W G/ ¯B) weights per cycle from a 

W -bit memory interface, where ¯B is the mean group-row size; at G = 128 and L = 16 , a 64-bit interface keeps up with every layout. Appendix D-A includes the detailed implementation. 9

with per-group layouts and two-way sharing. SliM-LLM and BIT SHARE require bit alignment for variable-size records. 3) Results: Table VI presents the hardware overhead for the B IT SHARE decoder implementation. At equal throughput, the B IT SHARE decoder takes 1.34 × the area of the SliM-LLM decoder at 3 bits and 1.37 × at 2 bits, and 1.74 × and 1.85 × that of INT-n. Its logic delay is 1.23 × and 1.24 × that of INT-n, and 1.07 × and 1.24 × that of SliM-LLM. At 3 bits, the three group decoders incur 53.8 (INT3), 65.0 (SliM-LLM) and 69.8 k μm2 (B IT SHARE ) of flip-flop area, but 46.9, 64.7 and 140.5 k μm2 of logic area. An INT-n lane reads a fixed bit slice and a SliM-LLM lane chooses among three fixed strides. A B IT SHARE lane selects its field at a layout-dependent offset, and the active group advances by a layout-dependent number of bits per beat. All three share the same binary16 dequantizer, while SliM-LLM and B IT SHARE have nearly identical bit-aligner area. This overhead supports a different quality-storage trade-off. At comparable storage, B IT SHARE adds approximately 0.016 bits per weight and achieves lower perplexity in most evaluated settings. At matched perplexity, the configurations reduce storage (Table V) and weight traffic (Appendix D-E) in most cases. The decoding circuitry can be reused across groups and layers, so its area scales with the number of instantiated decoding engines rather than the number of stored weights. Consequently, the decoder-only area ratios do not represent whole-accelerator overhead: the latter also depends on the area of the compute array, memories and interconnect, together with the required decoder replication. 

4) Energy savings: We consider batch-1 decoding in which quantized weights are streamed from DRAM once per gener-ated token. At equal perplexity, fewer bits per weight reduce storage and, consequently, the weight traffic. Under a constant energy-per-transferred-bit model, this yields proportional sav-ings in weight-transfer energy (Appendix D-F). VI. C ONCLUSION 

We have presented B IT SHARE , a group-wise weight for-mat that combines sensitivity-aware binning and static bit sharing with optimal bit-value allocation to improve the quality-storage trade-off of LLM weight quantization without codebooks or fine-tuning. With calibration on the Pile and at the same storage, both modes of B IT SHARE have lower perplexity than GPTQ with the same range search, and at 2 bits B IT SHARE -N lowers WikiText-2 perplexity by up to 70% against SliM-LLM and up to 35% against GPTAQ. At SliM-LLM’s 2-bit perplexity, B IT SHARE -U stores up to 19.4% fewer bits per weight. The hardware implementation of the decoder verifies correct operation and practical feasi-bility while characterizing its modest overhead. The flag lets the encoder choose that end, and with it the offset of the low bin’s grid, for one bit per group-row. Equivalently, the flag is a bit shared by all Mg + Dg middle- and low-bin weights of the group-row. On the INT4 grid the middle bin is then a 3-bit grid whose step, 2/15 of the range, is 6.7% finer than INT3’s 1/7; on the INT3 grid it is a 2-bit grid with step 

2/7, 14% finer than INT2’s 1/3.

Lower rates. Layouts with δ < 0 store fewer bits. To go further, the pair bit generalizes to a bit shared by B consecutive low-bin positions, which gives D/B bits per group-row and 

δ = U − D(1 − 1/B ). Without such a bit, b1 is dropped :every low-bin code takes it from bit 1 of the zero point 

zig , and δ = U − D. Both modes use pairs ( B = 2). To extend the rate-quality curve of B IT SHARE -U down to collapse, Sections IV-D and IV-E also use B ∈ { 4, 8, 16 } and the dropped bit in every group. 

Layouts. Figure 5 shows three layouts of the menu of BIT SHARE -N. The layouts follow the groups’ sensitivity without being set by it alone: the rank correlation between a group’s mean hj and its offset is +0 .3 to +0 .7 across the models we evaluate. 

Rounding. For a bit shared by B low-bin positions, a block of 

B columns is rounded as a pair is, one column after another. A weight is always rounded to the nearest code consistent with the shared bits, which may change its stored field as well (a carry or borrow), rather than having its own shared bit overwritten. 

Extra work of the encoder. The encoder needs no fine-tuning and no codebook. Its extra work over GPTQ with the same range search is the range search for each layout of the menu before the column loop (in B IT SHARE -N), the second flag value in that search and the two candidate roundings per low-bin pair and row. A 44-entry ROM maps the index to (Ug , D g ), and hence to Bg ; for the one-layout-per-layer variants of Section IV-D the ROM is bypassed by a fixed (U, D ), with b1 shared by two or four low-bin weights. A walker requests group-rows in storage order, and a bit aligner cuts each Bg -bit record from a reservoir filled by W -bit memory words. Because every row has the same layout sequence, the walker needs no stored offsets; for random access, a prefix-sum unit returns the bit offset of every group within a row. Reserved indices and illegal layouts raise an error and stop decoding. 

Group decoder. The group decoder (dotted box in Figure 4(a)) emits L weights per clock cycle, at consecutive positions 

t = base , . . . , base +L−1 of one group-row; we call one such output transfer a beat . Following Section III-E, a lane finds the bin of its position by two comparisons, t < U g and 

t ≥ G − Dg . Its field starts n h + ( n−1) m + ( n−2) l bits into the beat, where h, m and l count the high, middle and low positions in [base , t ). The lane then forms q by Equation 2, with fig as b0 for t ≥ Ug and the pair bit cigk as b1 in the low bin. Three registered stages compute q − zig , the exact integer product of |q − zig | with the significand of sig , and a single round-to-nearest-even to binary16, which is Equation 1 evaluated exactly as for INT-n. The decoder also outputs 

q − zig for datapaths that accumulate in integers and apply 

sig once per group-row. Beats may span bin boundaries, so every group-row takes exactly G/L beats irrespective of its layout. 

Throughput. For G/L ≥ 2, the decoder sustains 

min( L, W G/ ¯B) weights per cycle, where ¯B is the mean record size: L when the memory interface keeps up, and the input bandwidth otherwise. With G = 128 and L = 16 , a 64-bit interface suffices for every layout of the menu, which needs at most 54.6 bits per cycle at n = 4 and 38.5 at n = 3 . In simulation, a row containing all 44 layouts decodes in exactly one beat per cycle at L ∈ { 4, 8, 16 } for both grids. At L = 32 

with W = 64 , it takes 369 and 544 cycles for 352 beats at 

n = 3 and n = 4 .

Permutation. The decoder emits weights in binned order, so activations are read through the permutation π of Section III-B. Since π is shared by all R rows of a layer, an activation vector needs to be permuted once per token and layer rather than once per row. We therefore report the decoder without the activation gather. 

B. Storage layout  

> •

A layer is stored row by row; each row is the con-catenation of its C/G group-rows; nothing is padded. The stream is least-significant-bit first: bit 0 of the first memory word is bit 0 of the layer.  

> •

A group-row is, in this order: sig (16 bits, IEEE bi-nary16); zig (n bits); fig (1 bit); the Ug , Mg and Dg

fields of n, n−1 and n−2 bits in position order; and the 

Dg /S shared bits, with S = 2 , or S = 4 for the four-way variant of Section IV-D. Every field is least-significant-bit first.  

> •

Layout index k ∈ { 0, . . . , 43 } enumerates the menu Λ in lexicographic (U, D ) order; indices 44-63 are reserved. The SliM-LLM decoder stores s (16 bits), z (N +1 bits, wide enough for the largest precision) and G fields of the group’s precision; the INT-n decoder stores s, z (n bits), one unused bit and G fields of n bits.

F. Weight-traffic energy at equal perplexity 

In batch-1 decoding every quantized weight is read from DRAM once per token, so the energy of weight traffic is 

E = N × b × ebit , where N is the number of quantized weights, b the bits per weight and ebit the DRAM energy per bit transferred. N and ebit are the same for every format, so the relative saving is 1 − b/b ref and depends on no assumed constant. At comparable storage, B IT SHARE uses 2.156 bits per weight against 2.141 for SliM-LLM, approximately 0.7% more; its benefit at this operating point is improved perplexity. Table XIV converts the storage saved at 2 bits into weight energy, with ebit ≈ 4 pJ/bit for HBM2 [45] and ≈ 20 pJ/bit for off-package DRAM [46]. B IT SHARE -U reads 13.6-19.4% fewer weight bits than SliM-LLM at the same WikiText-2 perplexity (21.6% or more on C4), which saves 1.2-1.7 pJ per weight with HBM2 and 5.8-8.3 pJ per weight with off-package DRAM. On Llama-2-7B ( 6.5 × 10 9 quantized weights) this is 336 MB less weight traffic per token, or 11-54 mJ per token. The estimate is analytical: it covers weight traffic only, not the KV cache, activations or the FP16 head, which are the same for both formats, and it takes ebit from the literature.
