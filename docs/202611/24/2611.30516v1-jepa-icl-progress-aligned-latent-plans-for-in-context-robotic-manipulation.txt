## Abstract 

Demonstrations show how a task can be completed, but dif-ferences in embodiment, appearance, and execution speed make it difficult to identify the guidance relevant to the robot’s current progress. We introduce JEPA-ICL, a vision-language-action framework that couples predictive plan-ning with demonstration learning. The policy learns to plan by predicting ordered future states in JEPA feature space up to subtask completion, capturing what remains to be accomplished. These plans guide action genera-tion and locate relevant stages in demonstrations, whose JEPA-supervised content features provide progress-aligned context. Learning from this context, in turn, updates the shared policy that produces the plans and actions. This coupling connects anticipating the robot’s own execution with reusing demonstrated experience. On the Imitator Game, JEPA-ICL achieves 80.0% seen-task success under C15 pretraining, outperforming Zero-WAM by 12.0 per-centage points. Without contextual demonstrations, JEPA-ICL outperforms the VLA-JEPA variant without human-video pretraining, improving success rates from 96.1% to 97.6% on LIBERO and from 62.9% to 73.2% on LIBERO-Plus. With demonstration-based training and contextual guidance at inference, LIBERO-Plus success further in-creases from 73.2% to 75.2%. 

## 1. Introduction 

Demonstrations provide more than examples of successful actions: they reveal how a task progresses toward comple-tion. To use this experience, a robot must relate the demon-strated process to what remains to be accomplished in its own execution, even when embodiment, appearance, and execution speed differ. This calls for a representation that supports both anticipating task progress and identifying rel-evant guidance. We study it through the state changes that remain before subtask completion. 1arXiv:2611.30516v1 [cs.RO] 23 Nov 2026 We introduce JEPA-ICL, a vision-language-action framework that connects predictive planning and demon-stration learning through progress-aligned latent plans. Our central idea is to describe an execution stage by the ordered state changes that remain before the current subtask is com-plete (Fig. 1). We learn this representation through a plan-conditioned Markov predictor, a latent world model that forecasts future states in JEPA feature space. The resulting plans support action generation and provide progress keys for locating relevant stages in demonstrations. Prediction and demonstration alignment jointly supervise these plans, connecting the ability to anticipate an execution with the ability to learn from another. Specifically, plan queries from the VLA backbone con-dition this world model, which starts from the current JEPA state and predicts four ordered checkpoints in the remain-ing subtask, one shared Markov transition at a time; su-pervising the intermediate checkpoints as well as comple-tion makes the plan retain the remaining process, not only the endpoint. For demonstration use, the same backbone produces plans for robot observations and demonstration frames, and a learned indexer associates their stages un-der stage and temporal-consistency supervision. The policy then reads JEPA-supervised content features from a short window around the aligned stage through memory tokens, and the retrieved context is updated during execution so that guidance stays tied to the robot’s observed progress. We evaluate these capabilities separately and in combi-nation. On LIBERO [32] and LIBERO-Plus [15], JEPA-ICL achieves 97.6% and 73.2% success without contextual demonstrations, respectively; demonstration-based training and contextual guidance at test time further raise LIBERO-Plus success to 75.2%. On the Imitator Game, JEPA-ICL achieves 80.0% seen-task success under C15 pretraining, outperforming Zero-WAM [63] (68.0%) by 12.0 percentage points. Under C45 pretraining, it reaches 85.5% unseen-task success after fine-tuning on 10 robot demonstrations per task. Real-robot gear assembly further demonstrates the use of recorded experience: JEPA-ICL achieves 80.0% success with a deployment-setup demonstration, compared with 40.0% for the kai0 ( π0.5) baseline. Controlled com-parisons separate the contributions of latent planning, of progress-aligned retrieval, and of context at test time. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • Markov-supervised latent planning. We learn progress-aware plans through recurrent prediction of ordered future JEPA states within the remaining subtask, supervised at intermediate checkpoints and at subtask completion. • Progress-aligned demonstration context. We use la-tent plans to locate relevant demonstration stages and read separate JEPA-supervised content features through mem-ory tokens, which separates progress correspondence from content retrieval and updates guidance as execution progresses. • Joint planning and demonstration learning. We com-bine future prediction, demonstration alignment, and ac-tion supervision to train one shared policy that runs both with and without contextual demonstrations. 

## 2. Related work 

Predictive planning in VLAs. VLA models map obser-vations and an instruction to action chunks with a pretrained vision-language backbone [4, 5, 23, 37, 38, 42]. To give them foresight, recent work adds a model of the future: gen-erated frames [7, 18, 29, 56, 59], latent actions [6, 55], or predicted features [30, 50, 61, 65]. VLA-JEPA [50] and JEPA-WAM [30] are closest to ours, since both supervise a latent plan with frozen V-JEPA features [1, 3]. Their plan predicts a fixed number of steps ahead, so it says what comes next but not how much of the task remains; progress is instead estimated by a separate model [34] or given to the policy as sub-goals [47, 52, 53]. JEPA-ICL instead pre-dicts ordered future states up to subtask completion. Its la-tent plan provides a shared interface for action generation and demonstration matching, connecting prediction of the remaining subtask with reuse of demonstrated experience. 

Demonstrations as context. In-context imitation condi-tions the policy on a demonstration at test time [8, 13, 14, 16, 19, 20, 25, 26, 36, 39, 43, 45, 46, 48, 51, 63]. The dif-ficulty is to decide which part of the demonstration matters now. Policies that read it by time index tend to copy or ig-nore it [21, 51, 53, 54], because a long demonstration and a short action chunk rarely advance in step; policies that align by similarity need a hand-crafted metric, such as DTW over actions [21, 54] or DINO features [39, 48, 62]; HOST [9] and Demo-JEPA [17] learn a dedicated correspondence be-tween demonstration and robot. In these methods the repre-sentation used for matching is built apart from the one the policy acts with. JEPA-ICL aligns by progress with the plan it already computes for control, so it needs no hand-crafted metric and no separate correspondence model, only a small projection of the plan, and the stage it reads is the one that fits what remains to be done. 

From context back to the plan. When the matching rep-resentation is fixed, learning to use demonstrations does not change how the policy plans. MUTEX [44] shows that training one policy on several task modalities improves each of them alone, and memory of the policy’s own his-tory [22, 27] improves it within an episode. ## 3. Method 

JEPA-ICL is a latent-world-model VLA [50] whose plan predicts the states that remain until the current subtask is complete. Such a plan describes an execution stage by what is left to do, so it serves two purposes at once: it condi-tions the robot’s actions, and it locates the same stage in a demonstration, whose content is then read back into action generation. Figure 2 shows the three parts. We first describe the shared policy and its query tokens (Sec. 3.1), then how the plan is supervised (Sec. 3.2), how it retrieves demon-stration context (Sec. 3.3), and how the whole is trained and run (Sec. 3.4). 

3.1. Overview 

At time t the policy observes camera images ot, an instruc-tion ℓ and the proprioceptive state st, and outputs a chunk of 

H actions at:t+H . As in VLA-JEPA [50], a vision-language model fθ (Qwen3-VL [2]) encodes ot and ℓ, and learnable query tokens appended to this sequence carry the outputs of the model (Fig. 2a). The plan queries P yield the latent plan Zt; the future queries F yield the content features Et;the action queries A yield the features ht that, together with 

st, condition a flow-matching action head [31, 40]. When a demonstration is available, memory tokens Mt computed from it join the same sequence. World states are described by a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder [35], and we write S(o) for the patch features of an image o.The attention mask fixes who may read whom. The plan queries read only the observation and the instruction, so Zt

depends on nothing but the current input. The future and action queries read Zt and Mt, and the action queries do not read the future queries. Every action is thus generated with access to the plan and, when present, to the demonstra-tion context, while the plan is never altered by the context it retrieves. The queries only add tokens to the VLM and read its outputs, so they are not tied to one backbone; Sec. 4.4 instantiates them on a second one, π0.5 [42].

The plan queries precede the memory tokens and do not read them, so Zt is read out first and the rest of the sequence is processed afterwards; the π0.5 instance reuses the cached prefix, so the observation is encoded once. 

## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate JEPA-ICL on the Imitator Game, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Plus in simulation, and on a real bimanual robot. The experiments compare policies with and without the pre-dictive plan component and examine performance under different demonstration-context conditions. 

4.1. Setup 

Benchmarks and protocols. The Imitator Game [64] asks a robot to perform a task shown in a human video. Policies are pretrained on the paired human–robot episodes of IG-10K at two scales (C15 and C45: 15 or 45 pretrain-ing tasks) and evaluated in simulation on five seen and five unseen tasks. Each task is tested at four imitation levels (L0–L3) that move the robot’s scene away from the demon-stration, from the same scene to objects that serve the same intent through a different affordance. Seen tasks are eval-uated with the pretrained policy; unseen tasks are held out from pretraining and evaluated under the P+FT protocol, which first fine-tunes the policy on 10 robot demonstrations per task: adaptation with limited data, not zero-shot trans-fer. In both cases our policy receives the human video as context at test time. LIBERO [32] has four suites of ten tasks (Spatial, Object, Goal and the long-horizon LIBERO-10) on a single-arm Franka. LIBERO-Plus [15] perturbs the same tasks along seven dimensions (camera, robot ini-tial state, language, lighting, background, sensor noise, lay-out); following its protocol, models are trained on the orig-inal LIBERO data only and evaluated zero-shot. On both, the policy runs without a demonstration, except for the row of Tab. 3 that gives it one from a different training episode. The metric is the task success rate (SR); the Imitator Game adds its subtask progress (Sub-SR, defined in Tab. 1), and LIBERO-Plus is summarised by the macro average over its seven dimensions. Baseline numbers in Tabs. 1 to 3 are those reported by the benchmarks or by VLA-JEPA [50], except the LIBERO-Plus averages, which we recompute as this macro average for every row; they compare complete systems, and gains are attributed to the plan or to the demon-stration only by the within-method comparisons of Fig. 4 and Tab. 4. Our simulation results follow each benchmark’s official protocol and come from one training run evaluated once with fixed seeds, so we report no variance. 

Implementation. On LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus we build on StarVLA [49] with Qwen3-VL-2B [2] as the back-bone and a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L [35] as the target en-coder; the policy is trained on all four suites for 30 K updates without demonstrations and, for the in-context model, 10 Kfurther updates with a demonstration attached to most sam-ples, at a global batch of 256 . On the Imitator Game and the real robot we instantiate the same queries on π0.5 [42], so the method is tested on two backbones. All instances use 

K = 4 , 16 plan queries, ∆ = 16 , H = 16 , β = 0 .02 ,

γ = 0 .1 and λ = 0 .05 ; one action chunk of the Qwen3-VL instance takes about 0.13 s on an A100 GPU without a demonstration. Further implementation details are in the appendix.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented JEPA-ICL, a VLA framework that couples predictive planning with demonstration learning. Super-vised through recurrent prediction of future JEPA states up to subtask completion, its latent plans guide actions and 8retrieve demonstration context, while learning from this context updates the shared policy. Experiments on the Imitator Game show the advantage of progress-based re-trieval over visual matching, complemented by LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and real-robot evaluations with and without contextual demonstrations. These findings suggest that an-ticipating what remains to be accomplished can provide a shared basis for guiding a robot’s own execution and orga-nizing its use of demonstrated experience. Two questions remain open: our ablations test the plan as a whole rather than its design choices (intermediate checkpoints, recurrent transition), and we do not measure how training with con-text changes the plan itself.

11 A. Implementation details of the progress-aware plan 

Targets. Subtask boundaries on RoboTwin 2.0 come from a manually reviewed semantic segmentation of all 2,500 

training episodes. On LIBERO, the 1,693 training episodes of the four suites carry fine-subtask segments, and a bound-ary is anchored on a gripper open or close event wherever the action stream confirms one. The K = 4 checkpoints (Sec. 3.2) are rounded to frames and repeat the end frame when fewer than K frames remain. Each target is encoded as a pair state S(onk −1, o nk ), the encoder features of the node frame and the frame before it, so that it also carries the local motion at that point; the current state is S(ot, o t).Features have 24 × 24 patches of dimension 1024 .

Loss. Most patches of a manipulation scene are static, so the L1 plan loss of Sec. 3.2 is computed separately on the patches that move within each pair (detected by frame dif-ferencing) and on the remaining patches, weighted 0.8 and 

0.2; it falls back to the dense average when either region is empty. The world-model rollout stops the gradient between recurrent steps. 

World model and its pretraining. The world model is an eight-layer transformer decoder over the patch grid with shifted-window self-attention and cross-attention to the plan ( 16 × 512 ). It is pretrained on the training episodes together with a two-layer resampler that compresses the K

target states into a teacher plan, so that the plan is the only channel for future information. In policy training, the plan projection is first regressed onto the teacher plan for 1,250 

updates (smooth L1, weight 0.1); afterwards the teacher is removed and the plan is trained through Lplan . The world model is kept fixed until update 2,500 and then trained jointly; from update 5,000 an additional term of the same form, with the gradient to the plan stopped, keeps it ac-curate. β is annealed linearly from 0.02 to 0.005 between updates 5,000 and 30 ,000 while the weight of this term is 

0.02 − β, and both are scaled down to 60% with a cosine schedule until update 50 ,000 ; γ = 0 .1 is warmed up over 

1,000 updates. 

Policy. There are 16 plan queries, 64 future queries and 

32 action queries. The future queries are hidden from the action queries by the attention mask in every layer, so the plan is the only predictive signal the action head can read. The action head is a 16-layer DiT that integrates the flow in four steps. On RoboTwin 2.0 the policy observes three 

256 × 256 cameras and a 14-dimensional joint state. Learn-ing rates are 10 −5 for the VLM, 10 −4 for the action head and 3 · 10 −6 for the world model. 

In-context learning. The indexer is a two-layer MLP on the flattened plan ( 16 × 512 → 256 → 128 ) with co-sine logits at temperature 0.1. Its weak supervision uses the annotated fine subtasks (frames of the same subtask are positives, with reviewed event intervals such as grasps as a stricter positive set), a return-cycle loss in both directions, and a soft-DTW path loss. The read-out window uses off-sets Ω = {− 7, 0, 7, 14 } frames around the matched stage; windows outside the demonstration are masked. The mem-ory adapter has two cross-attention blocks with 16 learnable queries over the 4 × 64 future-query tokens, and its 16 out-put tokens have the width of the VLM. On LIBERO, the plans and content features used as keys and values are com-puted by a frozen copy of the policy that is refreshed ev-ery 1,000 updates, so the alignment losses train the indexer but not the plan. The in-context components are added to a policy trained for 30 K updates without demonstrations: the indexer is warmed up for 2,000 updates, and the pol-icy is trained for 10 K further updates at a global batch of 

256 , with a same-task demonstration from a different train-ing episode attached to 80% of the samples and zero mem-ory for the rest. Demonstrations and test cases never share episodes.

## C. Real-robot implementation 

Instantiation on π0.5. We build on the JAX implemen-tation of π0.5 [42] in kai0 [57] and keep all of its parame-ters. After the image and language tokens of its VLM prefix we append 16 plan queries, 16 memory tokens and 64 fu-ture queries; as in the Qwen3-VL instance, the plan queries read only the observation and the preceding plan queries, the memory tokens read the observation and the plan, and the action expert reads the observation, the plan and the memory but not the future queries. The plan is projected to 16 × 512 for the world model, and the future queries are decoded to the V-JEPA 2.1 feature grid of the frame a fixed offset ahead. The proprioceptive state is discretised into the language prompt as in π0.5, and the 14 -D actions are zero-padded to the 32 -D action space of the checkpoint; the flow loss is computed on the 14 valid dimensions only. 

Data and training. The 100 demonstrations are recorded in the training setup with the top camera and two wrist cam-eras, resized to 256 ×256 , and each carries reviewed subtask segments, from which the K = 4 checkpoints are com-puted as in Sec. 3.2. Actions are predicted in chunks of 

16 steps. All parameters are fine-tuned for 20 K updates at a global batch of 256 , with a linear warm-up of 1,250 

updates followed by decay to the end of training. The in-context branch is switched on after 5K updates: from then on, a demonstration recorded in a different session (a dif-ferent source group ) is attached to 80% of the samples, and zero memory to the rest. On this task the stage is lo-cated by the cosine similarity between the flattened plans of the robot and of the demonstration, without a learned indexer; the demonstration plans and content features are cached in a bank computed by the current policy and re-freshed every 2,500 updates. The read-out window uses off-sets {− 16 , 0, 16 , 32 } frames, i.e . one chunk before, at, and one and two chunks after the matched stage. At test time the demonstration (one episode from the training setup, or one recorded in the deployment setup and excluded from train-ing) is encoded once by the deployed policy, and the stage is matched by cosine similarity at every chunk.

## D. Results on RoboTwin 2.0 

RoboTwin 2.0 [10] is a bimanual benchmark of 50 tasks with a clean and a domain-randomized evaluation setting. We follow the protocol of VLA-JEPA [50]: one policy is trained on 50 clean demonstrations per task, for all 50 tasks at once, and evaluated on the clean setting (Tab. 5). The plan raises the average success rate of the same model from 

35 .6% to 37 .4% , and JEPA-ICL improves over VLA-JEPA ‡

by +12 .8 points. The numbers are not comparable to the current leaderboard, whose entries train on 50 clean plus 500 domain-randomized demonstrations per task; under the 50-demonstration protocol, the single-task policies reported by [10] reach 34 .5% (RDT) to 55 .2% (DP3). We did not tune the training recipe for this benchmark and leave the randomized setting to future work. 13
