ABSTRACT
Robots operating in the real world must often adapt when user instructions
change mid-execution, yet current instruction-conditioned robot policies, includ-
ing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs),
are largely evaluated under fixed-instruction episodes. We study mid-execution in-
struction switching and identify trajectory hijacking as a key failure mode, where
the policy fails to redirect sufficiently toward the switched target, with its post-
switch behavior remaining fully or partially drawn toward trajectories associ-
ated with non-target tasks. We propose Counterfactual Action Steering (CAS),
a training-free, model-agnostic inference-time method that identifies interfering
non-target tasks, queries the same policy with corresponding counterfactual in-
structions, and uses target–counterfactual action differences to steer execution
away from interfering trajectory priors. Experiments on LIBERO-Goal-Switch
and CALVIN-Custom-Switch across five VLA and WAM policies, together with
real-robot evaluation, show that CAS consistently improves switching success; for
example, on CALVIN-Custom-Switch in the [15, 21] switching range, CAS im-
proves π0.5 from 5.8% to 54.6%. Successful CAS-guided rollouts further provide
higher-quality self-training supervision than vanilla successful rollouts with the
same number of trajectories, yielding stronger policies that generalize to unseen
later instruction switches. Code will be made publicly available.

INTRODUCTION
Instruction-conditioned robot policies, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Black
et al., 2025b;a; Kim et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024) and World-Action Models (WAMs) (Li et al.,
2026; Yuan et al., 2026), have emerged as a promising approach to general-purpose robot manipula-
tion, where a single policy can perform diverse tasks conditioned on natural-language instructions.
Despite this progress, existing benchmark evaluations typically provide a fixed task-level instruction
at the beginning of each episode and assess whether the corresponding task is completed. This pro-
tocol implicitly assumes that the language command remains unchanged throughout execution (Liu
et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a).
In real-world manipulation, however, instructions are not always fixed: users may update the task
goal after execution has already begun (Chen et al., 2026b). For example, a user may first ask a
robot to grasp an apple, then change the command to grasp an orange after the robot has already
moved toward the apple. This demands robot policies capable of redirecting their behavior from an
intermediate state in response to a new instruction. Consistent with recent work (Chen et al., 2026b),
we observe a striking performance degradation across modern VLA and WAM policies under mid-
execution instruction switching, even when both the source and target tasks have been individually
well-mastered.

To address instruction switching failures, ReSteer (Chen et al., 2026b) relies on a relatively elab-

considerable engineering and implementation complexity. These limitations motivate us to seek a
lightweight and broadly applicable strategy for instruction switching.
Fig. 1 (c) illustrates the design intuition behind our approach. In this representative example, after
the instruction switches from the red block to the blue block, the target-conditioned action may
still be insufficient to fully redirect the robot toward the blue block. Nevertheless, compared with
the counterfactual action predicted under the red-block instruction, it is still biased more toward
the desired blue-block behavior. Motivated by this, we ask: Can action differences between target
and hijacking instructions provide a corrective signal for steering execution away from interfering
non-target trajectories during mid-execution instruction switching?
Our solution.
In this paper, we propose Counterfactual Action Steering (CAS), a training-free,
model-agnostic inference-time method for mitigating trajectory hijacking. CAS first estimates which
non-target tasks may exert strong trajectory priors at the current robot state by comparing the current
end-effector position with task demonstration trajectories. It then queries the policy with the target
instruction and with counterfactual instructions corresponding to the identified hijacking tasks. The
difference between the target-conditioned action and each counterfactual action provides a corrective
direction for steering execution away from the corresponding non-target trajectory prior.
We evaluate CAS across five VLA and WAM policies on our two instruction-switching benchmarks,
LIBERO-Goal-Switch and CALVIN-Custom-Switch, as well as in real-robot experiments. CAS sig-
nificantly improves mid-execution instruction switching success rates and outperforms PCD (Wu
et al., 2026); for example, on CALVIN-Custom-Switch in the [15, 21] switching range, CAS im-
proves π0 from 6.7% to 45.4% and π0.5 from 5.8% to 54.6%. Beyond inference-time steering,
CAS also supports policy self-improvement: successful CAS-guided rollouts exhibit more effective
de-hijacking behavior, providing higher-quality self-training supervision than vanilla successful roll-
outs. As a result, self-training on CAS-guided rollouts improves the original policy more effectively
than vanilla self-training with the same number of trajectories, and this advantage persists when
evaluated on unseen, later instruction switches. To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

PROBLEM FORMULATION AND MOTIVATION
We first formulate the mid-execution instruction switching problem and then present key observa-
tions that motivate our method. Further discussion of related work is provided in Appendix A.
2.1
MID-EXECUTION INSTRUCTION SWITCHING
We study mid-execution instruction switching for instruction-conditioned robot policies, including
both VLA and WAM models. Starting from environment step t = 0, the robot acts under a source
instruction Isrc. At step ts, referred to as the switching step, the active instruction is changed to a
target instruction Itgt. From step ts onward, the policy is expected to redirect its behavior from the
current state to complete the target task specified by Itgt. The success of an instruction-switching
episode is determined by completion of the target task.
2.2
KEY OBSERVATIONS
Observation 1:
Mid-execution instruction switching degrades robot policy performance.
Fig. 2 evaluates OpenVLA-OFT, π0, π0.5, Fast-WAM, and LingBot-VA at different switching steps
ts on LIBERO-Goal and CALVIN-Custom, where ts = 0 denotes directly executing the target task
without an instruction switch. At the switch, we discard any unexecuted source-conditioned actions
in the current action chunk and clear cached temporal context to eliminate explicit carryover from
the source phase. Performance generally declines with increasing switching step ts, with particu-
larly severe degradation at larger ts on CALVIN-Custom, although robustness varies across policies.
These results show that current robot policies struggle under mid-execution instruction switching.
Further experimental details are provided in Appendix H.1.
Observation 2: Robot policies are strongly influenced by visual priors.
One possible explana-
tion for this degradation is that policy behavior may be insufficiently sensitive to language instruc-
tions. This motivates us to examine how modalities affect policy behavior. To this end, we conduct
inference-time modality interventions on LIBERO. We separately perturb vision, language, and,
when available, proprioception: we replace visual inputs with a constant mean image, task instruc-
tions with “remain still”, and proprioceptive inputs with the initial robot state. Across policies, we
observe a clear contrast between language and visual interventions. When task-specific instructions
are replaced, policies often retain non-trivial success rates and continue to exhibit recognizable task-
like manipulation behaviors. In contrast, when visual inputs are disrupted, policies rarely exhibit
recognizable task-like behaviors, instead producing erratic motions, repetitive movements along
nearly fixed directions, or movements with substantially reduced magnitudes. Consistent with this
behavioral degradation, success rates under visual intervention collapse to near zero, even though
object positions in LIBERO are nearly fixed.
We further finetune the policy on the instruction “remain still”. When the instruction is switched
to “remain still” mid-execution, the policy often still fails to stop. For example, when the visual
observation shows the gripper already close to an object, the policy tends to continue the grasping
behavior cued by the observed gripper–object configuration, moving further toward the object in-
stead of remaining stationary. These results suggest that robot policies are strongly influenced by
visual priors.

remain focused on the previous target after an instruction switch (Fig. 3). Further analyses of visual

Observation 3: Non-target trajectory priors hijack post-switch behavior.
A key failure mode
in mid-execution instruction switching is insufficient redirection toward the target task. Consider the
switch shown in Fig. 4 (a,b), from grasping the bowl to turning on the stove. By the switching step,
the gripper has already moved close to the bowl under the source instruction. After the instruction
changes, the target-conditioned action may fail to redirect the robot sufficiently toward the stove,
and the subsequent behavior remains fully or partially drawn toward the bowl-grasping trajectory,
as if hijacked by this trajectory. More generally, the interfering trajectory need not correspond to the
source task: another non-target trajectory can similarly bias the post-switch behavior away from the
target. We refer to this failure mode as trajectory hijacking, encompassing both complete execution
of a non-target task and partial drift toward a non-target trajectory. We observe non-target task
completion across multiple policies and switching ranges, as detailed in Appendix D.2.
Observation 2 suggests that misleading visual priors play an important role in trajectory hijacking.
For example, in standard demonstrations, states in which the gripper is already close to an object
are typically followed by actions that continue toward grasping that object, whereas counterfactual

As a lightweight variant, Source-only CAS uses only the source instruction as the counterfactual
and applies a fixed steering weight, requiring neither trajectory data nor additional candidate-task
instructions while retaining the core counterfactual steering mechanism of CAS. Although less ef-
fective than full CAS, it still noticeably improves switching success, as shown in Sec. 4.4.
3.3
CAS-GUIDED SELF-TRAINING
Beyond inference-time steering, CAS can also improve the underlying policy through self-training.
We collect successful CAS-guided rollouts and finetune the original policy on their post-switch
segments under the updated instruction. These trajectories provide explicit de-hijacking supervi-
sion, helping the policy learn to escape interfering trajectory priors and generalize recovery to later
switching states unseen during self-training. Additional details are provided in Appendix H.3.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION
We construct two benchmarks for evaluating mid-execution instruction switching: LIBERO-Goal-
Switch, built on LIBERO-Goal (Liu et al., 2023), and CALVIN-Custom-Switch, built on the
CALVIN simulator (Mees et al., 2022).
For LIBERO-Goal-Switch, we evaluate switching at
ts ∈{10, 15, 20, 25}, covering progressively later stages of source-task execution. For CALVIN-
Custom-Switch, episodes are grouped into three switching ranges, ts ∈[9, 15], [15, 21], and [21, 27],
corresponding to progressively later progress along the source-task trajectory. Further benchmark
construction and evaluation details are provided in Appendix C.
4.2
CAS MITIGATES TRAJECTORY HIJACKING AT INFERENCE TIME
Simulation evaluation.

gressive and diffusion-based policies and is not directly applicable to the mainstream OpenVLA-
OFT implementation. As shown in Table 1, CAS consistently improves over the corresponding base
policies across both benchmarks, all five evaluated policies, and all switching settings, while also
outperforming PCD in every setting where PCD is applicable. The gains are particularly pronounced
on CALVIN-Custom-Switch. For example, in the [9, 15] switching range, CAS improves the success

different initial states (Appendix E.5).
Real-world evaluation.
We evaluate CAS on an AIRBOT Play robot arm. The robot first executes
an instruction to pick up one block (red or blue), and the instruction is then switched to the other at
step ts. We test both switching directions at ts = 20 and ts = 30, with 20 trials per setting, using
matched object placements for the base and CAS trials. As shown in Table 3, CAS improves π0 in all
settings. The real-world policy predicts delta joint actions rather than the delta end-effector actions
used in simulation, suggesting that CAS is not tied to a specific action representation. Fig. 6 shows
a representative switch from picking up the red block to picking up the blue block. Without CAS,
the policy remains biased toward the red-block trajectory prior and fails to redirect sufficiently; with
CAS, it escapes the interfering prior and successfully picks up the blue block.

collected at ts = 25; we evaluate at ts = 25 and further test generalization at the unseen later switch

further show that CAS can produce more effective de-hijacking behavior even when both methods
ultimately complete the target task, highlighting differences in trajectory quality that are not cap-
tured by binary success alone. The consistent gains of CAS-ST at unseen later switches further
indicate that the recovery behaviors learned from CAS-guided trajectories extrapolate beyond the
switching regimes used for self-training.
Motivated by this extrapolation, we further investigate whether CAS-guided self-training can be ap-
plied iteratively to progressively improve recovery at later switches. Starting from the one-round
π0.5 CAS-ST checkpoint on LIBERO-Goal-Switch, whose self-training trajectories were collected
at ts = 25, we collect additional successful CAS-guided trajectories at ts = 40 and perform a
second round of self-training. As shown in Table 4, the one-round checkpoint already outperforms
the base policy at ts = 40, 50, 60, well beyond its data-collection setting. The second round fur-
ther improves performance at all three switching steps. Notably, although the second-round data
are collected only at ts = 40, the gains persist at the unseen later switches ts = 50 and ts = 60.
These results show that CAS-guided self-training can be iterated to progressively improve recovery
under increasingly challenging switching conditions. To further analyze the effect of self-training on
instruction-conditioned action predictions, we compare instruction-conditioned actions before and

guishable actions that are better aligned with the corresponding task directions, even in challenging
states where the base policy predicts nearly overlapping actions; see Appendix F.2.

CONCLUSION
We studied mid-execution instruction switching across VLA and WAM policies and identified trajec-
tory hijacking as a key failure mode. We proposed CAS, a training-free, model-agnostic inference-
time method that steers the policy away from interfering trajectory priors using target–counterfactual
action differences. Across two switching benchmarks and real-robot evaluation, CAS consistently
improves switching success.
CAS-guided rollouts also provide better self-training supervision,
yielding stronger policies that generalize to unseen later switches.

RELATED WORK
Generalist instruction-conditioned robot policies.
Instruction-conditioned robot policies have
emerged as a promising paradigm for general-purpose robot manipulation, enabling a single policy
to perform diverse tasks from visual observations and natural-language instructions. A prominent
line of work develops Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies that directly connect multimodal per-
ception and language understanding to action generation. π0 formulates robot control as a vision-
language-action flow model (Black et al., 2025b), while π0.5 extends this framework toward open-
world generalization through co-training on heterogeneous robot data, high-level semantic predic-
tion, web data, and hybrid multimodal examples (Black et al., 2025a). OpenVLA-OFT focuses on
efficient adaptation of pretrained VLA models to downstream manipulation tasks through improved
action decoding, action representations, and finetuning objectives (Kim et al., 2025). VLAct fur-
ther explores representation-centric continued pre-training of VLM backbones for improved transfer
across action heads, tasks, and robot embodiments (Yang et al., 2026a), while Mage-VL investigates
codec-native visual encoding for efficient multimodal perception (Yang et al., 2026b). More re-
cently, World-Action Models (WAMs) have explored predictive world modeling for robot control.
LingBot-VA studies causal world modeling for action generation (Li et al., 2026), while Fast-WAM
investigates whether explicit future imagination is necessary at test time and develops a more effi-
cient WAM formulation for action prediction (Yuan et al., 2026). Together, these VLA and WAM
approaches represent a diverse family of generalist instruction-conditioned robot policies.
Alongside model development, benchmarks and evaluation frameworks such as LIBERO,
simulation-based evaluation of real-world robot manipulation policies, VLABench, and RoboTwin
2.0 provide broad testbeds for language-conditioned manipulation, knowledge transfer, long-horizon
reasoning, and robust bimanual control (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen
et al., 2026a). However, standard evaluations typically assume that the task instruction remains fixed
throughout an episode. They therefore measure whether a policy can execute a specified task from
an initial state, but do not directly test whether it can redirect its behavior from an intermediate state
after the instruction changes. We study this setting under mid-execution instruction switching and
show that strong policies across both VLA and WAM families can suffer substantial performance
degradation, with trajectory hijacking emerging as a key failure mode. These observations motivate
approaches that directly address switching failures after execution has already begun.
Instruction switching in instruction-conditioned robot policies.
A small but growing body of
work has begun to study whether robot policies can follow newly issued instructions from interme-
diate execution states. Existing approaches have primarily focused on VLA policies, while mid-
execution instruction switching in WAMs remains comparatively underexplored.
ReSteer (Chen et al., 2026b) studies a closely related problem under the notion of policy steerability,
asking whether a multitask robot policy can respond to newly issued task instructions from inter-
mediate execution states. In addition to an exhaustive rollout-based evaluation protocol, ReSteer
introduces conditional mutual information (CMI) between language and action as a proxy for iden-
tifying states in which the policy is weakly conditioned on the instruction. Low-CMI states are then
prioritized for additional steering supervision. To improve steerability, ReSteer develops a multi-
stage data-centric refinement pipeline. It first segments manipulation demonstrations into semantic
stages, such as pre-grasp, transport, and place, using kinematic and contact cues. For a selected
low-steerability source state, it searches for a stage-compatible state from a target-task demonstra-
tion and synthesizes a cross-task transition between the two, followed by execution under the target
instruction. The resulting steering trajectories are added to the training data for policy finetun-
ing. ReSteer further applies self-refining behavioral cloning (SRBC), collecting successful task-
switching rollouts from the updated policy and using them for further refinement. Overall, ReSteer
improves steerability through a relatively elaborate workflow that combines state-wise steerability
estimation, stage-aware trajectory processing, cross-task transition synthesis, policy finetuning, and
self-refinement, resulting in considerable methodological and engineering complexity.
This work establishes the importance of execution-time instruction switching, but addresses switch-
ing failures through additional data generation and training. In contrast, the core CAS method targets
instruction-conditioned robot policies broadly, including both VLA and WAM policies, and operates
as a training-free, model-agnostic inference-time intervention. Rather than learning or synthesizing
a dedicated transition mechanism, CAS queries the same policy under the target instruction and

Computing multiple instruction-conditioned action chunks.
CAS requires action-chunk pre-
dictions under multiple instructions at the same observation, including the target instruction and the
counterfactual instructions associated with the selected hijacking tasks. A simple implementation
is to compute these chunks through multiple policy forward passes: one forward pass for Itgt and
one additional forward pass for each hj ∈Ht using instruction Ihj. Each policy query returns an
action chunk, and CAS applies Eq. (2) to corresponding horizon indices across the target and coun-
terfactual chunks. This implementation requires only minimal changes to an existing robot-policy
inference pipeline, since the policy can be queried repeatedly with the same visual observation and
different language instructions.
When inference efficiency is important, these instruction-conditioned action chunks can instead be
computed in a single batched forward pass. Specifically, the current visual observation Vt is repeated
across the batch dimension, while the language inputs are set to {Itgt, Ih1, . . . , Ihm}. The policy
then returns the target-conditioned action chunk and all counterfactual action chunks together. In
this batched implementation, CAS does not introduce additional sequential policy-forward time for
obtaining multiple action chunks in principle.

BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION DETAILS
C.1
COMPLEMENTARY ONLINE AND OFFLINE PROTOCOLS
We construct two primary simulation benchmarks, LIBERO-Goal-Switch and CALVIN-Custom-
Switch, using complementary protocols for evaluating mid-execution instruction switching in VLA
19


Preprint
and WAM policies. We additionally introduce CALVIN-Custom-Switch-Random as a supplementary
variant of CALVIN-Custom-Switch, in which object placements are broadly randomized to evaluate
mid-execution instruction switching without fixed nominal object locations.
LIBERO-Goal-Switch adopts an online switching protocol. The evaluated policy first executes a
source instruction and, at a designated switching step ts, receives a new target instruction within
the same rollout. The switching state is therefore produced naturally by the policy’s own preceding
execution, closely reflecting realistic instruction-switching scenarios in which a user changes the
instruction while the robot is already acting. A minor consequence of this protocol is that different
policies may make slightly different spatial progress along the source-task trajectory by the same
switching step ts, due to small differences in execution speed. This can introduce modest variations
in switching difficulty across models. Nevertheless, comparisons between methods built on the same
base policy remain well controlled, since they use the same pre-switch policy and any intervention
is activated only after the instruction changes.
CALVIN-Custom-Switch, in contrast, adopts a controlled offline switching protocol. Each episode is
initialized from a predefined intermediate state representing partial progress toward a source task,
and the policy is then evaluated on whether it can complete the updated target instruction from that
state. All evaluated policies and methods receive exactly the same robot state, object configuration,
and target instruction. This state-matched design removes differences caused by pre-switch execu-
tion speed and avoids small variations introduced by policy stochasticity before the switch, making
comparisons across all evaluated models and methods strictly fair by construction.
The two primary benchmarks thus provide complementary views of instruction switching. LIBERO-
Goal-Switch emphasizes realistic online adaptation from states naturally reached by the evaluated
policy, whereas CALVIN-Custom-Switch isolates post-switch adaptation under identical physical
conditions. CALVIN-Custom-Switch-Random retains the controlled offline protocol of CALVIN-
Custom-Switch while removing its fixed nominal object locations, providing a complementary eval-
uation under broadly randomized object placements. In all benchmarks, success is determined by
completion of the target task after the instruction change.
C.2
CALVIN-CUSTOM-SWITCH DETAILS
Scene and tasks.
CALVIN-Custom-Switch is built on top of the CALVIN simulator. We design
a tabletop scene containing three colored blocks—red, pink, and blue—and a press button. The
benchmark contains four atomic tasks with fixed natural-language instructions: “lift the red block”,
“lift the pink block”, “lift the blue block”, and “press the button”.
Demonstration generation.
Since the evaluated pretrained policies do not directly solve these
custom tasks, we construct a small demonstration dataset for finetuning. Demonstrations are gener-
ated by a rule-based scripted oracle. For each episode, object poses and the robot initial configuration
are perturbed around nominal values, invalid configurations such as object collisions are rejected,
and the scene is allowed to settle before execution.
For block-lifting tasks, the oracle moves above the target block, descends to grasp it, closes the
gripper, and lifts it to a fixed height. For the button-pressing task, the oracle moves above the button
and presses it downward with the gripper closed. We retain only successful rollouts verified by the
CALVIN task oracle. In our experiments, we collect 50 demonstrations per task and use them to
finetune the evaluated policies.
These 200 demonstrations contain only standard single-instruction episodes and no mid-execution
instruction switches. Thus, the evaluated policies learn the four underlying manipulation tasks with-
out being explicitly trained on the switching behavior evaluated by the benchmark.
Switching-state construction.
To construct switching states, we sample intermediate robot con-
figurations along a scripted reference path for each source task. The path moves from the default
robot pose toward a pre-grasp or pre-contact pose and then toward the corresponding interaction
pose. We sample a progress value along this path, solve for the corresponding robot joint configura-
tion using inverse kinematics, and save the settled robot and scene state. Object poses are sampled
near fixed nominal locations with small perturbations, and all states are generated with random seed
0.
20


Preprint
We divide the reference path into early, middle, and late progress intervals. Using a 30-step reference
schedule, these intervals are reported as the switching-step ranges [9, 15], [15, 21], and [21, 27].
These ranges indicate the progress of the saved state along the source-task reference path; they
do not imply that the evaluated policy executes the source instruction online for the corresponding
number of actions. States from later ranges lie further along the source-task behavior and generally
make redirection toward a new target more difficult.
Evaluation protocol.
For each switching range, we generate 20 object configurations and con-
struct one intermediate state for each of the four possible source tasks. Each intermediate state is
paired with the three distinct target tasks, yielding
20 × 4 × 3 = 240
instruction-switching episodes per range. The state-generation scripts additionally produce 80 diag-
onal source–target controls in which the instruction remains unchanged. These no-switch controls
are excluded from the switch-only results in Table 1 and from the reported Switch Success Rate.
At evaluation time, the policy is initialized directly from the saved intermediate state and receives
only the target instruction. It may execute at most 180 environment actions. An episode is considered
successful only if the CALVIN task oracle verifies completion of the target task; completing the
source task or another non-target task does not count as success. All policies and methods are
evaluated from exactly the same saved states.
C.3
CALVIN-CUSTOM-SWITCH-RANDOM DETAILS
The standard CALVIN-Custom-Switch benchmark samples object poses as small perturbations
around fixed nominal locations. As a result, demonstration trajectories of the same task tend to
occupy relatively concentrated regions of the workspace, which may induce strong and spatially
localized trajectory priors. This raises a natural question: does the difficulty of mid-execution in-
struction switching persist when such fixed object-centered spatial regularities are removed, and can
CAS remain effective under randomized object placements?
To study this question, we construct CALVIN-Custom-Switch-Random, a qualitatively different vari-
ant in which block locations are sampled over a broader workspace region without fixed nominal
centers. Unlike the concentration-controlled variants, which preserve task-specific nominal object
locations and vary only the perturbation range, the random regime substantially changes the spa-
tial organization of the resulting task trajectories, as illustrated in Fig. 16 (d). Consequently, task
trajectories are no longer tightly associated with fixed object-centered regions of the workspace.
Randomized object placements and demonstrations.
Instead of sampling block poses as pertur-
bations around task-specific nominal locations, we uniformly sample the center of each block over
the entire valid tabletop region. This removes the fixed spatial association between each block and
a particular region of the workspace, while ensuring that sampled block locations remain within the
valid manipulation area. We generate training demonstrations under this randomized placement dis-
tribution using the same scripted-oracle procedure as in CALVIN-Custom-Switch. For each of the
four tasks, we collect 100 successful demonstration trajectories, yielding 400 training trajectories in
total.

D.1
ANALYSIS OF VISUAL PRIORS
We examine visual priors from four complementary perspectives: modality interventions test behav-
ioral reliance on visual input, an online stop test measures persistence despite explicit stop supervi-
sion, fixed-state attention probes examine whether an updated instruction redirects visual attention,
and targeted visual interventions examine which robot–object cues preserve task-like behavior under
a conflicting instruction.

Modality intervention results.
The modality interventions reveal a strikingly consistent depen-
dence on visual input. Under the reported clean settings, all five policies achieve high average
LIBERO success rates, ranging from 94.2% to 98.5%. In contrast, replacing the visual observations
with a spatially constant image reduces the average success rate to at most 1.9% for every evaluated
policy. OpenVLA-OFT and Fast-WAM drop to 0.0%, LingBot-VA to 0.1%, and π0 and π0.5 to 1.7%
and 1.9%, respectively. This collapse is also highly consistent across individual suites: almost all
model–suite combinations achieve zero success under the vision intervention, with the main residual
successes occurring on LIBERO-Goal for π0 and π0.5, which achieve 6.8% and 7.4%, respectively.
We inspect these successful rollouts and find that they do not resemble normal task-conditioned ex-
ecution. Instead, the policy repeatedly produces nearly the same motion, continuously translating
toward the stove while rotating in a nearly fixed direction. This repetitive motion occasionally con-
tacts the stove knob and accidentally satisfies the “turn on the stove” success condition. The residual
success under vision intervention therefore largely reflects coincidental task completion rather than
coherent execution based on the visual scene.
Replacing the task instruction produces a markedly different outcome. Substantial task success
remains for several policies even though they no longer receive the original task-specific language.
OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5, Fast-WAM, and LingBot-VA retain average success rates of 60.3%, 55.6%,
46.0%, and 58.7%, respectively, while π0 retains 19.3%. The residual performance is strongly suite-
dependent. For example, OpenVLA-OFT still achieves 96.2% on LIBERO-Object and 77.4% on
LIBERO-Long, while LingBot-VA achieves 82.6% on LIBERO-Spatial and 84.2% on LIBERO-
Long. In contrast, success under the instruction intervention is much lower on LIBERO-Goal for
all models, ranging from 1.6% to 12.8%. These results do not imply that language is unimportant;
rather, they show that removing the original task-specific instruction does not necessarily eliminate
recognizable task-like manipulation behavior.
The proprioception intervention exhibits substantially greater variation across policies. OpenVLA-
OFT retains 41.7% average success, π0 drops to 7.9%, whereas Fast-WAM remains at 86.6%. Thus,
the contribution of online proprioceptive updates appears strongly policy-dependent. Notably, π0 is
24


Preprint
also sensitive to the proprioception intervention, suggesting that current robot-state information can
play an important role in its behavior in addition to visual input. This observation is compatible with
our KNN-based design in CAS, which uses the current end-effector position to estimate proximity
to task trajectories.
In contrast, the near-complete failure under visual intervention is remarkably consistent across all
five evaluated architectures.
Interpreting residual success under instruction intervention.
We further inspect why substan-
tial success can remain under the instruction intervention. Except for LIBERO-Goal, the other three
LIBERO suites contain visual scene or configuration cues that can often provide information about
which task is likely to be executed. Such visual cues may therefore partially explain the relatively
high residual success rates when task-specific language is replaced. Although these visual cues can
be task-discriminative, they do not necessarily drive the policy toward the very task behavior associ-
ated with the original environment task. For example, by inspecting rollouts on LIBERO-Object, we
observe many cases in which the policy continues to exhibit a recognizable task-like manipulation
behavior after the instruction is replaced by “remain still.”, but the resulting behavior corresponds to
a different task from the one originally specified.
Importantly, our interpretation that robot policies are strongly influenced by visual priors is not
based solely on the success-rate differences across modality interventions. Success rate only indi-
cates whether the resulting behavior happens to satisfy the original task predicate; it does not fully
characterize what behavior the policy is attempting to execute. A failed episode under instruction
intervention may still contain a highly recognizable task-like manipulation behavior, whereas, as dis-
cussed above, a successful episode under vision intervention may result from accidental interaction
with the environment.
Behavioral analysis.
Our main evidence also comes from direct inspection and visualization of
policy behavior. Consistent with the observation in Sec. 2.2, under instruction intervention, policies
frequently continue to exhibit recognizable task-like manipulation behaviors, as if still executing
a plausible task. This remains true even in cases where the resulting behavior corresponds to a
task different from the one originally specified. In contrast, under vision intervention, such task-
likeness is substantially degraded, manifesting as erratic motions, repetitive motion along a nearly
fixed direction, or movements with noticeably reduced magnitudes.
Below, we visualize representative rollouts for all five evaluated policies under three conditions:
clean inference, instruction intervention, and vision intervention (Figs. 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12). We vi-
sualize each rollout using five frames sampled every 20 environment steps, at steps 0, 20, 40, 60, and
80. At 20 fps, these frames correspond to video timestamps 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 seconds, respectively.
These examples illustrate the contrast in behavioral task-likeness across input conditions.
For OpenVLA-OFT (Fig. 8), the rollout under instruction intervention closely resembles the clean
rollout, with the robot successfully reaching and grasping the correct object in both cases. Under
vision intervention, however, the gripper instead repeatedly translates toward the right without ex-
hibiting a recognizable manipulation behavior. For π0 (Fig. 9), the instruction-intervened policy still
reaches and grasps the correct object, although it does not successfully complete the lift. In contrast,

MID-EXECUTION STOP TEST AFTER TRAINING ON THE “REMAIN STILL”
INSTRUCTION
Experimental setup.
We further test whether OpenVLA-OFT can reliably halt an ongoing be-
havior when the “remain still” instruction is explicitly included in training. After adding explicit
remain-still supervision, we evaluate online instruction switching from each of the ten LIBERO-
Goal source tasks to “remain still” at different stages of execution, and measure how much the robot
continues to move after the switch.
Training with explicit remain-still supervision.
We augment the OpenVLA-OFT training data
with 100 explicit remain-still trajectories. Each trajectory contains 20 environment steps and is ini-
tialized from a robot state sampled across the workspace covered by LIBERO-Goal demonstrations,
with the sampled states distributed across the ten LIBERO-Goal task contexts. Throughout each
trajectory, the six end-effector translation and rotation action dimensions are set to zero, while the
gripper is kept in its initial open state. Thus, the training data explicitly associates the instruction
“remain still” with zero commanded end-effector motion.
To retain supervision for the original LIBERO-Goal tasks during this additional training, we also in-
clude 50 original LIBERO-Goal demonstration trajectories, with five demonstrations sampled from
each of the ten tasks. We finetune OpenVLA-OFT on this mixed dataset and use the resulting Step-
55,000 checkpoint for evaluation.
Online mid-execution stop evaluation.
We evaluate the trained policy using an online
instruction-switching protocol. For each of the ten LIBERO-Goal tasks, the policy first executes
the corresponding source instruction from the common initial state. We then switch the instruction
to “remain still” at
ts ∈{5, 10, 15, 20, 25},
resulting in 10 × 5 = 50 switching rollouts in total. At the switching boundary, any unexecuted
source-conditioned actions in the current action chunk are discarded, and the policy immediately
replans under the “remain still” instruction. We then continue execution for 100 environment steps.
OpenVLA-OFT executes an open-loop action chunk of eight environment actions before each new
policy query. All movement statistics below are computed only over the post-switch portion of the
rollout.

D.2
NON-TARGET TASK COMPLETION IN FAILED INSTRUCTION SWITCHING
We further quantify how often failed instruction-switching episodes result in the completion of a
non-target task. We analyze the CALVIN-Custom online switching evaluations used in Observa-
tion 1, covering four switching ranges, ts ∈[3, 9], [9, 15], [15, 21], [21, 27]. Each policy is evaluated
on 240 episodes per switching range, yielding 960 episodes in total.
We define a wrong-task completion as an episode in which the target task is not completed, but the
robot completes a different task. This captures a strong form of trajectory hijacking in which the
policy does not merely drift toward a non-target trajectory, but ultimately completes a non-target
task. It is therefore a conservative indicator of trajectory hijacking, since partial drift toward a non-
target trajectory need not result in completion of that task.
Tables 11 and 12 show that non-target task completion occurs across multiple policy architectures
and switching ranges. For OpenVLA-OFT, π0, and π0.5, wrong-task completion accounts for 17.5%,
17.0%, and 14.6% of target-task failures overall, respectively. Thus, a non-negligible fraction of
switching failures do not simply end without completing a task; instead, the robot completes a task
other than the switched target.
For several policies, wrong-task completion also becomes more frequent at later switching stages.
The trend is particularly pronounced for π0, for which wrong-task completion rises from 0.0% of
target-task failures in [3, 9] to 31.4% in [21, 27]. OpenVLA-OFT shows a similar increase from
11.8% to 22.6%. This pattern is consistent with later switching states lying further along the source-
task behavior, where redirecting execution toward a new target becomes more challenging.
The frequency of complete wrong-task execution varies substantially across policies, with Fast-
WAM and LingBot-VA exhibiting this outcome less frequently. Importantly, a low wrong-task com-
pletion rate does not imply that the remaining switching failures are unrelated to trajectory hijacking.
Our definition of trajectory hijacking also includes partial drift toward a non-target trajectory, which
may disrupt target-task execution without culminating in completion of another task. Overall, these
results provide quantitative evidence that complete execution of a non-target task is a recurring
failure pattern in mid-execution instruction switching, complementing the qualitative examples in
Fig. 4.

WHERE DO TASK DISTINCTIONS WEAKEN DURING ACTION GENERATION?
Observation 3 suggests that task distinctions can weaken as the gripper approaches an object. To
locate where this attenuation occurs, we trace task-conditioned representations inside π0.5, from the
language backbone through the action expert to the output. We ask where inside the policy different
tasks become less distinguishable as execution approaches the source object.
Our main comparison follows the same wine-source trajectory at two stages. At the early state
(t = 10), the gripper is still approaching the bottle and different instructions produce clearly distinct
action directions. At the later state (t = 25), the gripper is close to the bottle and the action directions
become less distinguishable across instructions. At each state, we hold the visual observation, robot
state, and initial action noise fixed while changing only the instruction. We then compare task
separation between the two states within each representation space, using the relative separation D
defined below.
Task differences persist throughout the language backbone.
Even when the gripper is close to
the bottle and the action directions become less distinguishable, the language backbone continues to
produce distinct representations for the four tasks. Figure 15a shows that these differences persist
across all 18 layers. At the backbone output, the late state’s object-token separation remains 95% of
its early-state value (Table 13).
The shared final newline token provides a complementary view. It has the same input embedding
under all four instructions, but develops different contextual representations as the instructions are
processed. Its late-state separation retains 90% of the early-state value. Thus, the near-bottle state
still preserves task-dependent language representations, even though the action-level distinctions
have been weakened.
Task differences become much weaker in the action expert.
We next follow the same four

state develops clear task separation during action generation, whereas separation is much weaker
in the near-bottle state (Fig. 15b). This contrast is already present at the first flow update, when
all instructions begin from identical action noise, and persists through the subsequent updates. The
difference remains visible at the final flow update (Table 13).

Implications for CAS and CAS-ST.
These observations suggest that task distinctions can be
weakened during action generation without being entirely lost.
Importantly, even when task-

D.4
LOCAL POSITIONAL ROBUSTNESS OF TRAJECTORY PRIORS
When the gripper is close to a drawer handle, demonstrations typically continue with contact and
a pulling motion. A policy trained on these regularities can learn to favor that continuation from
the current visual state. If this preference persists after the instruction changes, it contributes to
trajectory hijacking. The same tendency could also help complete the intended interaction after
redirection, provided it tolerates some variation in where the gripper arrives.
Local displacements let us examine this possibility while keeping the robot in the same task-
associated neighborhood. After the released OpenVLA-OFT policy takes 25 actions toward one
of three LIBERO-Goal tasks, we shift the gripper by up to 3 cm and let it replan without CAS. We
use CAS’s KNN distance to delimit the neighborhood, retaining physically valid shifts only when
the source trajectory remains closest among the three task libraries. Keeping the original instruction
tests whether the interaction remains recoverable; replacing it with “remain still” tests whether it
persists when the instruction no longer requests it.
The interaction remains highly recoverable: completion stays at 92–100% under the original in-
struction across the tested radii (Table 14). More strikingly, original-task completion remains at
88–100% after switching to “remain still”. For drawer opening, the policy completes 92% of trials
after a 3 cm shift under “remain still”, compared with 98% without perturbation. It is failing to stop,
yet still succeeding at the original interaction. This is consistent with trajectory hijacking, while
showing that the persistent behavior survives a range of local positions rather than depending on one
exact gripper pose.
The KNN measurements locate this behavior relative to demonstrations. Across the reported condi-
tions, median distances to the source task remain within 1.11–1.96 cm, while those to either alter-
native task are at least 6.40 cm. This proximity to demonstrated states is the lightweight proxy for
trajectory-prior strength used by CAS (Sec. 3.2): it indicates which task has local geometric sup-
port, without directly measuring the policy’s internal prior. Here, KNN defines the neighborhood in
which perturbations are sampled; the rollouts show that the behavior remains robust within it.
These results suggest that CAS can exploit the same behavioral persistence that makes instruc-
tion switching difficult. Its role is to redirect execution away from an interfering trajectory and
toward states compatible with the intended task. Within that region, the underlying policy can use
its learned behavior and visual feedback to refine positioning and complete the interaction. Thus,
effective redirection may allow some error in the precise gripper position. This local tolerance of-
fers an explanation for the broad effective range of λ observed in Appendix E.6.2: different steering
magnitudes may still reach states from which the policy can complete the intended task.

ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTS AND ANALYSES OF INFERENCE-TIME CAS
E.1
INFERENCE-TIME EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS
We further analyze the inference-time efficiency of CAS on CALVIN-Custom-Switch using π0 for
ts ∈[15, 21]. We compare the base policy, CAS, and PCD over the same 240 evaluation episodes
on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. All methods use the same maximum episode length of 180 envi-
ronment actions.
Efficiency of CAS.
CAS may query the policy under multiple task instructions at the same state,
including the target instruction and the instructions of selected hijacking tasks. These instruction-
conditioned action predictions are independent and can therefore be issued in a single batched policy
call. In principle, such batching can substantially reduce the additional sequential policy-query
latency introduced by counterfactual queries. In our current implementation, however, we use a
simpler serial implementation and query the policy separately for different task instructions.
Even without such parallelization, CAS introduces relatively modest additional policy-query cost.
As shown in Table 15, CAS has a lower average episode time, 12.13 s compared with 14.14 s for the
base policy. Since all methods use the same maximum episode length of 180 environment actions,
this lower average episode time is consistent with CAS more frequently completing the target task
before reaching the episode horizon.
The reported per-replan timing measures the accumulated latency of all policy queries within each
replan, including the base target-conditioned query and any additional counterfactual queries. We
therefore report this metric as Avg. Inference Latency per Replan. CAS increases the average infer-
ence latency per replan from 102.2 ms for the base policy to 157.4 ms, corresponding to only 1.54×
the base latency. In comparison, PCD has an average inference latency of 788.3 ms per replan, or
7.71× the base latency.
Across all CAS replans in this evaluation, CAS makes 1.60 policy queries on average, including the
target-conditioned query. Equivalently, in addition to the single target-conditioned query required
by the base policy, CAS introduces only 0.60 counterfactual policy queries per replan on average.
This low query count results from the selective nature of CAS: rather than querying every non-target
task, CAS only queries tasks whose trajectory distributions are sufficiently close to the current state
and thus exert strong interfering priors.
Scaling to larger task sets.
One potential concern is that, under a serial implementation, the
policy-query cost of CAS could increase if many counterfactual tasks need to be queried. Impor-
tantly, however, the number of counterfactual policy queries is determined by the number of locally
interfering trajectories rather than directly by the total number of tasks. Even as the overall task set
grows, CAS only queries non-target tasks whose KNN distance to the current state falls below the
selection threshold. Therefore, the number of counterfactual queries need not grow proportionally
with the size of the task set.
In settings where many trajectories strongly overlap around the current state, the computational bud-
get can be further controlled by reducing the KNN distance threshold δ, thereby retaining only the
strongest interfering trajectory priors. The selected counterfactual queries can also be evaluated in

E.2
GENERALITY ACROSS DIVERSE TRAJECTORY REGIMES
The strength and structure of trajectory priors may depend on the object-pose distribution used to
generate demonstrations. When object poses vary only slightly around fixed nominal locations,
demonstrations tend to form narrow and highly concentrated trajectory distributions. As object-
pose variation increases, the resulting trajectories become more dispersed, potentially changing the
interference patterns among tasks. A qualitatively different setting arises when object positions are
sampled over a broad workspace region without fixed nominal centers, where the resulting task
trajectories no longer follow the same object-centered spatial structure.
To evaluate whether CAS depends on a particular trajectory geometry, we consider two comple-
mentary variations of CALVIN-Custom-Switch. First, we construct three concentration-controlled
regimes, narrow, medium, and wide, by progressively increasing the object-pose perturbation range
around fixed nominal centers. Second, we construct a random regime in which object positions
are sampled over a broader workspace region without fixed nominal centers. For each regime, the
object-pose distribution used for training is aligned with that used for evaluation, so these experi-
ments isolate the effect of demonstration-trajectory structure rather than train–test distribution shift.
Fig. 16 visualizes the resulting trajectory distributions.

E.3
ROBUSTNESS TO SCENE APPEARANCE SHIFTS
Visual trajectory priors may be affected by the visual consistency between training and evaluation
environments. In our main experiments, the scene appearance at evaluation time matches that used
during training. To test whether CAS remains effective under visual appearance shifts, we construct
a separate scene-appearance-shift evaluation using π0.
As shown in Fig. 17, we create four appearance variants based on the CALVIN-Custom-Switch
scene. We denote the original training scene as D0 and the progressively darkened variants as D1–
D3. All four variants preserve the same task semantics, object layout, and scene geometry; only the
appearance of the wood-textured background surfaces is changed. The policy is trained only on D0
and evaluated on all four variants.
To isolate the effect of scene appearance, we construct a separate set of state-matched switching
episodes shared across D0–D3. Switching states are sampled at normalized reference progress u ∼
U[0.4, 0.6], corresponding to nominal reference steps [12, 18] on the same 30-step reference scale
used in CALVIN-Custom-Switch. For each scene variant, we evaluate all 12 ordered off-diagonal
source–target task pairs with 50 episodes per pair, yielding 600 switching episodes per variant.
The same underlying robot states, object configurations, source–target assignments, and sampled
reference-progress values are used across D0–D3; only the scene appearance differs. Success is
determined solely by completion of the target task, and we report the switch-only success rate over
these 600 episodes.
Table 18 reports the results.
The base π0 policy exhibits similar success rates across D0–D3,
ranging from 13.2% to 14.5%. CAS substantially improves switching performance in every scene
variant, with success rates remaining highly stable between 53.8% and 55.8% across appearance-
shift strengths. Notably, CAS achieves 55.0% on the original D0 scene and 53.8% even under the
strongest D3 shift.
These results show that the effectiveness of CAS does not rely on exact visual matching between the
training and evaluation scenes. Because the physical states, task assignments, and reference-progress
samples are matched across D0–D3, the consistent improvements indicate that counterfactual action
steering remains effective even when the visual appearance of the environment differs from that seen
during training.

E.6.2
HYPERPARAMETER ABLATIONS
We further study the effect of the three key hyperparameters in CAS: the number of nearest neigh-
bors K, the KNN distance threshold δ, and the steering strength λ. Specifically, K controls the
local neighborhood used to estimate the distance between the current end-effector position and each
task trajectory distribution, while δ determines which non-target trajectories are selected as potential
hijacking tasks. The steering strength λ controls the overall magnitude of the counterfactual correc-
tion applied to the target-conditioned action. When λ = 0, the counterfactual correction vanishes
and CAS reduces exactly to the base policy without action steering.
Unless otherwise specified, we use K = 30, δ = 0.1, and λ = 0.03 as the default setting, which
achieves a Switch SR of 45.4%. Starting from this configuration, we vary one hyperparameter at a
time while keeping the other two fixed.
As shown in Fig. 19, CAS maintains substantial improvements over the base policy across a broad
range of hyperparameter settings. Increasing λ from zero progressively strengthens counterfactual
steering and leads to substantial gains in switching performance. In contrast, the performance re-
mains highly stable across different choices of K, indicating that CAS does not require a precise
neighborhood size for estimating local trajectory proximity. Varying δ changes how selectively
CAS includes non-target trajectories as potential hijacking tasks, yet CAS remains substantially bet-
ter than the base policy across all tested values. The best tested δ achieves a Switch SR of 49.2%.
Overall, these results suggest that CAS is effective across a reasonably broad range of choices for
K, δ, and λ, rather than relying on a narrowly tuned hyperparameter configuration.

the manipulation less smooth. With CAS, the gripper redirects toward a more suitable position for
interacting with the plate and pushes it more smoothly toward the target location. Thus, although
both rollouts succeed, CAS yields a cleaner post-switch manipulation trajectory.
Correction direction and final-action norm.
To test the direction of the correction, we apply a =
a0 −(aCAS −a0), reversing the CAS correction while preserving its magnitude at a given observa-
tion. Success drops to 14.4% on LIBERO and 0.0% on CALVIN. To separately examine final-action
magnitude, we preserve the CAS output direction and rescale it as a = (∥a0∥2/∥aCAS∥2)aCAS,
with zero norms handled separately. This matches the joint six-dimensional motion-command norm
before native controller processing. The resulting success rates are 40.0% and 42.5%. The norm-
matched variant outperforms full CAS on LIBERO but underperforms it on CALVIN, suggesting
that the effect of action magnitude depends on the benchmark. Its performance on both benchmarks
indicates that increasing the final action norm alone is insufficient to explain the benefits of CAS.
Random counterfactual action interpolation.
As a simple interpolation baseline, we apply
a = (1 −β)a0 + βar with β = 0.5, using the same uniform non-target instruction sampling
rule. This yields only 8.9% success on LIBERO and 2.9% on CALVIN. Together, these compar-
isons support the importance of the content and direction of the counterfactual correction beyond
merely amplifying the target action or mixing it with an arbitrary non-target action.

BEYOND INSTRUCTION SWITCHING: TRAJECTORY-BIASED INITIAL
STATES
CAS is introduced in this work to address trajectory hijacking under mid-execution instruction
switching. However, interfering trajectory priors may also arise when the robot is initialized from
a configuration that is already strongly biased toward particular task trajectories. This raises a nat-

that is not induced by instruction switching? To study this complementary setting, we construct
trajectory-biased initial states and evaluate whether CAS can improve target-task execution from
these challenging initial configurations. As an auxiliary analysis beyond our primary instruction-
switching setting, we focus on the VLA policies OpenVLA-OFT, π0, and π0.5.

execution instruction switching, these results further show that successful CAS-guided trajectories

alone, even when the trajectory bias is already present in the initial robot state.

MID-EXECUTION INSTRUCTION SWITCHING EVALUATION DETAILS
Switch-boundary handling.
We define the switching step ts at the environment-action level. At
the switch, we discard any unexecuted source-conditioned actions in the current action chunk, and
the policy replans from the current observation under the target instruction. For LingBot-VA, we
additionally clear its cached temporal state to eliminate explicit carryover from the source phase.
LIBERO-Goal online evaluation.
We evaluate fully online instruction switching with ts ∈
{0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25}. At ts = 0, the policy executes the target instruction directly from the common
initial state. For ts > 0, it first executes the source instruction for exactly ts environment actions
and then switches to the target instruction.
For each ts, we evaluate all 90 ordered pairs of distinct source and target tasks and report the mean
target-task success rate. The initial state, simulator stabilization, 300-action target-phase budget,
and success criterion follow Appendix C.4.
CALVIN-Custom online evaluation.
The CALVIN-Custom results in Fig. 2 use an online
switching protocol, unlike the state-matched CALVIN-Custom-Switch protocol used for the main
method evaluation. We use the same four tasks as in CALVIN-Custom-Switch.

H.2
REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENT DETAILS
We provide additional implementation details for the real-robot experiments reported in Table 3.
The experiments are conducted on an AIRBOT Play 6-DoF robot arm equipped with a parallel-jaw
gripper. We evaluate the same mid-execution instruction-switching setting as in the main text, where
the robot is first instructed to lift one colored block and the instruction is then switched to lifting
the other block. We test both switching directions, blue-to-red and red-to-blue, at switching steps
ts = 20 and ts = 30, with 20 trials for each setting. For a fair comparison, the base policy and CAS
use matched object placements.
The deployed policy is a π0 flow-matching policy initialized from the π0 base checkpoint and fine-
tuned on our real-world block-lifting demonstrations. We train a single checkpoint jointly on two
tasks, pick and lift the red block and pick and lift the blue block. For each task, we collect 50 success-
ful demonstration trajectories using a leader-follower teleoperation setup. The policy is finetuned
for 60k gradient steps with a batch size of 32, using two NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The policy takes two
RGB observations as input, including a static third-person camera view and a wrist camera view.
Since the real-robot policy follows the standard π0 observation interface, unused camera inputs are
masked out.
Unlike the simulation experiments, where policies output delta end-effector actions, the real-robot
policy predicts delta joint actions for the six arm joints, together with a gripper command. We re-
construct absolute joint targets by adding the predicted joint deltas to the current joint configuration.
CAS is applied only to the arm-action dimensions, while the gripper command is directly taken from
the target-conditioned action. This follows the same principle as in simulation, where the gripper
dimension is excluded from counterfactual steering. The gripper command is then discretized into
open or close using a fixed threshold.
For CAS, hijacking tasks are selected using the same KNN-based trajectory-distance criterion as in
simulation. We use the KNN distance threshold δ = 0.03 for all real-world experiments. The final
CAS-corrected joint action is sent to the robot controller for execution.
Rationale for task selection.
We deliberately choose two object-specific block-lifting tasks to
provide a controlled evaluation of mid-execution instruction switching, rather than to benchmark
general manipulation complexity. First, block lifting is a relatively simple and well-defined manip-
ulation skill that can be reliably learned from demonstrations. This helps minimize failures arising
from insufficient competence in the underlying tasks, allowing us to focus more directly on the dif-
ficulty of redirecting ongoing behavior after an instruction change. Second, the tasks have clear and
objective success criteria: the robot must successfully lift the block specified by the updated instruc-
58


Preprint
tion. Compared with more complex or extended tasks, such as cloth folding or room cleaning, this
enables unambiguous evaluation of instruction-switching success.
Third, the two tasks involve blocks with identical geometry but different colors, and their instruc-
tions differ only in the target object. This design keeps the required manipulation skill and object
geometry consistent while testing whether the policy can distinguish between closely related instruc-
tions and redirect its actions toward the newly specified target. Finally, the experimental setup allows
careful control of physical conditions: for each corresponding Base–CAS comparison, we match
object positions and orientations as closely as possible, while varying object placements across
trials. In contrast, more complex manipulation tasks, such as cloth folding, involve deformable ob-
jects whose configurations are difficult to reproduce consistently between the corresponding Base
and CAS evaluations. Such variations can introduce additional confounding factors, making it harder
to attribute performance differences specifically to the instruction-switching intervention. Our use
of rigid blocks therefore enables more controlled and directly comparable evaluations of CAS’s
effectiveness.
Together with bidirectional instruction switching and evaluation at two execution stages, these de-
sign choices provide a focused physical validation of CAS while minimizing confounding factors
unrelated to instruction switching.

PCD IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
PCD comparison method.
Policy Contrastive Decoding (PCD) (Wu et al., 2026) is a training-free
inference-time method that contrasts the action distribution predicted from the original observation
with that predicted from an object-masked observation. The object-masked observation removes
task-relevant objects from the image, so the resulting contrast is intended to amplify actions that
depend on the task object rather than on spurious visual cues. We apply PCD to the flow-matching-
based policies π0 and π0.5 using KDE-based probabilistic modeling over sampled actions. We keep
the original PCD decoding procedure unchanged and set the contrast strength to α = 0.2, following
the setting used for π0 in the original paper.
Adapting PCD to our benchmarks.
The main adaptation needed in our setting is the construction
of object-masked observations. PCD requires identifying the task-relevant objects to be removed,
but our benchmarks provide only RGB observations and natural-language instructions at test time.
We therefore implement an instruction-grounded open-vocabulary detection pipeline. Given an in-
struction, we first extract object names using a rule-based parser tailored to the instruction patterns
in LIBERO-Goal. Single-object commands such as open X, push X, or pick up X return the manip-
ulated object; placement commands such as put X on Y or stack X on Y return both the manipulated
object and the receptacle; and compound instructions joined by “and” are split into sub-commands,
from which all referenced objects are collected.
The extracted object phrases are then normalized before being passed to the detector. We remove
articles and location modifiers after the object noun; for example, the bowl between the plate and
the ramekin is rewritten as bowl. Since bare LIBERO object names are often difficult for open-
vocabulary detectors to ground reliably, we further apply a small hand-built alias map that rewrites
them into more visually grounded queries, such as bowl to “black bowl”, plate to “white plate”,
block to “wooden cube”, and drawer-specific names to “drawer”. The resulting queries are sent to
Grounding DINO for object detection, followed by segmentation and inpainting to construct the
contrast observation. We also preserve the robot arm during masking, so that only task objects are
removed. If no valid object is parsed or detected at a step, we use the original observation as the
contrast observation, in which case PCD reduces to the base policy for that step.

Results.
SteerGen improves over the base policy at each of the four main switching times (Ta-
ble 41). Inference-time CAS achieves higher success rates at three times and matches SteerGen at
ts = 20, demonstrating its effectiveness without switching-specific policy training. At later switches
(Table 42), SRBC improves over SteerGen at all three times. Two-round CAS-ST further exceeds
SRBC by 15.6, 10.0, and 7.8 percentage points at ts = 40, 50, 60, respectively. These results sup-
port CAS both as an immediate inference-time intervention and as a source of useful supervision for
subsequent self-training.
