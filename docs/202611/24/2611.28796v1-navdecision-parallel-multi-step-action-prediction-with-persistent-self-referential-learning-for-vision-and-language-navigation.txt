Abstract
Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) requires timely de-
cisions based on instructions and visual observations.
However, autoregressive VLN models generate action de-
scriptions token by token, introducing substantial infer-
ence overhead. Decision models offer an efficient alterna-
tive but face challenges in maintaining short-horizon ac-
tion coherence and long-horizon goal alignment. To ad-
dress these challenges, we propose NavDecision, an effi-
cient multi-step VLN decision model. We introduce parallel
multi-step action prediction (PMAP) to predict multiple ac-
tion distributions from a shared vision-language state, and
memory-augmented self-referential policy optimization (M-
SRPO) to learn from unsuccessful trajectories using suc-
cessful trajectories as references. A cross-iteration success
memory further accumulates historical successful trajecto-
ries to provide informative training feedback. NavDecision
achieves success rates of 64.60% on R2R-CE and 64.57%
on RxR-CE with a 4B backbone. Compared with existing
autoregressive VLN methods, it delivers significant infer-
ence speedups of up to 22.28× and 18.96× while main-
taining comparable navigation performance.

Autoregressive

externally predicted candidates [35]. We therefore seek to
predict multi-step action distributions from a shared vision-
language state, capturing short-horizon temporal dependen-
cies for coherent execution at low inference cost.
Second, agents must track task progress under changing
observations to keep local actions aligned with long-horizon
goals. Autoregressive policies can scale test-time computa-
tion through explicit reasoning or search to improve navi-
gation success [14, 28]. To retain low inference overhead,
long-horizon exploration experience needs to be internal-
ized in the local policy during training. Online reinforce-
ment learning optimizes policies using task-level rewards
from complete navigation trajectories, including terminal
binary success rewards [19, 51]. However, decision models
lack explicit test-time computation scaling, making it dif-
ficult to consistently discover successful trajectories during
long-horizon exploration. Consequently, successful trajec-
tories may be scarce, and terminal binary success rewards
provide identical feedback for unsuccessful trajectories de-
spite differences in their progress toward the goal. This lim-
itation highlights the need for more informative trajectory-
level feedback to learn from unsuccessful trajectories and
improve local action policies.
We propose NavDecision, an efficient multi-step VLN
decision model trained with cross-iteration self-referential
reinforcement learning.

In-

we encode complete navigation trajectories with a frozen

tories as references, we combine bounded similarity-based

the current batch contains no successful trajectories and in-

This internalizes long-horizon exploration experience dur-
ing training while retaining fast action prediction at deploy-
ment. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We extend single-step option selection to parallel multi-
step action prediction. Using a shared vision-language
state representation, we learn short-horizon temporal de-
pendencies through temporally causal interactions among
prediction features and joint multi-step supervision. A
single forward pass outputs action distributions for multi-
ple steps, supporting coherent navigation actions and ef-
ficient decision-making.
• We design a reinforcement learning reward for decision
models that uses current-batch and historical successful
trajectories to provide more informative training feed-
back for unsuccessful trajectories. This internalizes long-
horizon exploration experience in the decision policy.
The world model and cross-iteration success memory are
used only during training and do not participate in action

2. Related Work
Vision-and-Language Navigation.
Vision-and-language
navigation has progressed from R2R and RxR bench-
marks to low-level action execution in continuous envi-
ronments [4, 24, 26]. Prior work improves generalization
through instruction synthesis, environment perturbations,
and large-scale data augmentation [14, 36, 37].
Cross-
modal pretraining and history encoding strengthen state
representations [8, 18, 20]; topological planning supports
global path reasoning [9], and waypoint prediction connects
planning with local continuous control [2, 21]. Navigation
methods based on large models explore explicit reasoning
and map-based planning [7, 28, 53, 54], as well as video-to-
action modeling and hierarchical control [10, 39, 45, 46].
Recent methods use action chunking to predict actions
across multiple time steps [19, 55], while dataset aggrega-
tion [32], reinforcement fine-tuning with expert-action su-
pervision [31], and multi-turn online reinforcement learn-
ing [19, 51] improve policies. Sequential autoregressive de-
coding introduces additional action latency. Although Nav-
Jev directly selects candidate waypoints to avoid autore-
gressive action generation, it relies on an external waypoint
predictor [35]. In contrast, we introduce parallel multi-step
action prediction over discrete primitive actions, eliminat-
ing sequential decoding and reducing model calls.
Decision Models. Decision models predict probability dis-
tributions over candidate options, enabling structured de-
cisions without autoregressive generation of action descrip-
tions [1]. Recent studies explore candidate probability read-
out, decision-distribution distillation, and batched decisions

3. Our Approach
As shown in Figure 2, we propose a multi-step deci-
sion model for vision-and-language navigation and a cross-
iteration self-referential policy optimization framework.
From a shared vision-language state representation, the
model predicts action distributions in parallel through tem-
porally causal interactions among prediction features. Dur-
ing reinforcement learning, current and historical success-
ful trajectories jointly define a trajectory-level reward for
training the model, allowing long-horizon navigation expe-
rience to improve the local action policy. The two compo-
nents support consecutive action execution over short hori-
zons and alignment with long-horizon goals, respectively.
Section 3.1 defines the navigation task and the decision
model. Section 3.2 presents parallel multi-step action pre-
diction and its supervised training, and Section 3.3 intro-
duces cross-iteration self-referential policy optimization.
3.1. Task Formulation
Vision-and-language navigation.
Given a natural-
language instruction I, an agent repeatedly selects actions
from visual observations to reach the specified goal and
stop [24]. At decision step t, let Ot denote the available
current and historical visual observations, and let Ht de-
note the history of executed primitive actions. The decision
context is xt = (I, Ot, Ht), and the primitive action space
is A = {STOP, FORWARD, LEFT, RIGHT}. Here, t
indexes model decisions, each of which may execute multi-
ple primitive actions. A VLM with an autoregressive action
interface formulates navigation action output as conditional
text generation: given xt, it generates an action response
token by token and parses the response into one or more ex-
ecutable actions [10, 40]. Let yt = (yt,1, . . . , yt,Nt) denote
a response with Nt tokens. Its probability factorizes as

Single-step decision model. A decision interface predicts
probabilities over candidate options from an input state, a
decision question, and the options [1, 27]. For VLN, the
input is xt, the question is “Which action should be exe-
cuted now to follow the navigation instruction?”, and the
candidate options are the primitive actions in A. The model
outputs their conditional probabilities. We instantiate this
interface as an action classification model built on a VLM:
the VLM encodes xt, and an action head produces a score

same navigation context and action space, this interface di-
rectly predicts probabilities over executable actions.
3.2. Parallel Multi-Step Action Prediction
Although single-step decision models remove token-by-
token decoding, each model call outputs only the current
primitive-action distribution. They do not explicitly model
short-horizon action sequences, and consecutive action ex-
ecution requires repeated model calls. We extend the model
to parallel multi-step action prediction with a prediction
horizon of K. Each position predicts over A, and the action

short-horizon temporal dependencies.

3.3. Memory-Augmented SRPO
With only terminal binary rewards, unsuccessful trajectories
with different levels of progress receive identical feedback.
Following the self-referential reward design of SRPO [13],
we evaluate unsuccessful trajectories against successful tra-
jectories in the world-model representation space. We ex-
tend this design with cross-iteration success memory to con-
tinually reuse available references and provide trajectory-
level training signals for the multi-step decision model.
Cross-iteration success memory.

drawn only from the current iteration are unavailable when

Datasets and metrics.

The

4.2. Main Results
Navigation performance.
Our direct multi-step policy
achieves competitive navigation success on both bench-
marks (Table 1). With the default execution cap E = 2, our
method obtains 64.60% SR and 54.31% SPL on R2R-CE,
and 64.57% SR and 52.84% SPL on RxR-CE. NavDecision
achieves performance comparable to the strong autoregres-
sive baseline FutureNav-4B [50], with only a 0.80% lower
SR on R2R-CE and a 3.67% higher SR on the more chal-
lenging RxR-CE benchmark. These results underscore that
our parallel decision framework preserves robust navigation
capabilities without the prohibitive computational overhead
of token-by-token decoding.
Inference efficiency. The model achieves these competi-
tive success rates while significantly reducing inference la-
tency (Table 2). With E = 2, the amortized model in-
ference time per executed primitive action is 55.10 ms on
R2R-CE and 62.17 ms on RxR-CE. This yields approxi-
mately 3.3× speedups even over the fastest timed baseline
on each benchmark. Compared with FutureNav-4B [50],
the corresponding speedups are 11.06× and 9.50×, respec-
6


tively, while retaining the competitive success rates dis-
cussed above.
These significant speedups are primarily
attributed to PMAP, which eliminates token-by-token de-
coding latency and maximizes computational efficiency by
sharing a single backbone pass across multiple actions.
Performance–efficiency trade-off.
With the prediction
horizon fixed at K = 4, the execution cap E controls
how frequently the policy acquires observations and re-
plans. Moving from E = 1 to E = 2 approximately halves
the amortized inference time, while changing SR by less
than 0.5 percentage points on either benchmark. Setting
E = 4 further reduces the amortized inference time to ap-
proximately 30 ms per primitive action, achieving around
10× speedup over the median inference time of the eval-
uated autoregressive baselines. This comes at the cost of
approximately 3.8 percentage points in SR compared with
E = 2 on both benchmarks, offering a substantially faster
execution mode with a moderate performance trade-off.

We evaluate the contributions of parallel multi-step action

Overall ablation. Table 3 compares the single-step SFT

trajectory-level policy optimization.
PMAP improves the success rate on both benchmarks,

performance. The full PMAP+M-SRPO achieves 64.60%

the effectiveness of trajectory-level reinforcement learning

5. Conclusion
We presented NavDecision, an efficient multi-step deci-
sion model for VLN that combines parallel action pre-
diction with cross-iteration self-referential reinforcement
learning. PMAP captures short-horizon action dependen-
cies, while M-SRPO leverages a frozen world model and
cross-iteration success memory to internalize long-horizon
exploration experience during training without additional
deployment-time inference modules. Experiments on R2R-
CE and RxR-CE demonstrate competitive navigation per-
formance with a 4B backbone, achieving 64.60% and
64.57% success rates, respectively, with up to 22.28× and
18.96× inference speedups.
