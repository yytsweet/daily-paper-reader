Intervention-Robust VLA:
Diverse Demonstrations for Robot Deployment with Safety Filters
Tzu-Yuan Huang1, Shao-Hsuan Chang1, Dai-Jie Wu2, Ralf R¨omer3, Yung-Hui Li4,
Stefan Sosnowski1, Angela P. Schoellig3,5, Kenneth Marino2 and Sandra Hirche1,5,6
Abstract— Vision-Language-Action
models
(VLAs)
have
shown strong potential for learning complex robotic behav-
iors from demonstrations. However, learning safe behavior
directly from data requires extensive coverage of safety-critical
situations, which is often impractical in real-world settings.
Test-time safety filters provide an alternative by correcting
unsafe actions during deployment, but these interventions can
drive the robot into states poorly covered by the training
data, thereby degrading task performance. We introduce InteR-
VLA, an intervention-robust framework for VLAs that lever-
ages diverse task-completing demonstrations to broaden task-
relevant state coverage and maintain competence under safety
interventions. By better aligning the training distribution with
safety-filtered deployment under the same data budget, our
approach mitigates intervention-induced distribution shift while
preserving task performance. Real-world experiments across
in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings show that InteR-
VLA maintains safety, increases Safe Success Rate up to 90%
from 0% for nominal policies, and consistently outperforms
nominal-data training on both evaluated VLAs.
I. INTRODUCTION
Vision–language–action
models
(VLAs)
[1]–[3]
have
emerged as a promising direction toward generalist robot
policies capable of performing diverse tasks from multi-
modal instructions. Building on vision–language foundation
models, VLAs map language instructions and visual obser-
vations to robot actions within an end-to-end framework.
Through imitation learning on expert demonstrations col-
lected via robot teleoperation [4] or portable interfaces such
as UMI [5], these models can acquire complex manipulation
behaviors without manually designing task-dependent con-
trollers. Recent VLAs have demonstrated increasingly broad
capabilities across objects, tasks, and environments, high-
lighting the potential of large-scale demonstration learning
for general-purpose robotic manipulation.
Despite this progress, deploying VLAs in real-world en-
vironments introduces a critical requirement beyond task
completion: safety. In particular, robots must avoid collisions
even when deployment conditions differ from those repre-
sented during training. Recent approaches [6], [7] address
this challenge by broadening the training distribution with

includes incorporating multiple unsafe and safety-critical
behaviors during training [8] and providing safety-critical
benchmark environments and datasets for learning safe be-

exposure to risky situations, but their effectiveness depends
on how well these scenarios cover deployment conditions and
may be further limited by sim-to-real gaps. This motivates
complementary test-time safety enforcement.
Safety filters [10], [11] provide such test-time enforcement
by modifying policy actions when necessary to satisfy safety
constraints. Control barrier functions (CBFs) [12], [13] are a
widely used realization, enabling real-time safety correction
without retraining the policy. This principle has been applied
to generative robot policies [14], [15] and extended to VLA
deployment using vision–language reasoning and perception
to derive safety constraints directly from the scene [16].
However, these test-time corrections can alter the policy’s
behavior and drive the robot toward out-of-distribution states,
as shown in Figure 1, thereby substantially degrading task
performance [17]. Path-consistent intervention can reduce
this mismatch by keeping execution close to the nominal
trajectory [17], but becomes restrictive when safe task com-
pletion requires deviating from the nominal path. Thus, ex-
isting inference-time safety mechanisms alone do not ensure
task completion after intervention. This limitation motivates
our key insight that intervention robustness can be improved
by broadening the distribution of task-relevant behaviors
represented in the demonstrations, as shown in Figure 1.
We introduce InteR-VLA (Intervention Robustness for
VLA), a framework for successful task completion which
accounts for deviations induced by safety-filter interven-
tions. InteR-VLA combines complementary roles: diverse
demonstrations preserve task competence under interven-
tion, while a safety filter enforces safety at deployment.
We propose and analyze two offline collection strategies:
diverse demonstrations, which complete tasks in different
ways without obstacles to broaden task-relevant coverage,
and multimodal avoidance demonstrations, which use path-
blocking obstacles to provide direct intervention coverage
when hazards are known during collection.
Our main contributions are:
• Analysis of intervention-induced covariate shift. We
analyze how demonstration coverage affects VLA robust-
ness to safety-filter interventions and characterize the shift
between training and safety-filtered deployment.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Vision-Language-Action Models and Safety Learning
VLAs [1]–[3] leverage pretrained vision–language repre-
sentations and large-scale robot demonstrations [18], [19]
to learn multimodal policies for diverse manipulation tasks.
These demonstrations naturally capture variations across
tasks, environments, embodiments, and task-completing be-
haviors [20], [21], from which VLAs acquire broad capa-
bilities such as cross-embodiment transfer [22] and dex-
terous manipulation [23]. However, since most VLAs are
trained through imitation learning, their performance remains
strongly dependent on the state–action coverage of the
demonstration data. To facilitate VLA deployment in safety-
critical scenarios, several works introduce safety objectives
during training [6] and optimize them through SafeRL,
commonly formulated as constrained Markov decision pro-
cesses (CMDPs) [24]. SafeVLA [8], for example, models
safety requirements and generates diverse unsafe and safety-
critical behaviors for SafeRL, while SafeLab [9] provides
safety-critical environments and safety learning frameworks.
These methods broaden safety-relevant training coverage, but
provide no hard constraint when deployment conditions fall
outside their training scenarios. Unlike these approaches,
InteR-VLA leaves safety to a test-time filter and uses demon-
strations to maintain competence under safety interventions.
B. Safety Filtering for Learned Robot Policies
Safety filters [10], [11] enforce constraints at deployment
by modifying nominal policy actions, often through online
optimization. Predictive safety filters [25] explicitly optimize
for safe actions, while control barrier functions (CBFs) [12]
provide safety conditions enforceable through lightweight
optimization. When applied to collision avoidance, CBFs are
commonly constructed from differentiable distances between
geometric approximations such as spheres [26] and ellip-
soids [27], while recent work learns barrier functions from
high-dimensional latent representations [28]. CBF constraints
have been integrated into generative robot policies [14],
[15], and VLSA [16] uses vision–language reasoning and
perception to construct explicit geometric constraints for
VLA filtering. However, safety interventions can deviate
from demonstrated actions and drive the system toward
underrepresented states. Path-consistent braking mitigates
this shift by preserving the nominal trajectory [17], but
cannot provide alternative task-completing motions when
the nominal path must be bypassed. Unlike path-consistent
braking, InteR-VLA lets the filter deviate from the nominal
path and maintains competence under safety interventions
by training on demonstrations that complete the task along
different task-space paths.
III. PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM SETTING
A. Imitation Learning with Vision-Language-Action Models
VLAs learn to execute language-specified tasks from a
demonstration dataset D = {S(n), A(n)}N
n=1. It encodes the
desired behavior for the task and contains N episodes with
state S(n) = {s(0), s(1),· · ·, s(T)}(n) and action A(n) =
{a(0), a(1),· · ·, a(T)}(n) sequence of length T. Each state
s(t) = {l, o(t)} ∈S belongs to the VLA state space S and
contains the language instruction l and current observation
o(t), typically comprising camera images and robot propri-
oception, e.g., joint angles. The action a(t) ∈A belongs
to the action space A and typically specifies a high-level
command such as the relative Cartesian displacement.
Given an expert dataset D, a VLA policy πθ(·|s(t))
parameterized by θ is trained through imitation learning
to model the conditional action distribution ρ(·|s(t)) rep-
resented by the demonstrations:
a(t) ∼πθ(·|s(t)) ≈ρ(·|s(t)).
(1)
Modern VLAs often predict actions in chunks of horizon
H [20], executing the first m ≤H actions before replanning
from the latest state, resulting in receding-horizon closed-
loop execution. However, imitation learning only reproduces
behaviors represented in D and does not explicitly enforce
safety at test time. Thus, even a well-trained VLA policy
πθ(·|s(t)) ≈ρ(·|s(t)) cannot guarantee safe execution.

C. Problem Statement
Our goal is to enable the robot to complete the commanded
task while maintaining safety, e.g., by avoiding collisions
with surrounding objects. Given a VLA policy πθ(· | s(t))
trained on a demonstration dataset D, the policy is executed
in closed loop until task termination. At each time step, an
action a(t) ∼πθ(· | s(t)) is sampled and executed, affecting
the evolution of the robot state x(t). Safe execution requires
x(t) to remain within the safe set defined in (4) at all times.
Let Esucc and Esafe denote the sets of rollouts achiev-

VLA policy with high probability of safe task success,
Prπθ(Esucc ∩Esafe) . When task-completing behaviors sat-
isfy the safety requirement in (4), safety and task success
need not conflict. Our objective is therefore to enforce safety
while preserving the task competence of the VLA policy.
IV. METHODOLOGY
Safety filters guarantee constraint satisfaction by modi-
fying actions at deployment time, but the resulting state
transitions can push the VLA away from the distribution rep-
resented by its demonstrations. We address this intervention-
induced covariate shift from the data-collection perspective
by allocating a fixed demonstration budget across diverse
task-completing behaviors rather than repeated nominal ex-
ecutions, thereby broadening task-relevant state coverage.
The demonstrations are collected offline and do not require
repeated rollout and retraining of the learned policy.

C. Intervention-Robust Data Collection
Since pS is unavailable during offline data collection,
we seek a general way to broaden task-relevant coverage
without targeting a specific deployment distribution. Our key
principle is to diversify along task-state dimensions that can
be altered by safety interventions. For collision avoidance,
this corresponds to collecting alternative task-completing
paths around the nominal trajectory. We instantiate the
demonstration scheme ν by collecting such diverse behaviors
under a fixed demonstration budget, increasing the likelihood
of covering states reached after safety-filter interventions.
We consider two collection strategies. Diverse demonstra-
tions vary the task-space path without path-blocking obsta-
cles, e.g., curved, left-offset, and right-offset motions, requir-
ing neither safety-critical scene construction nor deployment
of a policy or safety filter. This broadly covers alternative
states that may be reached when the nominal trajectory is
perturbed. Multi demonstrations use path-blocking obsta-
cles and collect multiple successful avoidance modes, e.g.,
above, left, and right, directly targeting task-space deviations
induced by collision-avoidance interventions when hazards
are known. In both cases, the VLA is trained offline on the
augmented dataset D′, while the safety filter is applied only
at deployment: demonstrations preserve task competence
under intervention, and the filter enforces safety.

c) Safety Constraint: For each task, an obstacle is
placed along the nominal shortest path to the task goal,
requiring the robot to deviate from its direct motion to
avoid collision. We use GroundingDINO [30] to localize the
obstacle in the RGB image and obtain its bounding box. The
corresponding depth measurements from the RGB-D camera
are then used to reconstruct the obstacle point cloud, which is
approximated by an ellipsoid. The robot end effector is sim-
ilarly represented by an ellipsoid. Following [16], the safety
constraint is defined through the separation between these
two ellipsoids, and the safety filter modifies the commanded
motion to prevent their intersection.
d) Evaluation Metrics: We evaluate each method using
the following metrics calculated from 20 rollouts and report
the mean and standard deviation:
• Safety: The fraction of episodes that remain collision-
free throughout the entire execution.
• Score: A progress score in [0, 1] that measures partial
task completion through predefined task milestones.
For Put sponge into basket, the milestones are: (i)
approach the sponge, (ii) grasp the sponge, (iii) move
the sponge to the basket, and (iv) place it inside the
basket. For Close the teacup drawer, the milestones
are: (i) approach the drawer, (ii) make contact with the
drawer, and (iii) push the drawer until closed.
• Success Rate (S-Rate): The fraction of episodes in
which the complete task is successfully accomplished.
• Safe Success Rate (SS-Rate): The fraction of episodes
in which the task is successfully completed while re-
maining collision-free throughout the entire execution.

C. Ablation Study
We conduct a series of ablation studies to better understand
how different factors affect safe task execution.
a) Effect of Reduced Dataset Size: We evaluate reduced
demonstration budgets. As shown in Figure 6, VLA-SF
(Multi) and VLA-SF (Diverse) perform worse as datasets
shrink. Yet, with only 50 further demonstrations, both still
exceed VLA-SF (Normal) in SS-Rate (0.60 & 0.50 vs.0.05).
b) Effect of Avoidance Modes: We vary avoidance
diversity during data collection using one mode (above), two
modes (left and right), and three modes (above, left, and
right) in Task 1. As shown in Figure 7-(a), SS-Rate increases
with more modes under the same data budget. Thus, the gain
in intervention robustness stems from behavioral diversity
rather than data quantity, consistent with our hypothesis that
additional modes broaden task-relevant coverage and overlap
more with states reached after safety-filter interventions.
c) Cross-Task Transfer: To assess whether InteR-VLA
can transfer intervention robustness across tasks, we train
π0.5 under the same data budget using 100 demonstrations
(Multi) from Task 1 and 100 demonstrations (Normal) from
Task 2, and evaluate on Task 2. As shown in Figure 7-(b), the
mixed dataset yields 0.65 SS-Rate, compared with 0.05 for
nominal-only training, indicating that diverse demonstrations
can improve intervention robustness across tasks.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced the InteR-VLA framework,
which leverages diverse demonstrations to reduce the dis-
tribution mismatch induced by safety filters. Our approach
separates the roles of training and deployment: safety filters
enforce safety at test time, while diverse demonstrations
broaden task-relevant coverage to preserve competence under
intervention. Across two tabletop manipulation tasks and ID
and OOD scenarios, InteR-VLA improves the Safe Success
Rate from 0% up to 90%. Under a fixed data budget,
increasing behavioral modes improves SS-Rate, showing
that diversity broadens coverage rather than merely adding
data, while removing the filter confirms that demonstrations
alone cannot ensure safety. These results support the use of
diverse demonstrations and test-time filtering as complemen-
tary components for robust, safe deployment. Future work
will explore more effective strategies for generating diverse
demonstrations and extend to broader robotic tasks.
