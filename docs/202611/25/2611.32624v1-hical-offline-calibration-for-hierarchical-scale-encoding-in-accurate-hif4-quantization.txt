# ABSTRACT 

Block floating-point (BFP) quantization is widely adopted for efficient low-bit large language model (LLM) inference, where the choice of block scales plays a critical role in quantization accuracy. HiFloat4 (HiF4) employs a hierarchical scale encoding scheme consisting of a global E6M2 scale and two levels of binary scale extensions. However, its conventional rounding rule and fixed scale-extension thresholds may not fully capture the statistical characteristics of the quantized tensors in practice, leading to suboptimal scale representations. In this work, we propose HiCal, an offline calibration approach that improves HiF4 quantization accuracy by refining the encoding decisions across all three scale levels. Specifically, we introduce a calibrated rounding bias for the Level-1 E6M2 scale and optimize the scale-extension thresholds at Levels 2 and 3 based on the empirical distributions of local group magnitudes. The resulting calibration parameters are determined offline and remain fixed during inference, while the hierarchical encoding continues to adapt to individual blocks through their local values. This design eliminates the need for per-block multi-candidate scale search while preserving the original deterministic HiF4 encoding procedure. Experiments across five synthetic and real activation distributions show that the proposed method reduces quantization MSE by 9.91%–23.50% relative to the original HiF4 scheme, including a 13.00% reduction on real Qwen3-8B activations. End-to-end evaluations across six benchmarks further show average-score improvements of 0.91 and 1.25 percentage points on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, respectively, without per-block multi-candidate search during inference. 

# 1 Introduction 

The growing computational and memory demands of large language models (LLMs) have made low-bit quantization an important technique for efficient model inference. Recent advances in low-precision numerical formats, including FP8 and FP4 representations, have enabled substantial reductions in model storage and data-transfer costs [1, 2]. In particular, block floating-point (BFP) and microscaling formats, such as MXFP4 and NVFP4, use shared scaling metadata to represent groups of low-precision elements [3, 4]. These formats provide a practical way to extend the numerical range of low-bit representations while maintaining hardware-friendly computation. However, at the 4-bit level, the quantization accuracy becomes highly sensitive to the selection and encoding of scaling factors, since even small changes in the scale can substantially affect the reconstructed values within a block. Conventional BFP quantization generally initializes the block scale according to the maximum absolute value of the block. Although this max-based strategy prevents overflow and fully utilizes the available quantization range, it does not necessarily minimize the overall quantization–dequantization (QDQ) error. This limitation has motivated recent studies on more effective block-scale selection strategies. Four Over Six introduces an adaptive scaling method that evaluates two candidate scale factors for each block and selects the one that produces lower quantization error [5]. ScaleSearch further explores fine-grained search over neighboring representable scale values to identify scales that minimize block-level reconstruction error [6]. More recently, SOAR proposes analytical optimization of global and block-wise scales, together with a decoupled scale-search strategy, while H-Scale uses a diagonal second-order approximation derived from calibration activations to refine per-group scales [7, 8]. These methods demonstrate that optimizing the scale-selection process beyond conventional max-based initialization can improve low-bit quantization accuracy, arXiv:2611.32624v1 [cs.LG] 24 Nov 2026 while introducing different optimization objectives and calibration procedures. In addition to scale selection, activation outliers remain a major obstacle for low-bit microscaling formats. MX+ addresses this by retaining outlier information outside the standard BFP representation [9], while AMXFP4 uses an asymmetric FP4 format to absorb activation outliers without calibration [10]. Channel-wise outlier separation has also been explored for W4A4 quantization [11]. Other studies have investigated the interaction between microscaling granularity, scale representation, and quantization accuracy, and have examined how activation outliers interact with these choices [9, 10, 11]. For example, recent analyses show that reducing the block size does not always improve model accuracy, as the resulting behavior depends on the statistical properties of the tensor distribution and the dynamic range of the scaling format [12, 13]. In addition, format-specific post-training quantization methods, such as Micro-Rotated-GPTQ, combine block-wise Hadamard transformations with FP4-oriented optimization to address the limitations of existing microscaling formats [14]. These studies highlight that low-bit quantization accuracy depends not only on the element-level codebook, but also on the interaction among scale representation, scaling granularity, and the encoding procedure. HiFloat4 (HiF4) is a 4-bit block floating-point format that employs a three-level hierarchical scaling structure to capture magnitude variations at different granularities [15]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, each HiF4 block contains 64 four-bit S1P2 elements and 32 bits of shared scaling metadata, corresponding to an average storage cost of 4.5 bits per element. The metadata consists of one global E6M2 base scale at Level 1, eight 1-bit scale extensions ( E8) at Level 2, and sixteen 1-bit scale extensions ( E4) at Level 3. The two levels of scale extensions provide progressively finer adjustments for groups of eight and four elements, respectively. Accordingly, the effective scale of the i-th element can be expressed as 

Si = Sbase · 2E8[⌈i/ 8⌉]+ E4[⌈i/ 4⌉], i = 1 , . . . , 64 , (1) where Sbase denotes the E6M2 base scale, and e(8) and e(4) represent the corresponding binary scale extensions. In the original HiF4 encoding procedure, the base scale is initialized through max-based normalization and E6M2 encoding, while the Level-2 and Level-3 scale extensions are determined by fixed magnitude thresholds. This hierarchical design provides flexible local scale adaptation with compact metadata, but its final quantization accuracy also depends on how the encoding decisions at the three levels are configured. A key characteristic of HiF4 is that its three scale levels are coupled. The Level-1 E6M2 base scale determines the normalization of local group magnitudes, which subsequently affects the Level-2 and Level-3 scale-extension decisions. Therefore, changing the Level-1 rounding rule may alter not only the global scale but also the assignment of the two subsequent levels of scale extensions. Meanwhile, the two magnitude thresholds that decide the Level-2 and Level-3 scale-extension bits may not be optimal for the tensors being quantized. These observations motivate a systematic investigation of the scale-encoding decisions across the complete HiF4 hierarchy, rather than optimizing the element-level codebook or an individual scale factor in isolation. In this work, we propose HiCal, a lightweight, offline-calibrated approach to improve the scale encoding accuracy of HiF4. Instead of performing multi-candidate scale evaluation independently for every block during quantization, we calibrate a compact set of shared encoding parameters offline, following the offline-calibration paradigm of prior post-training quantization methods [16], and apply them as fixed rules. Specifically, we introduce a rounding bias for the Level-1 E6M2 base scale and recalibrate the scale-extension thresholds at Levels 2 and 3 according to the empirical distributions of local group magnitudes. The calibration is performed sequentially: the Level-1 rounding bias is first selected, and the Level-2 and Level-3 thresholds are subsequently calibrated with the selected bias fixed. The resulting configuration uses αrnd = −0.35 , T H 8 = 4 .25 , and T H 4 = 2 .125 . During quantization, the selected parameters are directly applied to each block, while the actual hierarchical scale metadata continues to be determined from its local values. Thus, the proposed approach retains the original HiF4 hierarchical representation and S1P2 element codebook, without requiring per-block multi-candidate scale evaluation. We evaluate the proposed method using five synthetic and real-world activation distributions for scale-encoding analysis, and conduct end-to-end experiments on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B in which both weight and activation tensors are quantized with the same HiF4 format. The results show that the proposed calibration reduces QDQ mean squared error (MSE) by 9.91%–23.50% relative to the original HiF4 scheme, including a 13.00% reduction on real Qwen3-8B activations. Furthermore, evaluations on six downstream benchmarks show that the six-task mean accuracy improves by 0.91 and 1.25 percentage points for Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, respectively. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We characterize the hierarchical scale-encoding mechanism of HiF4 and analyze how the Level-1 base-scale rounding and the Level-2/Level-3 scale-extension decisions affect the final QDQ error. • We propose a lightweight, offline-calibrated approach that sequentially determines the Level-1 E6M2 rounding bias and the Level-2/Level-3 scale-extension thresholds. 2.1 HiF4 Architecture and Scale-Encoding Decisions

As illustrated in Fig. 1, each HiF4 block contains 64 four-bit elements and 32 bits of shared scale metadata, consisting of one E6M2 global scale factor ( SF ), eight 1-bit mid-level scale extensions ( E8), and sixteen 1-bit fine-level scale extensions ( E4). # 3 Proposed HiF4 Quantization Algorithm 

Based on the calibration results in Sections 2.2 and 2.3, we instantiate the HiF4 quantizer with three fixed parameters: 

αrnd = −0.35 , T H 8 = 4 .25 , and T H 4 = 2 .125 . The procedure follows the original data organization and reconstruc-tion formulation in Section 2.1. In particular, the 64-element block is partitioned into sixteen 4-element groups and eight 8-element parent groups, whose peak values V4[i], V8[k], and Vmax are defined in Eqs. (2)–(4). The S1P2 element codebook and reconstruction operation in Eq. (12) are kept unchanged. The proposed method introduces three modifications to the scale-control parameters of the original HiF4 quantization path. At Level-1, we introduce a fixed rounding bias of αrnd = −0.35 instead of the original unbiased setting (αrnd = 0 ). At Level-2 and Level-3, we adjust the scale-extension thresholds to T H 8 = 4 .25 and T H 4 = 2 .125 ,respectively. The remaining quantization operations follow the original HiF4 path. Algorithm 1 gives the corresponding block-wise procedure using the definitions and reconstruction equations from Section 2.1. Inline comments distinguish the three proposed changes from the operations retained from the original HiF4 path. After mantissa-range correction and the original E6M2 range handling, the resulting dSF is used by both hierarchy levels. The effective extension and scale DE i and Si are exactly those in Eq. (10); the output codes are reconstructed by Eq. (12). Therefore, the proposed method changes only the three calibrated scale-control parameters and retains the original HiF4 grouping, metadata hierarchy, and element-wise codebook. # 4 Accuracy Evaluation and Results 

We evaluate three numerical configurations—FP16 reference, original HiF4, and the proposed HiF4 Scale-bias method— on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B [18] across six benchmarks: MMLU [19], GSM8K [20], HumanEval [21], MBPP [22], ARC-Challenge (ARC-C) and ARC-Easy (ARC-E) [23]. To reduce measurement noise, each reported score is averaged over multiple independent evaluation runs, and the Mean column reports the arithmetic average over the six benchmark means. In all quantized configurations, both weight and activation tensors are quantized with the HiF4 format using the same scale-encoding parameters; the FP16 reference is used only as the accuracy baseline. At the quantization level (Table 1), the proposed configuration reduces QDQ MSE by 23.50% for Uniform, 9.91% for Gaussian, 11.25% for Uniform with outliers, 12.41% for Gaussian with outliers, and 13.00% on Qwen3-8B real activations, relative to original HiF4. These reductions decompose into a Level-1 rounding-bias contribution and an additional hierarchical-threshold contribution from (T H 8, T H 4) = (4 .25 , 2.125) , indicating that the calibrated parameters lower tensor-level reconstruction error across all five evaluated distributions. Table 2 reports the end-to-end benchmark results. For Qwen3-8B, the six-benchmark mean improves from 72.29% under original HiF4 to 73.20% under the proposed method, reducing the FP16 gap from 2.31 pp to 1.40 pp. For Qwen3-30B-A3B, the mean improves from 79.01% to 80.26%, reducing the FP16 gap from 2.39 pp to 1.14 pp. The proposed method improves the six-benchmark mean for both evaluated model sizes and reduces the accuracy loss relative to their FP16 references. The QDQ and benchmark results are complementary. The calibrated parameters reduce reconstruction error across synthetic and real activations, and this reduction is accompanied by improved six-task mean accuracy on both evaluated models. 

4.1 Hardware Friendliness 

The three calibrated parameters refine the HiF4 scale-encoding rules without requiring new hardware operations. At the instruction level, the Level-1 rounding bias is realized by a single constant added to the normalized E6M2 mantissa before the existing rounding step, and the Level-2 and Level-3 thresholds replace the immediate constants in the existing magnitude comparisons. Both operations are covered by the floating-point add, multiply, compare, and rounding instructions that are already present in general-purpose inference pipelines; no new instruction category, operand format, or data path is required. The three calibrated values ( αrnd = −0.35 , T H 8 = 4 .25 , and T H 4 = 2 .125 ) are compile-time constants and do not require additional runtime storage beyond the existing HiF4 scale metadata. Only customized instruction extensions or fused operators that hard-wire the original rounding constant or threshold values may require minor adjustment to 9expose the new parameters, and even in that case the change is limited to substituting the affected constants rather than redesigning the arithmetic pipeline; this is consistent with recent HiF4 deployment efforts on Ascend NPUs [24]. This single-path property distinguishes the proposed method from per-block scale-search approaches such as ScaleSearch [6], which evaluate multiple candidate scales for each block and therefore require one quantization path per candidate. In a hardware implementation, such per-block search either replicates the quantization datapath or serializes the candidate evaluations, both of which increase area or latency proportionally to the number of candidates. The proposed method avoids this cost entirely: all three scale-encoding parameters are fixed after offline calibration, and each block is quantized through a single deterministic path. 

4.2 Discussion 

The quantization-level and end-to-end results provide complementary evidence for the proposed scale-calibration method, and the hardware implications discussed above confirm that these accuracy gains do not require new arithmetic support. The calibrated parameters reduce reconstruction error on synthetic distributions and real Qwen3-8B activations, and this reduction is accompanied by improved six-task mean accuracy on both Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B. A lower tensor-level MSE does not necessarily translate into an equal improvement on every benchmark, because the six benchmarks have different sensitivities to quantization noise and different inherent evaluation variance; the consistent mean-accuracy gains across two model sizes nevertheless indicate that the calibration is useful beyond the local QDQ metric. The proposed method occupies a different point in the accuracy–cost trade-off than per-block scale-search approaches such as ScaleSearch [6]. ScaleSearch improves quantization accuracy by searching over neighboring representable scales for each block, at the cost of evaluating multiple quantization candidates at inference time. The proposed method instead recovers most of the accuracy gain achievable by per-block scale selection while fixing the encoding parameters to a single calibration-derived value, so that the deployed quantizer retains a deterministic single-pass structure. This trade-off is particularly relevant for hardware accelerators, where the cost of per-block multi-candidate evaluation scales with the number of candidates rather than being amortized across blocks. The method retains the original HiF4 hierarchical grouping and S1P2 element codebook. Its changes are limited to the Level-1 rounding bias and the Level-2/Level-3 scale-extension thresholds; these are selected through offline calibration and applied as fixed rules during quantization, without per-block enumeration of multiple scale candidates. This keeps the quantization procedure deterministic and preserves the existing HiF4 representation structure. The transferability of the calibrated parameter set to other model architectures and activation distributions remains to be studied. 

# 5 Conclusion 

This paper presents a deterministic scale-calibration method that improves HiF4 quantization accuracy by refining the scale-encoding decisions at all three levels. The final configuration uses αrnd = −0.35 , T H 8 = 4 .25 , and 

T H 4 = 2 .125 . These parameters are determined through offline calibration and are applied directly during quantization, without per-block enumeration of multiple scale candidates. The original HiF4 hierarchical organization and S1P2 element codebook are retained. The quantization-level evaluation demonstrates that the combined calibration reduces QDQ MSE across five tested activation distributions, including real Qwen3-8B activations. The end-to-end evaluation further shows that the six-benchmark mean accuracy improves from 72.29% to 73.20% for Qwen3-8B and from 79.01% to 80.26% for Qwen3-30B-A3B. Correspondingly, the accuracy gaps to the FP16 references decrease from 2.31 pp to 1.40 pp and from 2.39 pp to 1.14 pp, respectively. These results indicate that calibrating the hierarchical scale representation can improve the average end-to-end accuracy of HiF4 across the evaluated models.
