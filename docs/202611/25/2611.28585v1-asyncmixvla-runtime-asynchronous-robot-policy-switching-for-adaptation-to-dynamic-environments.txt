# AsyncMixVLA: Runtime Asynchronous Robot Policy Switching for Adaptation to Dynamic Environments 

Tamoghno Das, Keyon Jazayeri, Yerin Cho, Nazmul Takbir, Arshia Aravinthan, Nikil D. Dutt 

Abstract —Vision-language-action (VLA) models translate vi-sual observations and language instructions into robot actions, enabling general-purpose robotic control. However, environmen-tal perturbations, such as objects moving during execution, can undermine their reliability. Different policies exhibit comple-mentary failures under mid-rollout perturbations, motivating selective switching between policies for recovery. Such switching can stall the robot during inference of the newly invoked policy or, if performed asynchronously, act on stale observations. We present AsyncMixVLA, an adaptive inference framework for proactive, asynchronous switching across heterogeneous VLA policies. A learned trigger balances recovery potential against intervention harm to selectively escalate to a higher-compute policy. AsyncMixVLA overlaps its inference with committed outgoing actions and aligns its context to the anticipated takeover state. On LIBERO-Long benchmark augmented with mid-rollout perturbations, AsyncMixVLA improves perturbed task success from 69.5% to 77.1% over the lightweight policy. It achieves 84.9% overall success, comparable to 84.5% for synchronous switching, while reducing median exposed handoff latency from 226.5 ms to 0.1 ms. Without retraining or recalibration, it achieves 75.1% success across three unseen perturbation families, com-pared with 71.3% for the stronger constituent policy in aggregate. 

Index Terms —VLA models, Physical AI, asynchronous infer-ence, policy switching, model routing, robustness 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models map visual obser-vations and language instructions to robot actions, enabling a single policy to perform diverse manipulation tasks [1]– [3]. However, strong nominal performance does not guarantee reliable execution under deployment shifts: VLA policies remain sensitive to changes in adjoining environment [4]. Mid-task perturbations are particularly challenging because they can invalidate the state on which ongoing actions were generated. Robust deployment therefore requires both effective recovery and timely delivery of corrective actions. As shown in Fig. 1, heterogeneous VLA policies can exhibit complementary failure modes, motivating selective switching rather than continuous use of a single policy, or switching to a compressed version of the same policy. Table I confirms this empirically: VLA-Adapter [5] recovers substantially more OpenVLA-OFT [6] failures than the same-family OFT-Exit16 (Early exit [7] applied to OpenVLA-OFT) variant. We there-fore use VLA-Adapter as the default policy and selectively invoke OpenVLA-OFT for recovery. Policy switching, however, introduces a systems challenge. Launching OFT asynchronously can hide this computation behind remaining committed Adapter actions, but the robot continues moving during inference, making the launch-time context stale at takeover. Heterogeneous switching must therefore determine both when to intervene and how to transfer control without blocking or context misalignment. We present AsyncMixVLA , a runtime framework for proac-tive, asynchronous switching between heterogeneous VLA arXiv:2611.28585v1 [cs.RO] 21 Nov 2026 policies. A learned intervention trigger selectively escalates from VLA-Adapter to OpenVLA-OFT. Once accepted, the remaining Adapter actions form a committed-action bridge 

that executes while OFT preparation proceeds asynchronously. AsyncMixVLA then aligns the incoming policy’s propriocep-tive and visual context to the anticipated takeover state using controller-calibrated state roll-forward and an action-consistent visual-token residual. When the environment changes mid-execution, the runtime must decide whether another policy is more likely to complete the task from the current trajectory and, if so, prepare that policy for the state it will inherit. 

Unlike prior asynchronous VLA execution methods that pri-marily maintain temporal consistency within a single deployed policy [8]–[10], AsyncMixVLA couples selective transfer between independently trained policies with asynchronous, context-aligned handoff. Our contributions are: 1) Complementarity-driven heterogeneous switching. 

We characterize complementary failures of indepen-dently trained VLA policies and introduce a learned rescue-versus-harm trigger for selective escalation. 2) Proactive asynchronous handoff with context align-ment. We overlap incoming-policy inference with com-mitted outgoing actions and align proprioceptive and visual context to the anticipated takeover state. 3) End-to-end robustness, responsiveness, and gen-eralization. Across 238 LIBERO-Long episodes, AsyncMixVLA improves perturbed success from 69.5% to 77.1% over VLA-Adapter, achieves 84.9% overall success versus 84.5% for synchronous switching, and re-duces median handoff latency Lhandof f from 226.5 ms to 0.1 ms . Without retraining or recalibration, it achieves 

75.1% success across 289 episodes spanning three unseen perturbation families, showing generalization to unseen perturbations. II. R ELATED WORK 

VLA robustness and efficient inference. Robustness studies such as LIBERO-Plus [4] and RobustVLA [11] document VLA sensitivity to environmental and observation perturba-tions. Efficient inference reduces policy cost through com-pact backbones [5], [12], layer/token pruning and feature caching [13]–[15], parallel action decoding [6], and adaptive early exit [7]. AsyncMixVLA complements these optimiza-tions by addressing when to invoke a higher-compute policy during execution. It exploits complementary outcomes across independently trained policies while retaining lightweight in-ference when escalation is unnecessary. 

Heterogeneous policy routing. RoboRouter [16] selects poli-cies for tasks using semantic representations and accumu-lated execution experience. Pan et al. [17] perform event-based transitions between VLA and diffusion controllers for language-guided positioning and dexterous grasping. These works establish precedents for heterogeneous selection and switching during execution. AsyncMixVLA focuses on inter-vention under disturbances: learned rescue-versus-harm utility decides whether to transfer control from the current state, and asynchronous handoff reduces the transfer’s exposed cost. 

Asynchronous policy execution. Action chunking [18] amor-tizes inference but delays incorporation of new observations; asynchronous execution [12] overlaps prediction with ongoing control. Real-time chunking (RTC) [8] preserves commit-ted actions through inpainting to maintain chunk continuity. VLASH [9] conditions predictions on rolled-forward robot states and incorporates temporal offsets during fine-tuning. F2F-AP [10] uses predicted object flow to anticipate obser-vations and a contrastive objective to align visual features. These methods address temporal consistency for a deployed policy. AsyncMixVLA shares their need to account for motion during inference, while addressing selective handoff between independently trained controllers. Its incoming policy is con-ditioned on the context expected after the outgoing policy’s committed actions. Visual alignment is trained through action consistency: the frozen incoming policy’s outputs under pre-dicted and true handoff contexts are matched, rather than di-rectly matching visual features. AsyncMixVLA thus combines intervention-utility routing, preparation during committed out-going actions, and alignment to the incoming controller’s anticipated takeover context. III. A SYNC MIX VLA

Suppose acceptance occurs at step t, and p is the next unexecuted position in the Adapter chunk generated at step 

r. The committed bridge is 

Bt = [ aAp|r , . . . , a AH−1|r ], |B t| = H − p. (3) Every action in this suffix executes. The bridge is fixed at 

Tpredict , and no additional Adapter chunks are generated to extend it. Concurrently, the preparation path constructs ˆxswitch 

(Sec. III-D) and obtains the OFT response. An early response waits for bridge completion; a late response exposes a residual wait before takeover. A longer committed bridge gives OFT preparation more time to overlap with execution, but postpones the earliest possible OFT action even if the response is ready. After a disturbance, the remaining Adapter actions may no longer suit the changed scene. Takeover-context alignment accounts for the motion induced by these actions, but does not revise them. Let Tready denote response readiness, Tswitch completion of the final bridge action, and Taction issuance of the first OFT action. The robot-visible handoff latency is 

Lhandoff = Taction − Tswitch . (4) We count a handoff as stall-free (next actions ready before bridge action completion) when Tready ≤ Tswitch + Tctrl ,where Tctrl is one control period. Overlap hides context preparation and inference behind useful Adapter execution without reducing OFT’s intrinsic inference time. 

Initial takeover and replanning. The initial prefetched OFT chunk contains eight actions. The deployed configuration executes its first k = 4 actions (200 ms of control steps), discards the remaining four, and queries OFT from a fresh ob-servation. This limits open-loop execution from the predicted context while using multiple prefetched actions. The truncation applies only to the initial takeover; subsequent execution uses ordinary OFT replanning, with no return to Adapter.

Environment and perturbations. We evaluate on the ten long-horizon tasks of LIBERO-Long [19] at 20 Hz ( Tctrl =50 ms), with at most Tmax = 520 environment actions, 7-D actions, and the native H = 8 action horizon. The 20-Hz rate denotes the simulation control timestep rather than enforced wall-clock pacing: simulation state advances only through executed env.step() actions. It therefore continues to evolve during committed bridge actions but does not evolve during residual wall-clock waiting after bridge completion. We report this residual as exposed handoff wait while evaluating task recovery under action-synchronous dynamic perturba-tions. Frozen VLA-Adapter and OpenVLA-OFT checkpoints serve as default and recovery policies; only the trigger and alignment components are trained or calibrated. 

Perturbation Benchmark design (238 cases): The primary benchmark contains 120 clean episodes (12 fixed initial states 

× 10 tasks) and 118 feasible matched perturbed counterparts (two excluded by feasibility constraints), giving 238 total. The failure-overlap characterization in Sec. I uses a separate set of 500 matched perturbed rollouts, with no clean episodes, solely to measure episode-level policy complementarity; it is distinct from this final 238-episode evaluation. Perturbations displace a task-relevant movable source object (e.g., juice can) by 5 cm in-plane over five control steps. A frozen manifest fixes object, onset, direction, magnitude, and duration across methods. The perturbation hook precedes every environment action, including bridge and post-takeover actions, so overlap-ping disturbances continue through handoff. All methods share seeds, checkpoints, action processing, manifests, and horizons. 

Training and implementation. The trigger is trained offline and frozen before evaluation. V1 uses 64 ×64 front/wrist RGB images with temporal differences at lags {1, 4, 16 }; calibration on trials 32–39 selects τ1 = 0 .92296 with persistence one. V2 uses 199 fit examples from trials 12–31, calibration trials 32–39, and development trials 40–49. Matched continuations from the same causal prefix either retain Adapter or invoke the then-frozen AsyncMixVLA handoff; binary outcomes define 

∆y = yM −yA, with +1 , −1, and 0 denoting rescue, harm, and tie. Ties are retained; regularized logistic rescue/harm heads use no class balancing, with features standardized using fixed training statistics and clipped to [−10 , 10] . Calibration selects 

λ = 0 .1 and τ2 = −0.1 to maximize paired success while retaining at least 90% of direct-V1 rescues without increasing harms; ties favor fewer switches, then stronger regularization. We fit V2 on first intervention candidates using outcomes from paired continuations that either retain Adapter or invoke the AsyncMixVLA handoff. In SectionV-A, we deploy the frozen V2 with continuous candidate re-arming and execute the first k = 4 actions of OFT’s initial chunk at takeover. V2 outputs serve as intervention-utility scores. Proprioceptive alignment uses gains gpos = 0 .252 , gori = 0 .240 , and ggripper = 0 .Visual alignment concatenates mean-pooled 4096-D OFT to-kens with a 22-D bridge descriptor (mean/first/last actions and normalized length) and uses a two-layer MLP of hidden width 64 to predict a shared 4096-D residual, trained with Eq. 7 while OFT remains frozen. The visual-alignment predictor is fitted on 1,668 deploy-matched bridge samples from trials 12–39 across all ten LIBERO-Long tasks. An additional 597 samples from trials 40–49 are reserved for development, while the final 238-episode evaluation uses disjoint trials 0–11. 

Baselines. We compare Adapter-only and OFT-only execution with synchronous switching, naive asynchronous switching, and AsyncMixVLA. All switching methods share identi-cal frozen trigger decisions, committed bridges, and k =4 takeover horizons, without generating additional Adapter chunks to extend the bridge. Synchronous switching queries fresh context after bridge completion; naive asynchronous switching starts preparation at Tpredict using trigger-time context; AsyncMixVLA starts at the same boundary and constructs the aligned takeover context before querying OFT. 

Metrics and timing. We report overall, clean, and perturbed task success rates; Adapter failures rescued and successes retained; and the fraction of episodes invoking OFT. OFT invocation rate measures intervention selectivity, not compute or energy savings, since OFT may execute for the remainder of an episode after takeover. Responsiveness uses median/ p95 

handoff latency (Eq. 4) and the no-stall criterion of Sec. III-C. All timing experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, with VLA-Adapter and OpenVLA-OFT using BF16 inference as separate single-worker services within the same GPU allocation. Thus, asynchronous measurements include GPU contention between the concurrently resident policies. Under the final learned trigger, latency statistics are computed over the same 109 handoffs for each switching method. Handoff latency is measured in wall-clock time, independently of the simulator’s action-synchronous progression. Table V shows that policy complementarity persists un-der these unseen conditions: neither constituent policy domi-nates across all three perturbation families. OpenVLA-OFT is strongest on object rotation (85.6%), whereas VLA-Adapter substantially exceeds it under target displacement (70.0% vs. 58.3%). AsyncMixVLA achieves the highest observed SR on target displacement (73.3%) and post-grasp slip (65.8%), while remaining close to OpenVLA-OFT on rotation (84.8% vs. 85.6%). Across all 289 unseen-perturbation episodes, AsyncMixVLA reaches 75.1% success, compared with 71.3% for VLA-Adapter and 69.2% for OpenVLA-OFT. These results suggest that the selective heterogeneous cascade transfers be-yond the translation disturbance used for development without retraining or recalibration. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented AsyncMixVLA, a runtime framework for selective asynchronous switching between heterogeneous VLA policies. The approach is motivated by complementary failure behavior between VLA-Adapter and OpenVLA-OFT, uses a continuous learned rescue-versus-harm trigger to decide when escalation is worthwhile, overlaps OpenVLA-OFT preparation with already committed VLA-Adapter actions, and aligns the incoming policy’s proprioceptive and visual context to the anticipated handoff state. On the frozen learned-trigger evaluation, AsyncMixVLA reaches 84.9% overall and 77.1% perturbed success while retaining 185/193 VLA-Adapter successes. Its median ex-posed handoff wait is 0.1 ms, compared with 226.5 ms for synchronous switching, while zero-shot experiments show improved aggregate success across three unseen perturba-tion families. These results show that proactive asynchronous handoff can substantially reduce typical exposed switching latency while preserving the robustness benefit of selective heterogeneous policy execution.
