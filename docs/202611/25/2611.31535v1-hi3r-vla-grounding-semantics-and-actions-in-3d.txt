Abstract
While recent vision-language-action (VLA) models in-
creasingly incorporate 3D geometry, how to effectively ex-
ploit it for precise and generalizable action prediction re-
mains a central challenge. We introduce Hi3R-VLA, a 3D
policy that grounds heterogeneous modalities - vision, lan-
guage, and action - in explicit 3D geometry for robotic ma-
nipulation. Our model integrates spatially-aligned visual
features and language conditioning into point cloud rep-
resentations, enriching scene geometry with fine-grained,
task-relevant semantics.
It then anchors low-level con-
trol commands in the same 3D coordinate frame and uses
3D-RoPE enhanced action-to-point attention over coarse-
to-fine point features to model geometric relationships be-
tween action targets and observed points.
Our analysis
demonstrates the importance of semantic fusion within 3D
and spatial alignment between observations and actions.
With fewer than 250M trainable parameters, Hi3R-VLA
achieves state-of-the-art performance on RoboCasa, RL-
Bench, RoboTwin2, DexJoCo and DexSuite across diverse
manipulation tasks and embodiments, from single-arm and
dual-arm grippers to dexterous hands. Real-robot evalu-
ations further demonstrate its effectiveness in the physical
world. The model, code and dataset are publicly released.
1. Introduction
Language-guided robotic manipulation [4, 20, 24] requires
both understanding task-relevant semantics and reasoning
about physical geometry to generate precise actions. Recent
vision-language-action (VLA) [4, 24] models have demon-
strated promising capabilities across diverse manipulation
tasks, but their predominantly 2D visual representations do
not well capture metric 3D structure. This has motivated the
development of 3D VLAs [9, 53, 55] that incorporate ex-
plicit geometric observations, such as point clouds, to sup-
port robot action generation.
Existing 3D policies differ in how they represent geome-
try and combine it with vision and language for action pre-
diction. Some only associate pretrained 2D visual features
with 3D coordinates [15, 28, 66], while others explicitly en-
code point clouds with dedicated 3D backbones [51, 64, 73]
or fuse the resulting geometric features with visual repre-
sentations [9, 13]. The resulting features can be integrated

into a shared VLM backbone [13, 68] or fed into a sepa-
rate action expert [55]. PointACT [9] compares these ar-
chitectures and highlights the benefits of an action expert
with multi-scale point features.
Despite these advances,
it remains unclear which design choices are most critical
for mapping explicit geometry into effective action learn-
ing, and how well they apply across manipulation tasks and
embodiments, from single-arm grippers to bimanual dex-
terous hands. These questions motivate our investigation:
What makes explicit 3D geometry effective for action pre-
diction, and how robust are these design choices across dif-
ferent manipulation settings?
We introduce Hi3R-VLA, a 3D-based policy that
grounds heterogeneous modalities—vision, language, and
action—in explicit 3D geometry, as illustrated in Figure 1.
Our key idea is to use 3D point representations as a com-
mon geometric reference for multimodal perception and
action generation.
On the perception side, we integrate
spatially-aligned image features and language conditioning
into point representations, enriching scene geometry with
fine-grained, task-relevant semantics. On the action side,
a flow-matching action expert attends to these multimodal
point representations in a coarse-to-fine manner. However,
action representations, such as joint configurations or rel-
ative motion commands, may not be directly aligned with
the 3D geometry of the observed scene. To bridge this gap,
we introduce spatial anchors that augment the original ac-
tion representations with 3D end-effector positions. These
anchors lie in the scene coordinate frame, allowing 3D ro-
tary positional encoding (RoPE) [54] in action-to-point at-
tention to capture relative spatial relationships between ac-
tion targets and observed points. Additionally, we investi-
gate the benefits of pretraining Hi3R-VLA on a large-scale
real-robot dataset DROID [29].
We extensively evaluate Hi3R-VLA on multiple ma-
nipulation benchmarks, including RoboCasa24 [47], RL-
Bench [26], RoboTwin2 [10], DexJoCo [59], and Dex-
Suite [22], which cover diverse tasks and embodiments,
from single-arm and dual-arm manipulators to dexterous
hands.
Hi3R-VLA achieves state-of-the-art performance
across benchmarks with over 10× fewer trainable param-
eters than state-of-the-art methods such as π0.5 [24], even
without pretraining on large-scale real-robot data. Exten-
sive ablations show that spatial anchors and 3D positional
encoding are critical for precise action prediction by align-
ing actions with observed geometry, while fusing visual and
language features into the point backbone provides gains
beyond multimodal conditioning in the action expert.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce Hi3R-VLA, a 3D-based policy that uses
explicit geometric representations to connect multimodal
perception with spatially grounded action generation.
• We investigate key design choices in Hi3R-VLA, includ-
ing multimodal fusion, spatially grounded action predic-
tion, and pretraining, providing empirical insights for
building effective 3D policies.
• Hi3R-VLA achieves state-of-the-art performance across
diverse simulation benchmarks and real-world manipula-
tion tasks with fewer than 250M trainable parameters.
2. Related Work
Vision-language robotic manipulation. Vision-language-
action (VLA) models such as RT-2 [6], OpenVLA [31],
π [24, 25] and GR00T [4], have advanced generalist manip-
ulation by fine-tuning pretrained vision-language models
(VLMs) [2, 3] on large-scale robot demonstrations. More
recent world-action models (WAMs) [33, 70] further show
benefits from future state prediction and video pretraining
for robot control. To better understand the design choices
underlying these models, several works [32, 62, 65] exam-
ine visual representations, architectures, fine-tuning strate-
gies, and training data. While these studies provide valuable
guidance for image- and video-based policies, our work ex-
amines how explicit 3D geometry can support policy learn-
ing, a question that remains comparatively underexplored.
Geometric priors for visual policies. A growing body of
work incorporates geometric knowledge into visual policies
to improve spatial understanding and manipulation. Spa-
tialVLA [53] and SPEAR-1 [48] augment visual represen-
tations with pretrained depth estimators, while E0.5 [56]
adopts geometric foundation models like VGGT [60] as
policy backbones. Other approaches solely introduce ge-
ometric supervision during training.
SpatialForcing [37]
aligns intermediate visual features with VGGT representa-
tions; Pri4R [30] uses dense 3D point tracking as the aux-
iliary loss; and SA-WAM [17] incorporates geometry into
WAMs through joint RGB, depth, and action prediction.
To better connect the observation and action space, some
works first predict 3D intermediate representations such as
3D object pose [45], camera-frame SE(3) end-effector tra-
jectory [11, 45] or 3D scene flows [61], then condition an
action expert on the resulting latent features.
However,
this formulation does not align multi-view visual observa-
tions, and requires large-scale 3D pretraining [45, 61]. Our
approach directly encodes metric point clouds aggregated
from multiple views and uses spatial anchors to align action
tokens with scene points in a common coordinate frame,
enabling data- and compute-efficient action prediction.
3D-based robotic manipulation. The prevalence of 3D
sensors has encouraged explicit 3D policy learning. Exist-
ing 3D-based policies differ in how they represent scene ge-
ometry and incorporate it into action prediction. Rendering-
based methods [18, 19, 41] project point clouds into virtual
views, allowing to reuse pretrained image models. Depth-
or point-map-based methods [27, 35] represent geometry on
RGB-aligned 2D grids, facilitating image-based encoding

3.2. Grounding Multimodal Semantics in 3D
Point clouds provide explicit metric geometry, but their
sparsity limits the fine-grained appearance. We therefore
fuse spatially-aligned image features into point represen-
tations to incorporate richer visual information from pre-
trained image encoders. We further introduce language con-
ditioning to emphasize geometry relevant to the task goal.
Point-image fusion.
We associate image features with
scene points using calibrated camera parameters. For each
point pi and camera view v, we extract its visual feature
as gv
i = Bilinear(Vv, projv(pi)), where projv maps 3D
coordinates to image coordinates and Bilinear samples the
corresponding feature map. We retain projections that sat-
isfy image-boundary and depth-consistency constraints, av-
erage their features across valid views as the per point image
feature gi. Unlike PointWorld [23] which only concatenates
gi with point features at the encoder input, we inject aligned
visual features at every encoder stage. We propagate these
features to coarser scales by average pooling over the cor-
responding point downsampling groups and fuse them with
the point features at each stage. This enables visual seman-
tic information to be incorporated into geometric represen-
tations at different spatial resolutions.
Point-text fusion. We condition the point cloud backbone
on language to emphasize task-relevant scene geometry.
Learnable queries are first used to aggregate the language
token embeddings into a global representation cT through
attentive pooling. We then modulate point features using
adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) [50]:

3.3. Grounding Action Generation in 3D
We employ a transformer-based flow-matching action ex-
pert, predicting the velocity field that transforms sampled
noise into an action chunk given multimodal inputs.
Coarse-to-fine multimodal conditioning. Each decoder
block in the action expert combines action-token self-
attention with cross-attention to point, image, and language
features. To exploit hierarchical point representations effi-
ciently, we organize the decoder blocks into groups that at-
tend to progressively finer point features, from M1 to ML.
This coarse-to-fine schedule first captures broad scene con-
text and then incorporates local geometric detail. Direct
conditioning on image and language embeddings provides
complementary semantic information.
Spatial anchors for 3D positional encoding. Precise ma-
nipulation requires reasoning about the relative geometry
between action targets and observed scene points. How-
ever, robot actions may be expressed as joint configurations
or relative motion commands, which do not directly specify
positions in the scene’s 3D coordinate frame. We therefore
augment action representations with spatial anchors, typi-
cally the corresponding end-effector positions expressed in
the same coordinate frame as the point cloud. These an-
chors provide a geometric reference for attention while re-
taining robot-specific action parameterization.
We use spatial anchors to apply 3D rotary positional
encoding (3D RoPE) [15, 54] to action self-attention and
action-to-point cross-attention. The anchors and points are
expressed in metres in a shared coordinate frame.

Implementation Details. We use L = 5 stages in the point
encoder. The action expert assigns 3 decoder blocks per
stage, containing 15 blocks in total, each with a hidden
size of 512 and 8 attention heads. For 3D RoPE, we set
ωmax = 50 rad/m and θ = 100, which with dr = 20 gives
frequencies from 50 to 0.79 rad/m, i.e., wavelengths from
12.6 cm to 7.9 m. We initialize the point encoder from the
self-supervised Utonia PTv3 backbone [73] and fine-tune it
during training. The SigLIP2 [57] image and text encoders
remain frozen except for their final projection layers, result-
ing in fewer than 250M trainable parameters overall. We
train with the AdamW optimizer, using a learning rate of
10−4 and a cosine schedule. Training uses a global batch
size of 128 on 2 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. At inference, actions
are generated with 10 Euler integration steps. Section A of
the supplementary material provides more details.
Pretraining on DROID. To improve generalization, we
pretrain Hi3R-VLA on DROID [29], a real-world dataset
of teleoperated robot demonstrations of a single-arm grip-
per. Since the original DROID dataset lacks depth images
and contains noise in camera calibration and language an-
notations, we construct an RGB-D version using 3D recon-
structions from PointWorld [23] and curated language an-
notations from RoboInter [38], including 35.2k demonstra-
tions with 25.4k distinct instructions. Each frame has two
calibrated external RGB-D views and a wrist-camera RGB
image. Further details are presented in Section B of the sup-
plementary material.
4. Ablation Studies
This section performs controlled ablations of Hi3R-VLA to
identify key design choices for effective 3D policy learning.
Experimental setup. We use RoboCasa24 [47] as our pri-
mary ablation benchmark, which comprises 24 tasks span-
ning diverse manipulation skills and testing generalization
across environments and objects.
We train a multi-task
policy for ∼60k iterations, using 50 human teleoperation
demonstrations per task.
Following prior work [33], we
evaluate each task over 50 episodes per seed across three
random seeds and report the mean success rate and standard
deviation across seeds. All ablations in this section are con-
ducted without DROID pretraining. Additional ablations
on the dexterous manipulation benchmark DexJoCo [59]
and the benefits of DROID pretraining are presented in Sec-
tion D and E, respectively, in the supplementary material.
4.1. How to Ground Actions in 3D Geometry?
To isolate the contribution of 3D geometry, we remove the
image encoder and condition the action expert solely on
point cloud and language features in this subsection.
Spatial anchors. We compare three variants to investigate
how spatial anchors affect action prediction.
• Executable anchor: Absolute end-effector positions are
predicted, followed by the remaining control command.
The absolute action is used in 3D RoPE and action exe-
cution. Since RoboCasa24 uses delta control, predicted
absolute positions from executable anchors are converted
into positional deltas and combined with other control
commands like gripper openness for execution.
• Auxiliary anchor: The control action is augmented with a
prefix whose training target is the next end-effector posi-
tion. The anchor is only used for 3D RoPE, whereas the
robot executes the predicted low-level commands.
• No anchor: Only native control commands are predicted,
and therefore action-side 3D RoPE is omitted.
As shown in Figure 3a, the auxiliary anchor improves
the success rate by 9.2 points over the no-anchor variant,
demonstrating the benefits of explicitly grounding actions

4.2. How to Integrate Vision, Language and 3D?
We explore where and how to integrate heterogeneous
modalities into the point encoder and action expert.
Point-text fusion. In Table 1 (upper block), we investigate
whether incorporating language into point representations
provides additional benefits beyond direct language condi-
tioning in the action expert. We compare three variants: no
point-text fusion, fusion with a pooled text embedding via
AdaLN, and token-level fusion through cross-attention. In-
corporating language into point representations consistently
improves policy performance, even when the action expert
already has access to language, demonstrating the neces-
sity of learning task-relevant 3D representations. In addi-
tion, AdaLN outperforms token-level cross-attention with
lower computational overhead. We hypothesize that the in-
structions primarily specify task goals and provide limited
fine-grained object descriptions, so a global instruction em-
bedding may capture the task semantics needed to modu-
late 3D point features. A related observation is reported
in DiT [50], where AdaLN outperforms cross-attention for
class-conditional image generation.
Image-point fusion. Table 1 also examines whether and
where to fuse spatially aligned image features into the point
encoder. Image-point fusion substantially improves perfor-
mance, with multi-stage fusion (+5.8) outperforming input-
only fusion (+3.2) compared to the non-fusion baseline.

novel object placements and distractors. We train a single
multi-task policy on 100 demonstrations per task for 42k
iterations, and evaluate 100 episodes per task across three
random seeds. As shown in Figure 4, Hi3R-VLA achieves

the advantages of Hi3R-VLA for long-range keystep action

6. Conclusion
We present Hi3R-VLA, a geometry-grounded 3D policy
that unifies heterogeneous vision, language, and action
representations in a shared 3D space.
It integrates spa-
tially aligned visual and language features into hierarchical
point representations, while spatial anchors and 3D posi-
tional encoding bridge scene geometry and action genera-
tion. Extensive experiments across diverse robotic manip-
8


ulation benchmarks demonstrate strong performance with
a compact model. Our ablations highlight the importance
of aligning action targets with scene geometry, integrating
multi-modalities into 3D representations, and leveraging
robot pretraining, providing practical insights into the de-
sign of effective and efficient 3D-based manipulation poli-
cies. Limitation and future work are discussed in Section I
of the supplementary material.

A. Implementation Details
A.1. Model Architecture and Configuration
We provide additional implementation details of Hi3R-
VLA, including the multimodal point encoder and the flow-
matching action expert.
Overview. Given V calibrated RGB-D views, a proprio-
ceptive state st, and a language instruction L, Hi3R-VLA
first lifts the views into a point cloud Pt expressed in a cen-
tered metric frame (Section A.2). The multimodal point en-
coder EP then encodes Pt with a hierarchical Point Trans-
former V3 (PTv3) [64] initialized from Utonia [73], inject-
ing multi-view SigLIP2 [57] image features at every en-
coder stage and conditioning every point block on the in-
struction (Section A.3). Finally, a flow-matching action ex-
pert predicts the action chunk At by attending to the multi-
scale point representations {Mℓ}L
ℓ=1 and to the image and
language tokens (Section A.5), using spatial anchors and
3D RoPE to relate actions and points in metric space (Sec-
tion A.4). The encoders run once per observation, and their
outputs are reused for all flow integration steps. The model
configuration is summarized in Table 6 and the training hy-
perparameters in Table 7.
Point transformer architecture.
The PTv3 encoder
has L = 5 stages, indexed by s ∈{0, . . . , 4} from
fine to coarse, with (3, 3, 3, 12, 3) transformer blocks,
(54, 108, 216, 432, 576) channels, and (3, 6, 12, 24, 32) at-

connected by grid pooling with stride 2, so the cell size
grows from the 1 cm input grid to 2, 4, 8, and 16 cm.
Grid pooling uses max pooling over the projected fea-
tures and averages the coordinates of the points merged
into each cell. Each block consists of a sparse convolu-
tional conditional positional encoding, serialized patch self-
attention, and an MLP, each with a pre-LayerNorm resid-
ual connection. Patch attention uses a patch size of 1024
points grouped by one of the four serializations (z-order,
Hilbert, and their transposed variants), which are randomly
permuted during training.

A.6. Training Details
Table 7 lists the training hyperparameters. We optimize all
trainable parameters with AdamW using a single learning
rate. Weight decay is not applied to biases and normaliza-
tion parameters. The learning rate is linearly warmed up
over the first 3% of the iterations and then follows a co-
sine decay. We use bf16 mixed precision for the frozen
SigLIP2 encoders and for the linear and attention layers of
PTv3, whose sparse convolutions run in float32. The point
projections, the action expert, and the loss are computed in
float32, except for the attention kernels, which use bf16 in-
puts. Models are trained with a global batch size of 128 on
2 NVIDIA H100 GPUs for 40k to 100k iterations depend-
ing on the benchmark, which takes less than 1 second per
iteration. Hence, it takes around 8 hours to 1 day for each
experiment on downstream benchmarks.
B. DROID Pretraining
We use real-world demonstrations from DROID [29]
dataset to pretrain Hi3R-VLA.
Sources and selection.
DROID [29] is released with-
out depth maps and with noisy camera extrinsics.
We
take the per-frame stereo depth, estimated with Foundation-
Stereo [63], and the optimized extrinsics of the two exterior
cameras from PointWorld [23], and the RGB videos, robot
states, commanded actions and language instructions from
RoboInter [38]. Of the 76.5k successful DROID demonstra-
tions, 35,811 have PointWorld depth and calibration. We
discard 573 of them whose instruction does not describe a
task (e.g., “no action”), which leaves 35,238 demonstrations
(Table 8). Figure 7 shows some examples in the cleaned
dataset.
Observations and actions. Each frame provides two exte-
rior RGB-D views at 320×180 resolution with their intrin-
sics and extrinsics, and a wrist RGB view without depth.
The extrinsics map the robot base frame to each camera, so
point clouds, states and actions share the base frame. At
training time, we back-project the pixels of both exterior
views whose depth lies in [0.2, 3.0] m, which gives close
to 100k points per frame before downsampling. The state
is the 8-D end-effector pose, i.e. position, quaternion and
gripper opening. The action is the end-effector pose com-
manded by the teleoperation controller. We keep the native
15 Hz rate of DROID.
Pretraining configuration.
DROID pretraining follows
the training details in Section A.6, except that we use 4
NVIDIA H100 GPUs with a global batch size of 256 and
train for 2 epochs (∼79k iterations).

C.2. RLBench
Tasks and demonstrations. RLBench [26] is a simulation
benchmark built on CoppeliaSim, in which a Franka Panda
arm with a parallel gripper performs tabletop tasks. We fol-
low the 10-task setting [9, 46], which contains close box,
close laptop lid, toilet seat down, sweep to dustpan, close
6


fridge, phone on base, take umbrella out of umbrella stand,
take frame off hanger, stack wine, and water plants. Each
task is trained with 100 demonstrations, and one policy is
trained on all tasks.
Keyframes. As is common for RLBench, the policy pre-
dicts keyframe actions instead of dense trajectories.
A
frame is a keyframe if the gripper opens or closes, if the
joint velocities are close to zero, or if it is the last frame of
the demonstration. Each training sample pairs the observa-
tion at a keyframe with the end-effector pose and gripper
state of the next keyframes.
Observations and actions. We only use the front RGB-D
camera. At evaluation time, each predicted pose is reached
with a motion planner, followed by the gripper action.
Evaluation. Each task is evaluated on 100 episodes for 3
times with newly sampled scene initializations. An episode
succeeds if the task is completed within 25 keyframe steps,
and failures of the motion planner are counted as failed
episodes. We use a chunk size of H = 2 and replan at
each keyframe.
C.3. DexJoCo
Benchmark and embodiment.
DexJoCo [59] is a Mu-
JoCo benchmark of task-oriented dexterous manipulation,
in which each arm is a Franka Panda equipped with a 16-
DoF Allegro hand. We use 6 single-arm tasks, namely click
mouse, fold glasses, hammer nail, pick bucket, pinch tongs,
and water plant, and 4 bimanual tasks, namely assembly,
Hanoi, microwave cooking, and photograph.
We follow
PointWAM [49] and exclude the bimanual unlock-iPad task,
since 42 of its 100 demonstrations fail to replay.
Demonstrations.
Each task provides 100 teleoperated
demonstrations. We replay each demonstration in the simu-
lator to render RGB-D observations at 256 × 256, and dis-
card the demonstrations whose replay fails. One policy is
trained on all single-arm and bimanual tasks, and the two
arms of bimanual tasks are distinguished by the learned arm
embedding (Section A.5).
Observations and actions. Single-arm tasks use the front,
right ego-view, and wrist cameras, and bimanual tasks use
the ego-view and the two wrist cameras.
The action of
each arm consists of the absolute end-effector pose and the
16 absolute joint targets of the hand, which are native to
DexJoCo, and is expressed in the frame of the robot base.
The predicted actions are directly executed by the simulator
controllers.
Evaluation. Each task is evaluated on 50 episodes with ran-
domized object placements, and we report the mean success
rate over 3 random seeds. The episode horizon ranges from
1,000 to 1,500 steps depending on the task. The policy pre-
dicts chunks of 32 actions and executes the first 16 before
replanning.
C.4. RoboTwin 2.0
Benchmark and tasks. RoboTwin 2.0 [10] is a SAPIEN
benchmark of 50 bimanual tabletop tasks performed by the
Aloha-AgileX robot, which has two 6-DoF arms with par-
allel grippers.
Demonstrations. We train one policy on the official clean
demonstrations, with 50 demonstrations per task and 2,500
in total. Since the released data contain no depth, we rebuild
each scene from its seed, replay the recorded joint and grip-
per targets, and render RGB-D observations at the native
resolution of 240 × 320. The 92 demonstrations whose re-
play does not succeed are discarded, leaving 2,408 demon-
strations.
Observations and actions. The policy observes the static
head camera and the two wrist cameras. The action of each
arm contains the absolute end-effector pose as the auxil-
iary anchor, the gripper opening, and the 6 joint targets of
the next frame, expressed in the simulator world frame. At
evaluation time, the predicted joint and gripper targets are
executed by the joint-position controller of the benchmark.
Evaluation. Following the official protocol, each task is
evaluated on 100 episodes in two settings. The clean set-
ting matches the training distribution with seen instructions,
while the randomized setting adds cluttered distractors,
random background textures, lighting changes, and table-
height variations, together with unseen instructions. Eval-
uation seeds for which the expert planner fails are skipped,
and the episode horizon follows the task-specific step limit
of the benchmark. The policy predicts chunks of 32 actions
and executes the first 16 before replanning.

demand precise contact and tight coordination, such as as-
sembly, hanoi, photograph and pinch tongs, suggesting that
Hi3R-VLA is particularly beneficial when fine-grained spa-
tial reasoning is required.
F.4. Per-Task Results on RoboTwin2
Table 15 breaks down the results over the 50 RoboTwin2
tasks.
In the clean→clean setting, Hi3R-VLA achieves
the best or tied-best success rate on 42 of 50 tasks.
The largest gains appear on tasks that demand pre-
cise spatial reasoning about object pose and placement,
such as move stapler pad (+42 over the best baseline),
place fan (+30) and blocks ranking size (+29), suggesting
that explicit 3D representations help resolve fine-grained
geometric relations that are ambiguous from 2D fea-
tures alone. Under clean→randomized evaluation, Hi3R-
VLA still obtains the highest average and its advantage
is most pronounced on placement and stacking tasks,
e.g., stack blocks two (+45), place object stand (+39) and
blocks ranking rgb (+26), where geometric cues remain in-
formative despite changes in background, lighting and clut-
ter. However, the gain is less uniform than in the clean
setting: Hi3R-VLA is best on 23 tasks and falls behind
on several contact-rich or articulated-object tasks such as
lift pot and open microwave.
Since all models are fine-
tuned only on clean demonstrations, this indicates that out-
of-distribution distractors and visual shifts remain chal-
lenging, and we expect training with randomized data or
stronger pretraining to further close this gap.

G.1. Robot Setup
We use a 6-DoF UR5 robotic arm with a Weiss WSG50
gripper and UMI-style parallel jaws. The action loop runs
at 15 Hz.
The robot operates over a 1 m×1 m tabletop
workspace observed by a fixed Orbbec Femto Mega RGB-D
camera, see Figure 10. Observations include a single-view
RGB-D image and robot proprioceptive state (6-DoF robot
joint angles and 1D gripper openness). Actions are repre-
sented in the same 7D joint space.
G.2. Data Collection and Training
Data Collection.
We design 5 tasks as shown in Sec-
tion G.3.
For each task, a human teleoperator collects
50 expert demonstrations using a Meta Quest mask along
with its hand controller. Training demonstrations are post-
processed to remove idle frames.
Training. We use forward kinematics of the UR5 arm to
compute the end-effector pose in the robot base frame and
transform the observed point cloud into the same coordi-
nate frame. The computed end-effector pose serves as the
auxiliary spatial anchor in Hi3R-VLA. We fine-tune Hi3R-
VLA from the DROID-pretrained checkpoint on all col-
lected demonstrations for 10 epochs (approximately 20k it-
erations), with a batch size of 128. All other training con-
figurations follow Table 7.
G.3. Evaluation Protocol and Results
We manually measure the full task success rate and partial
credit for each task. Partial credit is awarded for each com-
pleted subtask, such as a successful grasp or placement. An
episode counts as a full success only when every subtask is
completed in the correct order.
• place toasts in toaster:
place the two toasts in the
toaster, then turn on the toaster. Partial credit: 0.2 for
grasp; 0.2 for placement; 0.2 for turning on the toaster.
• stack 3 cubes: stack the pink cube on the yellow cube,
then the green cube on the pink cube. Partial credit: 0.25
per grasp and 0.25 per placement for each of the two
cubes; −0.25 for wrong order.
• hang green mug onto hanger: pick the green mug and
hang it on the wooden hanger.
Partial credit: 0.5 for
grasp; 0.5 for placement.

H. Qualitative Results
We present successful and failed real-robot rollouts of
Hi3R-VLA on the UR5 in Figure 11 and Figure 12, respec-
tively. More examples are in the supplementary video.
We observe three main challenges underlying the fail-
ures: (1) Noisy and incomplete point clouds. Depth noise
and partial observations can lead to inaccurate scene geome-
try, as illustrated by the noisy mug point cloud in Figure 13.
More reliable depth sensing and multi-view observations
could mitigate this issue. (2) Limited failure recovery. For
the sweeping task, the demonstrations contain only single-
attempt executions.
As a result, when the robot fails to
sweep all paper crumples in one attempt, it struggles to per-
form corrective actions. Training on recovery demonstra-
tions or using reinforcement learning could improve robust-
ness to such failures. (3) Object orientation understanding.
While Hi3R-VLA improves spatial precision, it does not
explicitly model object orientation, which can lead to incor-
rect grasping or manipulation poses as shown in the prepare
coffee failed example. Training on more diverse data with
greater variation in object poses could improve orientation
understanding and manipulation robustness.
I. Limitations and Future Work
Our approach relies on calibrated RGB-D cameras, and
thus noisy depth estimates and calibration may affect per-
formance.
Due to limited computational resources, we
only pretrain on a subset of DROID and adopt a relatively
lightweight training setup.
Scaling up pretraining data,
training configuration, and model size could further im-
prove performance, given the promising results achieved
with our current setup.
Beyond scaling, future directions include integrating our
policy into agentic systems for long-horizon manipulation,
improving control through reinforcement learning, extend-
ing the shared 3D representation to additional modalities
such as tactile sensing, and incorporating world modeling
to predict future scene dynamics.
