## Abstract 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses large vision-language models (LVLMs) by calibrating quantization pa-rameters on a small dataset without retraining. How-ever, despite increasingly sophisticated calibration strate-gies, prior methods suffer substantial performance degra-dation under low-bit weight–activation quantization. To un-derstand this degradation, we locate where quantization er-ror is concentrated and examine the corresponding weight and activation distributions. We observe that a few outliers dominate the ranges shared by per-channel (PC) weights and per-token (PT) activations, leaving insufficient resolu-tion for most values and mapping many of them to zero. These findings motivate a shift in focus from calibration re-finement to the underlying quantization representation. We therefore present Rotate-and-Refine Quantization (RRQ) as a simple, effective baseline for LVLM PTQ, combining local Hadamard transforms with group quantization and efficient group-aware execution. Across 8 multimodal tasks and di-verse quantization settings, RRQ substantially outperforms baselines while reducing inference time and memory usage. At W4A4, it retains over 96% of full-precision performance on 8B models and over 99% on a 32B model, compared with less than 8% for these baselines. The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/kmc3661/RRQ .

Although modality-aware calibration can prioritize sensitive components, it remains constrained by the resolution available under PC/PT. Consequently, when many values already share the same quantization code, re-fining their relative importance alone cannot reliably pre-serve their distinctions. This motivates improving the un-derlying representation before further refining calibration strategies. We therefore present Rotate-and-Refine Quantization (RRQ), a simple, strong baseline that combines local Hadamard transforms [4] with group quantization [40, 53]. The Hadamard transform redistributes values within small feature blocks to reduce outlier-dominated ranges. Group quantization then accommodates residual range differences with group-specific scales, preserving finer resolution in groups without outliers. To make this finer representation efficient, we combine Hadamard transformation and activa-tion quantization in a single kernel to reduce memory traffic, and integrate group-wise corrections into integer accumula-tion to reduce computation overhead. With these improvements in representation and execu-tion, our baseline uses only a basic reconstruction-based scale search for calibration. It substantially outperforms prior LVLM PTQ methods across diverse quantization set-tings on 8 multimodal tasks. At W4A4, it retains over 96% of full-precision performance on three 8B models and 99.06% on a 32B model, with time and memory savings comparable to PC/PT. Our contributions are: • We identify outlier-dominated PC/PT ranges as a key bot-tleneck behind low-bit performance collapse in LVLMs. • We establish a strong baseline combining Hadamard transforms, group quantization, and efficient execution with simple calibration. • We demonstrate strong performance retention and effi-ciency across diverse LVLMs and multimodal tasks. 

## 2. Related work 

2.1. Vision-language models 

LVLMs integrate LLMs [25, 52] with pretrained vision en-coders [15, 37] through learned connectors. Models such as Flamingo [2], LLaVA [34], and InstructBLIP [13], along with subsequent LVLM families [10, 26, 45], support mul-timodal understanding and instruction following. These ad-vances rely on large language backbones and additional vi-sual tokens, increasing memory and computational costs. 

2.2. Post-training quantization for LVLMs 

LLM PTQ includes weight-only and weight–activation quantization. Weight-only quantization methods use er-ror compensation [17] or activation-aware scaling [31]. Weight–activation quantization reduces error through mixed precision [14], fine-grained quantization [49], acti-vation smoothing [48], learnable scaling and clipping [39], Hadamard rotations [4], and rotations with permuta-tions [30]. Efficient kernels support group quantization [53] and progressive quantization [33]. For LVLMs, Q-VLM [44] models cross-layer dependen-cies to guide quantization. 3.2. Motivation 

Existing post-training quantization (PTQ) methods for large vision-language models (LVLMs) [20, 23, 28, 47] em-ploy complex modality-aware calibration strategies, yet suf-fer substantial performance degradation under low-bit per-channel (PC) weight and per-token (PT) activation quanti-zation. To investigate this limitation, we use uniform CWE search as a basic calibration baseline for PC/PT quantiza-tion on 3 recent LVLMs [3, 5, 46] with different archi-tectures. We restore the weights and input activations of each projection type to full precision across all layers, while keeping the other projections quantized. On 32 randomly sampled COCO image–caption pairs [32], we measure how much this restoration reduces the increase in reference-response negative log-likelihood (NLL) relative to the full-precision model. As shown in Fig. 2(a), restoring only the down projections recovers over 90% of this NLL increase in all three models at W4A4, identifying these modules as a major source of degradation in this setting. Figure 2(b) examines selected distributions in these modules: weights within the same output channel and ac-tivations within the same vision or text token. Although 97.7–99.99% of values lie within 10% of each vector’s maximum absolute value, a few outliers extend the range far beyond this dense core. Under PC/PT quantization, these outliers determine the range shared by all values in the channel or token. For a vector v with entries vi,symmetric b-bit quantization uses a nominal step ∆( v) = max i |vi|/(2 b−1 − 1) . An outlier-driven increase in ∆( v)

therefore sacrifices resolution for the small values, with those satisfying |vi| < ∆( v)/2 rounding to zero. Figure 3 shows this effect when 4-bit PC/PT quantization is applied to the Qwen3-VL examples in Fig. 2(b): 98.27% of weights and 99.71%/99.98% of vision/text activations map to zero. These observations identify outlier-driven loss of quanti-zation resolution within weight channels and activation to-kens as a major contributor to low-bit LVLM degradation. To address this limitation, we combine Hadamard trans-forms with group quantization in an integrated pipeline that preserves finer resolution with efficient execution.

3.4. Efficient group-aware execution 

Although RRQ mitigates outlier-induced quantization er-ror, its online Hadamard transformation and group-specific scale handling introduce runtime overhead. This avoids writing and rereading separate transformed-activation and unpacked-code buffers. ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental setup 

Implementation details. We randomly sample 128 COCO [32] image–caption pairs from ShareGPT4V [8] to calibrate all methods. We set Hadamard block size h = 32

These results highlight the advantages of our method in ac-curacy, simplicity, and calibration efficiency. We then evaluate whether the execution optimizations preserve the efficiency benefits of low-bit quantization. Ta-ble 8 compares generation time and peak allocated memory on Qwen3-VL-8B at W4A4, using 25 randomly selected samples each from AI2D and NoCaps. Group + Hadamard denotes a direct implementation of local Hadamard trans-forms and group quantization without our proposed group-aware execution optimizations. Ad-ditional measurement details, results, and code for repro-ducing our experiments are included in the supplementary material. 85. Conclusion 

In this paper, we show that outliers severely limit the precision of per-channel weight and per-token activation quantization in LVLMs. To address this issue, we in-troduce Rotate-and-Refine Quantization, combining local Hadamard transforms and group quantization with efficient group-aware execution. Across 8 multimodal tasks and 8B/32B models, it substantially improves performance re-tention with simple calibration with time and memory sav-ings. Our results emphasize accurate quantization represen-tations and efficient execution over increasingly complex calibration.

A.4. Execution and runtime optimizations 

Our implementation combines general runtime optimiza-tions with RRQ-specific group-aware execution. The gen-eral optimizations include fusion of root mean square nor-malization (RMSNorm) [51] and sigmoid-weighted linear unit (SiLU) [16] operations where applicable, input reuse across compatible projections, and CUDA Graphs for de-coding. RRQ-specific execution instead fuses Hadamard transformation, group activation quantization, and pack-ing, and integrates group-wise corrections into integer accumulation with input-shape-specific execution paths. These mechanisms address the additional transformation and group-scale handling introduced by RRQ. The direct Group+Hadamard implementation includes weight packing and input reuse but omits our group-aware execution optimizations. ## B. Details of Distribution Analyses 

This section provides distribution-selection and measure-ment details for Figures 2, 3, 5, and 6 of the main paper. 

Illustrative distributions in Figures 2 and 3. For the ex-amples in Figures 2 and 3 of the main paper, we use neg-ative log-likelihood (NLL) recovery from the projection-restoration experiment to rank layers. For the illustrative distributions, we select a vector with the smallest fraction of values exceeding 10% of its maximum absolute value. This criterion highlights distributions in which a few large values dominate the range; their prevalence is assessed separately through the layerwise and aggregate analyses below. Re-covery values depend on interactions between components and should not be summed to partition the total quantization error. 

Layerwise and aggregate statistics in Figures 5 and 6. 

For the layerwise and aggregate analyses in Figures 5 and 6 of the main paper, we collect statistics over decoder weights and activation inputs, counting inputs shared by multiple projections only once. We obtain activation inputs from the full-precision (FP) model so that errors from preced-ing quantized layers do not affect the comparison. Af-ter excluding padding, we measure zero mapping as the fraction of nonzero values mapped to zero by quantiza-tion. Because Hadamard transformation mixes features, we compute statistics for each resulting distribution rather than track individual values through the transformation.
