## Abstract 

In-context learning (ICL) offers robots a way to acquire new skills from a single demonstration without parameter up-dates. However, existing robotic ICL methods either rely on large-scale pretraining constrained by scarce, costly robot interaction data or require additional annotations yet struggle to generalize across tasks. To bridge this gap, we propose MXI-MemICL, a framework for memory-guided ICL in vision–language–action (VLA) models. By treat-ing demonstrations as retrievable memory, MXI-MemICL enables in-context manipulation without additional large-scale pretraining or specialized annotations. Specifically, a visual–action causal encoder turns actions and their ob-served effects in demonstrations and completed interactions into reusable memory to guide execution. To retrieve expe-rience suited to upcoming execution, an intention predictor forecasts the next stage’s intent and uses it to query memory. The retrieved experience conditions the language backbone through experience-token prefixes and gated cross-attention, guiding action generation with a learned gate that regu-lates its influence. MXI-MemICL improves success over the baseline by +8.2% and +14.4% on RoboCasa365 and RoboTwin 2.0. Real-robot experiments show a +9.0% gain, with demonstrations adding +5.0% on seen and +16.7% on unseen tasks. The code will be made publicly available. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in vision–language–action (VLA) mod-els [2–4, 20, 36, 44] and world–action models (WAM) [21, 27, 40, 43] have demonstrated impressive capabilities in di-verse manipulation tasks. However, the diversity of embod-ied tasks requires adapting these capabilities to new goals, 1arXiv:2611.32564v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 objects, and environments. Figure 1a summarizes three existing strategies for this adaptation. Task-specific post-training [19, 22] updates the policy using newly collected robot data, tying skill acquisition to repeated data collection and optimization. Test-time training (TTT) [12, 18] adapts fast weights using demonstrations or interactions, but still incurs the cost of gradient optimization during deployment. In-context learning (ICL) [13, 28, 33, 42] offers an alter-native, allowing robots to adapt from demonstrations without deployment-time parameter updates. However, many exist-ing approaches [15, 31] follow the GPT-3 paradigm [6], seek-ing emergent ICL through large-scale pretraining on diverse physical experience. Scarce robot interaction data and high collection costs limit its scalability. Other methods expose demonstration structure through automatically generated se-mantic and motion annotations [38], but generalization to more demanding unseen tasks remains challenging. To bridge this gap, we propose MXI-MemICL , a 

mem ory-guided in-context manipulation framework for 

VLA models. By encoding successful demonstrations as retrievable memory, MXI-MemICL allows a VLA to reuse demonstrated experience on new tasks. The memory mech-anism is learned from existing robot trajectories, enabling in-context manipulation without additional large-scale pre-training or specialized annotations. At deployment, the robot adapts through experience retrieval with fixed parameters, avoiding task-specific retraining and online gradient opti-mization. The language backbone’s observation- and instruction-conditioned features provide semantic context for the current execution stage. An intention decoder makes this context explicit as a stage embedding for experience retrieval. To retain what was executed and its effects, we employ the causal three-stream architecture of BehaviorVLA [16] as a visual–action causal encoder , encoding actions and ob-served visual changes into memory. MXI-MemICL stores memories with context-dependent retrieval keys in a unified 

memory bank , organized by recency into short-term and long-term memory. Short-term memory maintains ongoing execution context, while long-term memory retains earlier experience for reuse. The relevant experience changes as execution progresses, even within a single demonstration. Retrieval must therefore account for both the observed stage and the intent of the up-coming action. To select experience that can guide the next stage, an intention predictor anticipates next-stage intent from experience-conditioned backbone features. The pre-dicted intent combines with the observed stage embedding to retrieve experience for upcoming actions from prior inter-actions and any supplied demonstration. Recent context and retrieved experience are fused into experience tokens that condition the language backbone through prefixes and gated cross-attention . The learned gates regulate the strength of this guidance during action generation. Because demonstra-tions and online interactions share a memory representation, the robot reuses demonstrated experience through the same retrieval and injection mechanism. We evaluate MXI-MemICL on real robots, Robo-Casa365 [26], RoboTwin 2.0 [9], and LIBERO [24]. Relative to π0.5, MXI-MemICL improves success by +8.2% on Robo-Casa365 and +14.4% on the reported RoboTwin 2.0 task subset. On real robots, it ranks first among the compared methods on all five tasks, with an average gain of +9.0% over the base policy. A single successful demonstration adds +5.0% on seen tasks and +16.7% on unseen tasks over the no-demonstration policy, enabling experience-guided adap-tation without parameter updates at deployment. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce MXI-MemICL, which turns demonstra-tions and interaction trajectories into reusable memory for demonstration-guided VLA adaptation without param-eter updates at deployment. • We develop intention-guided retrieval and two-stream ex-perience injection to select and incorporate experience relevant to upcoming actions. • Extensive experiments in simulation and on real robots demonstrate the superiority of MXI-MemICL’s memory architecture, with ablations and analyses further validating the contributions of individual components. 

## 2. Related Work 

VLA and WAM Foundation Models. VLA and WAM foundation models advance general-purpose manipulation by connecting multimodal understanding and dynamics mod-eling with action generation. RT-2 [44] and OpenVLA [20] predict discrete action tokens, while π0 [4] and GR00T N1 [2] generate continuous actions. π0.5 [3] and Xiaomi-Robotics-1 [36] further improve generalization through train-ing on broader datasets. LingBot-VA [21], τ0-WM [43], and Cosmos 3 [27] connect world modeling with control, while Fast-WAM [40] avoids test-time video generation. How-ever, adapting to new tasks often requires task-specific data and post-training [19, 22]. To address this limitation, MXI-MemICL introduces a demonstration-memory interface for reusing existing atomic skills without parameter updates. 

In-Context Learning in Large Language Models. GPT-3 [6] demonstrates that language models can perform tasks using in-context examples without parameter updates. Sub-sequent work examines what demonstrations convey, identi-fying the roles of label space, input distribution, and prompt format [25]. Mechanistic studies interpret ICL as implicit Bayesian inference [37], examine function learning from context [14], and show that transformers can implement regression algorithms within a forward pass in controlled settings [1, 32]. Robotic In-Context Learning Methods. Robotic ICL methods differ in how they encode and use demonstrations: VIMA [17] uses multimodal prompts, ICRT [13] models sensorimotor sequences, and Instant Policy [33] uses graph diffusion. 3.5. Training Strategy 

After base-policy preparation, the three training phases learn interaction representations, experience-conditioned control, and demonstration-guided retrieval. 

Phase 1: interaction encoding. We train the causal en-coder to predict executed actions and subsequent visual fea-tures, preserving both behavior and its effects in the inter-action representation. Auxiliary objectives group same-task experience through contrastive learning, preserve temporal progression through phase regularization, and associate ac-tions with observed changes through effect supervision. The resulting encoder is frozen in subsequent stages to provide a shared interaction representation for memory. 

Phase 2: experience-conditioned control. With the base policy frozen, we jointly optimize intent modeling and ex-perience retrieval, fusion, and injection. Action supervision uses flow matching [23] with intent and anchor objectives: 

LII = LFM + λI Lintent + λALanchor . (7) A fixed, normalized projection of ht supplies the semantic anchor ut. The intent loss aligns the predicted prior rt+1 

with the next anchor ut+1 :

Lintent = E[1 − cos( rt+1 , sg( ut+1 ))] . (8) Complementing this prediction, the anchor loss grounds the retrieval query qt in the current observation: 

Lanchor = E[1 − cos( qt, sg( ut))] . (9) Expectations are over valid supervised samples, sg stops target gradients, and λI , λ A weight the two auxiliary losses. Training across consecutive decisions also lets downstream action loss refine predicted intent through retrieval. 

Phase 3: demonstration-guided adaptation. Phase 3 starts from Phase 2 weights and uses independent same-task demonstration–execution pairs with their native task instruc-tions. The backbone and causal encoder remain frozen, while the intent and memory parameters trained in Phase 2 con-tinue to adapt. Target actions are supervised with the Phase 2 objective. Future execution observations supply intent la-bels only. Retrieval, recovery, and action-prior auxiliaries are disabled. Implementation details and complete training objectives are provided in the supplementary material.

4.1. Evaluation on Simulation Benchmarks 

Simulation Benchmarks. We evaluate MXI-MemICL on RoboTwin 2.0 [9], RoboCasa365 [26], and LIBERO [24], covering multi-stage manipulation, atomic kitchen skills, and diverse task suites. On RoboTwin 2.0, we evaluate 22 tasks over 100 episodes each under the Hard setting with scene randomization. For RoboCasa365, we select 13 tasks covering object placement and articulated-object manipu-lation and evaluate each task on 50 fixed scenes with one attempt per scene. Detailed LIBERO settings, results, and analysis are provided in the supplementary material. For all benchmarks, we report per-task success rates (SR) and their equally weighted mean. We evaluate each simulation experiment at least twice. 

Implementation Details. We build MXI-MemICL on 

π0.5 [3] with memory encoding, intention-guided retrieval, and two-stream experience injection. By default, we train the memory components for 8k steps in Phase 2 and 4k in Phase 3 on eight NVIDIA PRO 6000 GPUs with a batch size of 64. We use n = 4 unless otherwise specified. Training configurations and deployment details are provided in the supplementary material. 

Results on RoboTwin 2.0. On the RoboTwin 2.0 Hard tasks in Table 1, MXI-MemICL achieves a mean success rate of 51.9% versus 37.5% for π0.5, outperforming all listed baselines on every task. Specifically, success rises from 27% to 62% on Stack 2 Blocks and from 39% to 57% on Rank Blocks. Both tasks involve successive object interactions in which each completed interaction changes the next required action. Intention-guided retrieval supplies action–effect ex-perience for such transitions without requiring the current observation to encode the entire execution history. These results show that anticipating next-stage intent enables MXI-MemICL to retrieve relevant experience from the memory bank to guide subsequent action generation. 

Results on RoboCasa365. On the five tasks in Table 2, MXI-MemICL achieves 78.6% mean success, exceeding 70.4% for π0.5 and 70.8% for Xiaomi-Robotics-1. Demonstrations on seen tasks. On seen tasks, Figure 3(a) reports mean success rates of 60.0% for π0.5, 69.0% for MXI-MemICL without demonstrations, and 74.0% with a demonstration. These demonstrations are encoded into varying numbers of mem-ory embeddings according to their duration. MXI-MemICL maintains substantial gains as task horizons increase, further demonstrating the robustness of our soft experience retrieval and adaptive experience injection strategies. 

Demonstrations on unseen tasks. On unseen tasks, Fig-ure 3 (b) reports mean success rates of 21.7%, 25.0%, and 41.7% for the same three models. Demonstrations raise suc-cess from 50% to 75% on block induction, from 25% to 40% on clothes folding, and from 0% to 10% on bottle-cap twist-ing with different bottles. The 16.7-point gain exceeds the 5.0-point gain on seen tasks, consistent with demonstrations providing experience relevant to the target conditions that online interactions alone may not yield.

In contrast, direct inference with MXI-MemICL fails to lift the bottle in Fig-ure 5 (c), highlighting the value of demonstration experience for task adaptation. 85. Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce MXI-MemICL, a framework for memory-guided in-context robot manipulation. Specifically, MXI-MemICL encodes completed interactions into causal memory for closed-loop manipulation. By anticipating next-stage intent, it retrieves relevant experience and adaptively injects it to guide subsequent actions. By treating demon-strations as retrievable experience, MXI-MemICL enables robotic ICL without deployment-time parameter updates. Experiments across diverse simulated and real-robot tasks demonstrate state-of-the-art success rates and effective adap-tation to new tasks from a single demonstration.

## 2. Simulation Benchmarks 

We evaluate each simulation experiment at least twice, with per-task rollout counts below referring to each run. All sim-ulation evaluations are conducted without test-time demon-strations. We report per-task success rates and their equally weighted mean. 

2.1. RoboTwin 2.0 

We train our RoboTwin models using 50 demonstrations per task, matching the training-data setting of the leaderboard baselines. The reproduced π0.5 base is fine-tuned for 30k up-dates on eight NVIDIA PRO 6000 GPUs with a global batch size of 128. We use AdamW with (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.95) and weight decay of 0.01. The learning rate increases linearly to 2.5 × 10 −5 over 1,000 warm-up updates, then follows a cosine schedule to 2.5 × 10 −6. Gradients are clipped to a global norm of 1.0. Building on this base model, we train MXI-MemICL for 4k updates in Phase 1, followed by the shared Phase 2 and 3 schedule of 8k and 4k updates at batch size 64. ## 5. Additional Analyses 

5.1. Inference latency and task execution time 

Following OpenVLA-OFT [19] and RTC [5], we distinguish action-chunk inference latency from end-to-end task com-pletion time (Tables S5 and S6). 

Simulation protocol. Task time runs from the end of snap-shot restoration to official success, including inference waits and environment stepping but excluding setup and warmup. Means use successful rollouts with equal task weights. Each run shares one NVIDIA PRO 6000 between policy and sim-ulator. Concurrent runs use separate GPUs but share host CPUs. Models retain their checkpoint and decoding settings at 20 Hz simulation. Server latency includes memory pro-cessing but excludes websocket transport. We give each task equal weight when aggregating mean and P95 latency. 

From inference to execution. Table S5 reports a mean inference latency of 226.8 ms for MXI-MemICL versus 80.0 ms for π0.5, while mean completion time on successful rollouts increases from 7.4 to 9.6 s. The roughly 2.8× per-call latency translates into a 1.3× completion time because each predicted action chunk supplies multiple controls and total task time also includes environment stepping. Expe-rience retrieval, fusion, and injection add computation to each decision, but this cost is amortized across the controls in an action chunk. Together with the success-rate increase from 70.4% to 78.6%, these results quantify the execution overhead associated with more reliable manipulation. These results indicate that MXI-MemICL delivers substantial gains in manipulation success with inference overhead that re-mains manageable at the task level.

Figure S3 compares the frozen π0.5 base and MXI-MemICL at eight sampled decisions per task. Coffee Setup Mug and Turn On Electric Kettle provide examples of object transport and appliance control. 

Occlusion protocol. We divide each observation into an

MXI-MemICL recomputes its query and retrieved experience from the per-turbed observation. All frames and both policies share a logarithmic scale within each task, with separate scales for the two tasks. Colors show changes in action predictions under visual occlusion. 

Stage-dependent visual sensitivity. Figure S3 shows that the two policies respond differently to visual occlusion at different execution stages, even when given identical ob-servations. In Coffee Setup Mug, MXI-MemICL exhibits a stronger response around the mug and gripper at step 96, where the base policy is less sensitive. By step 128, both poli-cies respond strongly to occlusion near the mug, indicating that the difference depends on the execution stage. In Turn On Electric Kettle, MXI-MemICL responds strongly near the gripper and kettle at step 32, followed by a weaker re-sponse at step 48. These comparisons show that experience-conditioned action generation varies its dependence on local visual information over the interaction, consistent with using execution context to adapt the current action. 17
