# MXFP4 for LLM Inference: Complementary Post-Training Quantization Techniques and Their Interactions 

## Natalia Frumkin ∗, Alireza Khodamoradi, Kristof Denolf, Eric Dellinger, Stuart Biles AMD Research 

## ABSTRACT 

Recent advances in low-precision representation have introduced several microscaling numerical formats for LLM quantization. By sharing a scale factor across groups of 16 or 32 elements, these formats enable aggressive low-precision quantization with minimal accuracy degradation, particularly when combined with modern PTQ algorithms. Their accuracy depends on how the numerical format interacts with rotation, PTQ, and scale selection. We study the composability of PTQ configurations across six LLMs and three microscaling formats: MXFP6, MXFP4, and NVFP4, systematically varying Hadamard rotation granularity, PTQ algorithm, and scale-selection strategy. Our central finding is that effective quantization recipes are format dependent: both MXFP4 and NVFP4 support accurate 4-bit inference, but techniques that benefit one do not necessarily transfer to the other. MXFP4 benefits substantially from rotation and error-correcting PTQ, whereas NVFP4 gains less from rotation, consistent with its finer-grained scales capturing more within-block variation. Building on these findings, we introduce Midmax , a calibration-free scale-selection strategy that mitigates clipping error in power-of-two block scales and improves accuracy across a majority of the evaluated MXFP4 configurations. We show that pairing MXFP6 activations with MXFP4 weights recovers most of the accuracy lost at 4-bit and, on Llama-3.1-8B, matches or exceeds our in-house BF16 baseline. An additional study finds that augmenting MXFP4 with a per-tensor macroscale, a mechanism intrinsic to NVFP4, does not improve average accuracy, suggesting that global rescaling alone does not address the limitations of E8M0 block scales for MXFP4 quantization. Together, these results provide practical guidance for combining numerical formats, rotation, PTQ, and scale selection for accurate low-precision LLM inference. 

## 1 Introduction 

Memory bandwidth, energy consumption, and hardware efficiency are primary system-level constraints in neural network inference, from single-device deployments to large-scale serving systems (1, 2). Modern large language models (LLMs) are dominated by dense linear algebra and memory movement, and since memory traffic and compute cost scale directly with operand precision, numerical format is a central system-level decision rather than a low-level implementation detail (3, 4). The adoption of FP8 in recent hardware has demonstrated that low-precision floating-point formats can significantly improve throughput and energy efficiency while maintaining model quality (5). Motivated by these gains, prior work has pushed to 4-bit precision, with post-training quantization (PTQ) techniques such as weight rounding optimization (6), activation outlier smoothing (7), and sequential error correction (8) establishing that transformer-based LLMs can maintain accuracy at 4-bit. Block floating-point (BFP) representations have emerged as a natural fit for 4-bit LLM inference. Rather than applying a single scale globally, BFP shares a scale across a local block of elements, providing per-block dynamic range coverage while remaining compatible with efficient hardware implementation. The OCP Microscaling (MX) specification has standardized this approach (9), and recent hardware including AMD Instinct accelerators with the CDNA4 architecture provide native support for MXFP4 arithmetic (10), making 4-bit block-scaled inference a practical deployment target. We study three microscaling formats in this work. MXFP6 and MXFP4 are defined by the OCP standard and use a power-of-two (E8M0) block scale shared across 32 elements, with MXFP6 providing a higher-precision 6-bit element datatype and MXFP4 a 4-bit one. NVFP4 instead uses a finer-grained FP8 (E4M3) block scale shared across 16 elements, together with an additional high-precision per-tensor macroscale. On Llama-3 and Qwen3 models evaluated with standard zero-shot and math benchmarks, published results show NVFP4 ahead of MXFP4 when both 4-bit formats are paired with the same off-the-shelf PTQ method (11); concurrent work on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B shows that augmenting MXFP4 with specialized scale-factor techniques can close this gap to within 1% of NVFP4 on the same benchmarks (12). These comparisons, however, typically pair each format with a single fixed set of complementary techniques, which makes it difficult to tell how much of the observed behavior comes from the format and how much from the Hadamard rotation, PTQ algorithm, and scale-selection strategy applied alongside it.

In this work, we instead study the composability of PTQ techniques with microscaling formats: for each format we vary Hadamard rotation granularity, PTQ algorithm, and scale-selection strategy across six LLMs, and measure how these techniques combine. Our central finding is that, with an appropriate recipe, both MXFP4 and NVFP4 are strong candidates for 4-bit (A4W4) inference, and that MXFP6 recovers most of the remaining accuracy at 6-bit; the techniques that compose well with each format, however, differ. For MXFP4 at A4W4, Midmax scale selection, block-level Hadamard rotation, and PTQ yield approximately 3%

2.2 Block Floating-Point Representations 

In high-precision floating point, each value carries its own private exponent, providing fine-grained per-element dynamic range at the cost of storing one high-precision exponent per value, for example, an 8-bit exponent in FP32 and BF16 datatypes. Block floating-point (BFP) representations reduce per-element dynamic range and compensate with a single shared scale ∆ across a block of k elements, amortizing the block scale cost while maintaining local dynamic range coverage, as in MXFP4’s E2M1, which pairs a 2-bit element exponent with a shared block scale. The OCP Microscaling (MX) specification (9) has standardized this approach, and recent AMD Instinct accelerators with the CDNA4 architecture provide native hardware support (10). Within the OCP MX family, three configuration axes determine numerical behavior: the element datatype , the scale datatype , and the block layout . In contrast, NVFP4 adds another axis with an additional per-tensor macroscale; it also has a different block scale datatype and a smaller block size. The macroscale in NVFP4 is software-implemented and is not an exclusive hardware feature; it can therefore be applied to other block-scaled formats. In this work, we also study MXFP4 augmented with a per-tensor macroscale. Table 1 summarizes the four block-scaled configurations in this study. 3

2.3 Post-Training Quantization for Large Language Models 

Post-training quantization (PTQ) has become a key enabler of efficient LLM deployment. GPTQ demonstrated that transformer weights can be quantized to 3-4 bits with minimal accuracy loss (6), and subsequent methods have refined this through alternative reconstruction objectives that better preserve layer outputs (16, 17). Complementary approaches address activation quantization: SmoothQuant migrates outlier difficulty from activations to weights via per-channel scaling (7), while LLM.int8() isolates outlier channels in higher precision (13). Quantization error does not remain localized within individual layers but accumulates and propa-gates across the network. Sequential correction (18) explicitly accounts for how noise introduced in earlier layers affects downstream computations, and Qronos (8) demonstrates that correcting accumulated prior-layer error through the current quantization decision and updates to yet-to-be-quantized weights can significantly improve low-bit PTQ outcomes. While these methods have advanced the state-of-the-art in low-bit PTQ, they have largely been developed and evaluated under fixed format configurations. The interaction between PTQ algorithms and BFP configuration choices (block size, scale format, macroscaling, and scale selection) remains underexplored. In this work, PTQ algorithm is one axis of our sweep: we evaluate GPTQ and Qronos alongside format and rotation choices, and compare both against RTN (Round-to-Nearest), a non-learned baseline that independently rounds each value to its nearest representable point without any reconstruction objective. 4

2.4 Hadamard Rotations 

Rotation transforms such as the Hadamard transform have become an important preprocessing step for PTQ, redistributing outliers across dimensions to produce smoother, more quantization-friendly distributions (19, 20, 21). A Hadamard matrix R is an orthogonal matrix with entries ±1/√h. Each input coordinate is mixed into every output coordinate with a coefficient of magnitude 

1/√h. A large value concentrated in one channel therefore contributes to many rotated coordinates, which lowers the peak channel magnitude that sets the block scale and makes the tensor easier to quantize. Given an input tensor X ∈ Rn×d, the rotation is applied as ˜X = XR , where R is a Hadamard matrix, and the inverse rotation is absorbed into subsequent weights W as ˜W = W R , so the full computation ˜X ˜W ⊤ = XW ⊤ is preserved exactly. In practice, the Hadamard matrix dimension h may not be equal to d, so R is applied as a block-diagonal matrix with h × h Hadamard blocks tiling the last dimension of X. The choice of block size h determines the granularity of outlier redistribution: smaller blocks (e.g., 

h = 16 or h = 32 ) rotate within subsets of the feature dimension, while a full-dimensional rotation ( h = d) redistributes outliers globally, as used in prior work such as QuaRot (20).

Section 4.2 shows how the choice of h interacts with both the block-scale format and the PTQ algorithm. 

PTQ Algorithms. We evaluate two PTQ algorithms, GPTQ and Qronos , against a non-learned rounding baseline, RTN .

RTN (Round-to-Nearest) independently rounds each value to its nearest representable point without any optimization and serves as the baseline against which the two PTQ algorithms are measured. GPTQ (6) is a popular error correction method that minimizes layer-wise weight reconstruction error by solving a weighted least-squares problem column by column, compensating for the error incurred in previously quantized columns. Qronos (8) extends this by correcting accumulated prior-layer error through the current quantization decision and updates to yet-to-be-quantized weights, shaping those corrections to account for how quantization noise propagates forward through the network. These methods are evaluated across combinations of precision configuration, scale format, scale selection strategy, and rotation, letting us measure both the main effect of each PTQ algorithm and how each interacts with format and rotation choices. 

Precision Configurations. We evaluate three precision configurations to isolate the effects of activation and weight precision. In A4W4 , both activations and weights use E2M1 (FP4). In A6W4 , activations use E2M3 (FP6) while weights remain in E2M1, isolating the effect of increasing activation precision without changing weight storage. In A6W6 , both use E2M3, providing a higher-precision reference and measuring the additional benefit of increasing weight precision once activations are in FP6. Together, these configurations trace a two-stage path from A4W4 to A6W6 , first increasing activation precision and then weight precision.

4.1 The Recipe Matters as Much as the Format 

We begin with the central observation of this study: the accuracy of a microscaling format depends strongly on the quantization recipe. Figure 1 shows, for each model, the average benchmark accuracy of every evaluated PTQ configuration for MXFP4 and NVFP4. Two patterns motivate the analysis that follows. First, the accuracy variation across recipes within a format is comparable to or larger than the gap between formats, highlighting the importance of recipe selection. Second, effective recipes are format dependent. For Llama-3.2-3B, for example, the strongest NVFP4 configuration uses Qronos, whereas the strongest MXFP4 configuration uses GPTQ with Midmax scale selection. With suitable recipes, both formats achieve competitive accuracy, and neither consistently leads across models and tasks. These observations motivate studying how each format responds to the techniques combined with it. The following subsections examine the effects of rotation, PTQ algorithm, scale selection, macroscaling, and precision, including their interactions with the underlying format. Section 4.6 then summarizes the best-performing recipes for each datatype. 

4.2 Rotation and PTQ Algorithm Are Complementary Techniques 

As established above, a recipe tuned for one format does not transfer directly to the other. Hadamard rotation provides a clear example of why. As shown in Figure 2, rotation affects the two formats very differently at A4W4. For MXFP4, every rotation granularity improves average accuracy across the three PTQ methods, with Block-16 and Block-32 producing the strongest and most consistent gains. When paired with GPTQ or Qronos, these blockwise rotations improve accuracy by approximately 0.9–1.1% 

over no rotation, compared with gains of 0.44 –0.64% under RTN. This improvement suggests that rotation and PTQ address complementary sources of error: rotation redistributes outliers before quantization, while PTQ reduces the remaining reconstruction error. NVFP4 shows the opposite trend. Rotation reduces average accuracy in eight of the nine configurations shown in Figure 2. GPTQ and Qronos moderate this degradation relative to RTN, but do not make rotation consistently beneficial. Block-32 with Qronos is the only configuration that produces a positive average gain, and the improvement is limited to 0.10% . This finding is consistent with Egiazarian et al. (11), who similarly observe that applying Hadamard rotation to NVFP4 degrades performance. One possible explanation is that NVFP4’s smaller blocks and finer-grained E4M3 scales already capture more local variation, leaving less residual error for rotation to correct. The granularity of the rotation further shapes this interaction. Full rotation provides smaller gains than blockwise rotation for MXFP4 and produces some of the largest accuracy reductions for NVFP4. 4.3 PTQ Algorithm Effectiveness Depends on the Model and Format

For Qwen3-8B, the best overall configuration combines GPTQ with MXFP4, whereas Llama-3.1-8B achieves its highest accuracy using Qronos with NVFP4. The distribution of configurations further shows that a PTQ method that performs well for one format does not necessarily transfer to the other. 4.4 Midmax Provides Calibration-Free Scale Selection 

Figure 4 compares Midmax with standard floor-based scale selection across models, rotation granularities, and PTQ methods. Averaged over the full sweep, Midmax improves accuracy by 0.12% and outperforms floor rounding in 67% of the evaluated model, rotation, and PTQ combinations. Although the average improvement is modest, the consistency of these gains shows that scale selection remains relevant even after rotation and PTQ are applied. Furthermore, the benefit of Midmax depends strongly on the surrounding configuration. Without rotation, Midmax provides an average gain of 0.25% , including an improvement of 0.62% on Qwen3-8B. The gain becomes negligible with Block-16 rotation but increases again to 0.20% with Block-32 rotation. Midmax and Hadamard rotation are therefore neither fully additive nor fully redundant. Both reduce errors caused by outliers, but they act at different stages of quantization and remain complementary in selected configurations. The interaction with PTQ is even more pronounced. Midmax improves accuracy by 0.30% when paired with Qronos, compared with 0.06% for GPTQ and 0.02% for RTN. This suggests that reducing clipping near power-of-two scale boundaries remains valuable after Qronos corrects reconstruction and error-propagation effects. Midmax is not uniformly superior to calibrated strategies such as MSE, but it provides a reliable improvement over floor rounding without calibration data or additional preprocessing. It is therefore most useful as a low-cost scale-selection strategy for sensitive MXFP4 configurations, particularly when residual outliers remain after rotation.

4.6 Configuration Choices Must Be Evaluated Jointly 

Table 4 places the principal effects observed in our configuration sweep on a common scale. Among changes that retain A4W4 precision, GPTQ and Block-32 rotation provide the largest average gains for MXFP4, improving accuracy by 1.32% and 1.17% ,respectively. Midmax provides a smaller but calibration-free improvement of 0.25% without rotation. The corresponding effects differ for NVFP4: GPTQ provides a smaller gain of 0.78% , while Block-32 rotation reduces average accuracy by 0.45% . These differences explain why the same quantization recipe does not transfer uniformly between the two formats. When additional precision is available, increasing activation precision from A4W4 to A6W4 improves MXFP4 accuracy by 

1.74% , and subsequently increasing weight precision from A6W4 to A6W6 provides an additional 1.26% gain. The average effects provide useful guidance, but they do not define a universal recipe. Table 5 shows that GPTQ is selected by the best MXFP4 configuration for five of the six models, whereas Qronos is selected by the best NVFP4 configuration for five of the six models. Blockwise rotation appears in five of the six best MXFP4 configurations, while the preferred NVFP4 rotation spans none, Block-16, Block-32, and full rotation. Scale selection is similarly model dependent. The best MXFP4 configurations are evenly divided between MSE and Midmax, while NVFP4 primarily favors Stats but selects MSE for two Llama models. These results point to a useful default recipe, though, as Table 5 shows, not a single configuration that is optimal for every model. For MXFP4 at A4W4, PTQ and blockwise rotation provide the largest and most consistent gains, with Midmax offering an additional calibration-free improvement. Their combined contribution raises accuracy by approximately 3% over baseline. For NVFP4, Qronos is selected more frequently, while rotation requires greater model-specific care. When this A4W4 recipe does not provide sufficient accuracy, moving to A6W4 is an effective next step, though we did not evaluate the complementary A4W6 configuration needed to determine whether increasing weight precision first would be equally or more effective. Macroscaling should instead be reserved for the tasks and precision regimes where its targeted benefits justify adding it to MXFP4. The appropriate unit of comparison is 10 

therefore the complete quantization configuration rather than the block format alone. Format, PTQ algorithm, rotation granularity, scale selection, macroscaling, and element precision jointly determine the final accuracy. 

## 5 Conclusion 

Accurate microscaling quantization requires jointly choosing the numerical format, PTQ algorithm, rotation, and scale-selection strategy. Across the six language models evaluated, neither MXFP4 nor NVFP4 consistently achieves higher accuracy, and their strongest recipes differ. Our results offer three practical takeaways. First, GPTQ with blockwise rotation provides a strong starting point for MXFP4, while NVFP4 more often favors Qronos and generally benefits less from rotation. Second, calibration-free Midmax improves upon floor-based scale selection in 67% of the evaluated configurations, whereas adding a per-tensor macroscale to MXFP4 does not improve average accuracy despite selective task-level gains. Third, A6W4 recovers accuracy while retaining 4-bit weight storage, matching or exceeding our BF16 baseline on the three evaluated tasks for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Together, these findings support selecting and evaluating complete quantization recipes, using format-specific defaults as starting points for tuning to each model. 11
