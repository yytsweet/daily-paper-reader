Abstract— Sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) has en-
abled impressive robotic behaviors, yet deploying policies di-
rectly from vision remains challenging. A common approach
first trains a teacher policy with RL using privileged state ob-
servations, then distills its behavior into a vision-based student
via imitation. However, the resulting student can substantially
underperform its teacher. Directly training visual policies with
RL offers a promising alternative, but learning task-relevant
visual representations from scratch can be sample-inefficient,
making one-phase visual RL intractable.
We propose DRIFT, a simple recipe for efficient one-phase
visual RL using image observations available at test time.
Our key insight is to use frozen, pretrained foundation vision
encoders, allowing RL to focus on learning control from existing
visual representations. Freezing the encoder avoids costly back-
propagation through the vision backbone, while its pretrained
representations enable real-world transfer from fast, rasterized
simulation renders. Under matched architectures, observations,
and compute budgets, DRIFT outperforms two-phase distillation
and achieves a 58.8% relative improvement in average real-
world success rate across three manipulation tasks. Controlled
simulation evaluations demonstrate that DRIFT’s relative ad-
vantage grows from roughly 9% in-distribution to over 61%
under out-of-distribution shifts, suggesting that stronger gen-
eralization contributes to superior real-world transfer.

I. INTRODUCTION
Sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) provides a scal-
able alternative to real-world data collection by enabling
massive amounts of parallel trial-and-error in simulation.
This paradigm has pushed robot hardware toward its per-
formance limits in locomotion [1]–[4] and dexterous ma-
nipulation [5]–[7]. However, these successes largely rely on
compact state observations, such as object poses, which are
estimated through heavily engineered perception pipelines.
To deploy policies directly from visual observations, a
common approach is to distill privileged state-based teacher
policies into image-based students [8]. The teacher is first
trained with RL using privileged simulator state, after which
its behavior is transferred to the visual student via imitation.
However, this two-phase process introduces two sources of
performance degradation. First, the privileged teacher may
rely on information unavailable to the student, creating a
realizability gap that prevents faithful policy distillation [9].
Second, although imitation-trained students can achieve high
success rates in simulation, they may generalize poorly

One-phase visual RL offers the appealing alternative of
directly optimizing the policy with image observations avail-
able at test time. However, this couples the two difficult
problems of learning task-relevant visual representations and
control policies, with training the vision encoder from scratch
typically dominating the computational cost [12]. We address
this challenge by building on pretrained foundation vision
encoders [13], allowing RL to focus on learning control
from existing visual representations. Freezing the encoder
eliminates backpropagation through the vision backbone,
while its pretrained representations enable policies trained
on fast, rasterized simulation renders to transfer to real-
world images. Together, these design choices enable one-
phase visual RL to outperform two-phase distillation under
matched architectures, observations, and compute budgets.

II. RELATED WORK
Teacher-Student Distillation. Privileged-policy distilla-
tion has enabled sim-to-real transfer for dexterous grasp-
ing,
multi-fingered
manipulation,
and
humanoid
loco-
manipulation [14]–[16]. However, strong teacher perfor-
mance does not guarantee equally capable students [9]. A
typical limitation is information asymmetry (realizability
gap), in which teacher actions depend on state that the stu-
dent cannot infer from its observations [17]. To address this
issue, Messikommer et al. [18] jointly train teacher and stu-
dent policies, adding a teacher–student action-disagreement
penalty to the teacher’s reward and a supervised loss that
aligns the teacher with the student, encouraging behaviors
that are realizable from the student’s limited observations.
Singh et al. [19] train depth-based teachers with RL before
distilling them into RGB policies, improving performance
over distillation from state-based teachers. Our approach,
instead, directly trains the deployed RGB policy with RL.
Furthermore, we examine the robustness limitations of dis-
tillation beyond the well-established realizability gap.
Efficient Visual RL and Frozen Representations. Direct
visual RL has become increasingly tractable with advances in
GPU-parallelized simulation and rendering [20], [21]. Prior
work improves training efficiency through low-resolution im-
ages and lightweight encoders [22], encoder freezing and fea-
ture caching [12], and demonstrations that initialize policies
and guide RL updates [23]. Frozen pretrained encoders pro-
vide another way to simplify visual RL by removing the need
to learn visual features from task rewards [24]. Most closely
related, PoliFormer [25] combines frozen DINOv2 features
with on-policy RL for zero-shot sim-to-real navigation. We
build on this approach for contact-rich manipulation, where
policies must extract precise spatial information from visual
observations. Our recipe combines learned attention pooling
over frozen patch features with fast rasterized rendering,
reducing the computational burden of visual control while
preserving access to spatially detailed representations.
Generalization in RL vs. Supervised Learning. Studies
of foundation model post-training find that RL can improve
OOD generalization and preserve prior capabilities better
than supervised fine-tuning [10], [26]. In robotics, Liu et
al. [27] similarly find that RL fine-tuning improves semantic
generalization and execution robustness over supervised fine-
tuning of VLAs. Whereas these studies examine adaptation
of pretrained models, we compare direct visual RL with


on-policy teacher distillation under matched architectures,
observations, and compute budgets.

IV. METHOD
We propose DRIFT, a tractable recipe for one-phase visual
RL and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer (Fig.
2). We call
the approach ”one phase“ because it does not rely on a
privileged teacher policy, nor expert demonstrations during
training. DRIFT consists of a frozen ViT encoder, learned
attention pooling, an encoder-only transformer actor; it is
trained entirely in simulation with asymmetric actor-critic
RL [29] (PPO) before being deployed directly in the real
world.

A. Algorithmic Decisions for RL Training
We directly train visual policies with distributed Proximal
Policy Optimization [30] (PPO) using an asymmetric critic
vψ(spriv
t
) that accesses privileged simulator state during
training (Fig. 2, right). The actor is a three-layer encoder-
only transformer with hidden dimension 64 that uses bidi-
rectional self-attention. Similar to Dosovitskiy et al. [31], we
add learnable position embeddings to tokens by type. Self-
attention jointly integrates visual and proprioceptive tokens
through a common representation that can accommodate
different visual encoders, camera configurations, and per-
view token budgets. We parallelize rollouts and synchronize
PPO updates across GPU workers, using Adam [32] with a
constant base learning rate of 3 × 10−4.

Using frozen patch embeddings allows us to avoid back-
propagating through an expressive visual encoder, which is
a primary bottleneck for visual RL training. Freezing the
visual encoder also amortizes the forward-pass cost across
the K PPO epochs within each policy improvement step.
Computing patch embeddings once and reusing them across
epochs reduces encoder forward-pass compute by a factor
of K, leading to a further increase in training throughput.
Together, these advantages yield a substantial increase in the
amount of experience DRIFT collects in an hour of training
(Fig. 3, top), compared with unfreezing the encoder (Fig. 3,
bottom).

learned through large-scale pretraining on natural images,
avoiding encoder updates that could specialize these features
to the appearance of simulated images. We find that these

V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Task Design
We select three tasks (Fig. 4) spanning simple grasp-
ing, non-prehensile pushing, and precision insertion: 1)
Pick-Cube, grasping and lifting a cube; 2) Push-T,
aligning a T-shaped block with a visible goal through non-
prehensile pushing; and 3) Peg-Insertion, inserting a
square peg into a hole with 2mm clearance through precise,
contact-rich interaction. Object and goal initial poses are
randomized across tasks.
B. Training Details
We use ManiSkill [37] with a shared vectorized simulation
environment across tasks. The action space is a = [δq, g] ∈
R8, specifying relative arm joint-position targets and the
desired gripper position. Policies observe RGB images and
proprioception, o = (I, p), where p ∈R15 contains joint
positions, joint velocities, and measured gripper position;
Push-T omits gripper position, yielding p ∈R14. Sim-
ulated cameras and robot actuator dynamics are calibrated
against the real system nominal parameters, as detailed in
sections B and C in the appendix. To promote sim-to-
real transfer, we randomize object friction and appearance,
table textures, lighting through sampled HDR environment
maps, and camera extrinsics around the calibrated values. We
additionally replace image background pixels using simulator
segmentation masks with approximately 27k natural images.
C. Evaluation Protocol
We deploy policies at 20Hz on a Franka Research 3
(FR3) arm, using RGB images from a wrist camera and
proprioception observations. We report success rate and
throughput, defined as successful task completions per unit of
rollout time, counting successful episodes until first success
and failed episodes for the full horizon. For each method
and task, we periodically evaluate training checkpoints over
1,024 simulation episodes and retain the best checkpoint
from each of three seeds. We select among these checkpoints
using five real-world rollouts each, then report the selected
checkpoint’s performance over 20 or 30 rollouts.
VI. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
We answer the following questions:
A. How does DRIFT compare with two-phase distillation
under in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD)
settings in simulation, given matched training compute
budgets?
B. Does DRIFT transfer more effectively to the real world
than two-phase distillation?
C. Which visual encoder yields the best sim-to-real success?
A. In-Distribution and Out-of-Distribution Sim Evaluations
Setup. We compare DRIFT against two-phase distillation
under matched architectures and compute budgets. Both
methods use the same frozen DINO visual encoder and
are trained for 150 NVIDIA L40S equivalent GPU hours.
We evaluate their robustness to distribution shift on the
Peg-Insertion and Push-T tasks by measuring perfor-
mance under both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution
(OOD) conditions. In Fig. 5, we evaluate four sources of
distribution shift. We evaluate four distribution shifts: (i)
Object Properties, varying mass up to 4× and scale
up to 1.2×; (ii) Initial-State Distribution, ex-
panding goal-position ranges from ±1 to ±2cm for Push-T
and ±5 to ±10cm for Peg-Insertion; (iii) Viewpoint,
applying 24-pixel translations and 0.85× field-of-view scal-
ing; and (iv) Force Perturbations, applying 80N ran-
dom forces to the gripper. Fig. 6 further evaluates general-
ization over a wider range of initial goal positions.
Results. Figure 5 summarizes the aggregate results. Under
in-distribution (ID) conditions, the two methods achieve
broadly similar performance, with DRIFT attaining an 8.6%
relative improvement in average success rate over two-
phase distillation. Under OOD shifts, however, two-phase
distillation loses approximately 42% of its ID success
rate, compared with only 14% for DRIFT. Consequently,
DRIFT’s relative advantage grows to 61% in OOD settings,
demonstrating substantially stronger generalization beyond
the training distribution.

where discrepancies between simulation and the real world
inevitably induce distribution shift at deployment.
B. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer Results
Setup. We evaluate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer across
the three tasks in Section V-A, deploying policies trained
entirely in simulation without real-world data or real-world
gradient updates. We compare DRIFT against two-phase
distillation with either a frozen or unfrozen visual encoder,
using matched architectures and compute budgets. For each
method, we select checkpoints according to Section V-C and
report real-world success rate and throughput alongside the
corresponding simulation success in Table 1.
Results. Despite only modest differences in simulation,
various methods exhibit substantially different real-world
performance. DRIFT achieves slightly higher simulation
success across all three tasks, but its advantage becomes
much larger after transfer. On Peg-Insertion, DRIFT
achieves 66.7% real-world success compared with 23.3%
for two-phase distillation with a frozen ViT, while reducing
the sim-to-real drop from 55.2 to 15.7 percentage points.
Similarly, on Push-T, DRIFT reaches 76.7% real-world
success compared with 30.0% for the frozen-ViT baseline,
corresponding to sim-to-real drops of 22.6 and 63.6 per-
centage points, respectively. Unfreezing the visual encoder
during distillation further degrades transfer on both tasks.

Setup. We compare seven visual encoder configurations
on Peg-Insertion, spanning ViT and CNN backbones

the simulation success rate across 512 evaluation episodes

Results. Among the configurations shown, DINOv3 ViT-
S/16 achieves the highest simulation success at 82.2% and
the strongest real-world performance at 66.7% (Fig. 9).
DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B with fixed sinuisoidal positional en-

and 73.2%, respectively. However, their real-world success

that strong simulation performance alone does not ensure
effective real-world transfer.
VII. CONCLUSION AND REMARKS
We present DRIFT, a simple recipe for efficient one-phase
visual RL that combines frozen pretrained vision encoders,
learned attention pooling, and fast rasterized simulation.
These choices address the computational burden of visual
learning while preserving the spatial detail that precise
manipulation demands and the robustness that real-world
transfer requires. By making such representations inexpen-
sive to use, we free more of the GPU budget for experience
collection, making it practical to learn transferable manipu-
lation policies directly from pixels.
Beyond training efficiency, DRIFT transfers substantially
better than the standard two-phase distillation pipeline,
achieving a 58.8% relative improvement in average real-
world success across three manipulation tasks under matched
architectures, observations, and compute budgets. Although
the two approaches attain broadly similar performance within
the simulation training distribution, DRIFT retains higher
success under controlled distribution shifts and on unseen
real-world objects. These results suggest that directly opti-
mizing the visual policy with RL learns more robust behav-
ior, helping explain why its advantage becomes substantially
larger when policies encounter the discrepancies between
simulation and the real world.

B. Camera Calibration
We initialize simulated cameras from calibrated real-world intrinsics and camera poses estimated relative to the robot
through hand–eye calibration. At deployment, images are center-cropped to a square and resized to the policy resolution. We
incorporate this image-coordinate transformation into the nominal simulated intrinsics as Ksim = AKreal, where A is the
crop-and-resize transform.1 Together with matched extrinsics, this aligns viewing rays and projected object scales between
simulation and preprocessed real images under the pinhole camera model.
