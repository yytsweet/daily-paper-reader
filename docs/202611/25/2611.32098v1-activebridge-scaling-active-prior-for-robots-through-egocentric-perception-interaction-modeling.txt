# Abstract 

Robotics foundation models are now widely used for manip-ulation, and egocentric videos offer rich data for scaling. However, existing methods overlook the human active pri-ors captured in egocentric videos, which limits performance 

1arXiv:2611.32098v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 in unstructured scenes. They act on given observations in-stead of actively seeking information as humans do. We pro-pose ActiveBridge , a training framework that learns these human active priors and transfers them to robots through world-action modeling. We design a robot that actively controls its viewpoint through head actions to acquire the visual information needed for manipulation using a single head camera, without wrist cameras. During human train-ing, an instruction-conditioned model jointly predicts future egocentric videos and synchronized head-wrist action tra-jectories, with head actions providing explicit supervision for active perception. Experiments show that ActiveBridge outperforms baselines on active manipulation tasks, while ablations demonstrate the benefit of joint head-wrist action supervision. 

# 1. Introduction 

Robotics foundation models have expanded from multi-task vision policies such as RT-1 [7] to vision-language-action (VLA) models that adapt pretrained vision-language models for robot control [8]. Recent world-action models (WAMs) couple future prediction with action generation, drawing on pretrained video models to learn visual dynam-ics [30, 37, 68]. Under partial observability, manipulation also requires active perception: acquiring missing informa-tion through actions such as changing the viewpoint to lo-cate an occluded object (Fig. 1). Data collection has also expanded beyond robot-specific demonstrations, with Open X-Embodiment pooling robot experience across embodiments [43]. The Universal Manip-ulation Interface (UMI) enables robot-free collection with handheld grippers and a policy interface designed for trans-fer across robot platforms [16]. Human egocentric video further extends the available experience to everyday interac-tions across diverse objects and environments [21, 37, 46]. Recent methods incorporate human action supervision into WAMs [26, 51] and train policies that coordinate viewpoint changes with manipulation [29]. Egocentric video captures how people actively gather vi-sual information, use it to guide physical interaction, and respond to the resulting visual changes. This raises our first question: how can egocentric video unlock active percep-tion and interaction capabilities in existing world-action models? More specifically, the wrist-only action supervi-sion used in previous work leaves active perception im-plicit [26, 51]. Transferring human active priors to robots also calls for closer alignment between human percep-tion and robot sensing. Humans rely on head-centered vi-sion for active perception, whereas many manipulation sys-tems combine wrist cameras with a head or external cam-era [4, 62]. Wrist-camera views are constrained by arm mo-tion, and the additional hardware increases costs and col-lision risks. This raises our second question: how can ac-tive perception be explicitly supervised, and how can robot sensing be better aligned with human perception? 

We introduce ActiveBridge , a training framework that uses egocentric videos to train existing WAMs for coordi-nated active perception and physical interaction. Condi-tioned on instructions and observations, the model jointly predicts future egocentric videos and synchronized human action trajectories. By jointly modeling active perception and physical interaction, the model learns human priors that can be adapted to each robot embodiment. To provide ex-plicit supervision for active perception, we include head ac-tions as prediction targets alongside wrist trajectories and future video. The synchronized targets link head move-ments to the accompanying physical interactions and visual observations. To better align robot sensing with human per-ception, we design a platform with a single movable head camera and no wrist cameras. On this platform, the pol-icy coordinates active perception and physical interaction by jointly controlling the head and arms. This design sup-ports active manipulation while reducing sensor costs and collision risks associated with wrist-mounted hardware. Our experiments validate the advantages of Active-Bridge. On the TAVIS-Head benchmark, which focuses on active manipulation, ActiveBridge outperforms baselines and the wrist-only variant across all six evaluation condi-tions. The in-distribution success rate reaches 77.1% on Reachy-2 and 63.8% on GR1T2. Under spatial distribution shifts, gains over direct adaptation reach 5.7% and 3.4% on Reachy-2 and GR1T2, respectively. Real-world evalua-tions further demonstrate the effectiveness of ActiveBridge across diverse manipulation tasks and the feasibility of our robot platform with a single movable head camera and no wrist cameras. The ablation study supports the benefit of the joint human video-action objective and explicit head-action supervision. Our contributions are as follows: • Human Active Prior Scaling from Egocentric Videos. 

We explore how embodied models can learn from ego-centric videos to actively acquire task-relevant perception and use it to guide physical interaction in unstructured environments, including those with occlusions. • A Novel ActiveBridge Framework and Active Robot Platform. We propose ActiveBridge, which explicitly su-pervises active perception by predicting head actions. To align robot sensing with human perception, we design a platform with a single head camera. • State-of-the-Art Performance and Active Manipula-tion Improvement. Experiments in simulation and the real world show that ActiveBridge outperforms baselines on active manipulation tasks. Ablations further demon-strate the performance improvements from ActiveBridge. 22. Related Work 

World-action models. World-action models link control to future visual prediction. Early approaches use gen-erated videos or visual subgoals to guide separate con-trollers [5, 19], or learn action-conditioned simulators [64]. Subsequent methods integrate future prediction with action learning [54, 58], jointly predict videos and actions using pretrained generators [49, 68], and develop causal video-action modeling for closed-loop control [30]. Further work examines value-guided planning [25] as well as architec-tures, information flow, and training mixtures [56]. Ego-centric WAMs study prediction targets, data alignment, ac-tion supervision, and human–robot integration [26, 51, 57]. AnyWorld factorizes action, camera, and embodiment con-trols to generate robot-domain experience [10]. Active-Bridge extends wrist-supervised human WAM training with explicit head-action targets to jointly learn active percep-tion, physical interaction, and their future visual conse-quences. 

Egocentric training. Egocentric videos support repre-sentation and reward learning [41, 42], affordance extrac-tion [1], and latent planning for control [55]. EgoVerse ex-pands this resource with diverse demonstrations and shared transfer protocols [46]. EgoMimic and HAT learn from hu-man motion through joint human–robot training and shared state-action representations [24, 47], while EgoVLA pre-dicts wrist and hand actions before retargeting and robot fine-tuning [65]. Observation behavior also supplies su-pervision. CoMe-VLA incorporates visual and propriocep-tive memory with subtask-transition supervision, while Ac-tiveScale combines history, camera-pose targets, and mixed human–robot training [29, 74]. ActiveBridge learns human active priors from egocentric demonstrations by jointly pre-dicting future video and synchronized head-wrist trajecto-ries. Head motion provides explicit supervision for view-point acquisition. 

Active perception. Active perception acquires information through sensing actions [3], while interactive perception also uses physical interaction [6]. Learning to Look Around learns view-selection policies from uncertainty-reduction rewards [23]. For manipulation, AV-ALOHA uses an inde-pendently controlled camera arm, SaPaVe adopts separate camera and manipulation action representations [17, 34], and ActiveVLA selects informative views and refines task-relevant 3D regions [36]. ActiveWAM focuses on retaining and acquiring evidence during robot control [59]. TAVIS evaluates information acquisition and viewpoint control un-der distribution shifts [50]. ActiveBridge learns observa-tion–interaction priors from human egocentric demonstra-tions. To align robot sensing with these human priors, our robot platform uses a single movable head camera that pro-vides shared visual feedback for joint head-and-arm control, without wrist cameras. 

# 3. ActiveBridge 

ActiveBridge learns human active priors from egocentric demonstrations: how people acquire task-relevant percep-tion and use it to guide physical interaction (Fig. 2). Given a task instruction and observed frames, the model jointly pre-dicts future egocentric video and synchronized head-wrist action trajectories. These targets connect actions for active perception and physical interaction to the visual observa-tions that follow. We adapt these priors to coordinated head and arm control on our robot platform, which uses a single movable head camera and no wrist cameras. 

Overview. Our data engine pairs egocentric videos with task instructions and synchronized head-wrist trajectories to construct human demonstrations. During ActiveBridge training, the model first learns human active priors from these demonstrations through joint video-action prediction and then adapts to our platform’s sensing and control in-terface through robot post-training. # 4. Experiments 

Our experiments address three questions about learning hu-man active priors: Q1: (TAVIS-Head Benchmark and Real-Robot Analysis) Does human training improve robot per-formance on tasks that require active perception and inter-action? Q2: (Ablation Study) Does explicit head-action su-pervision improve model performance? Q3: (RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark) Does the same human active prior also sup-port general manipulation? 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Simulation Benchmarks. We use TAVIS-Head [50] as our benchmark for active manipulation. TAVIS-Head in-cludes five tasks for each of the Reachy-2 and GR1T2 robots: conditional-pick, wait-then-act, clutter-pick-cube, clutter-pick-lift, and multi-shelf-scan. We use native head-cam observations, including the head camera and both wrist cameras, with policy-controlled neck motion. ID (in-distribution) evaluation uses familiar task configura-tions, while spatial OOD (out-of-distribution) evaluation changes spatial configurations and initial-pose OOD evalua-tion changes initial pose configurations. For general biman-ual manipulation, we use RoboTwin 2.0 [12], which evalu-ates 50 tasks under clean and randomized conditions. We focus the reference comparison on clean-plus-randomized training, with 50 clean and 500 randomized demonstrations per task [30]. 

Robot platform. Our robot platform uses two NERO arms for bimanual manipulation and a PiPER arm as an actuated neck, all from AgileX Robotics. The neck carries a single RealSense D455 camera and enables active viewpoint con-trol alongside arm manipulation. We deliberately omit wrist cameras to reflect an egocentric observation setting and em-phasize active perception through head motion. This min-imal camera setup also reduces sensing hardware require-ments and can lower the cost and complexity of real-world deployment. 4.2. Experimental Results 

Results on the TAVIS-Head Benchmark. ActiveBridge achieves the highest success rates in Tab. 1 across both robot embodiments and all three evaluation conditions under na-tive headcam observations. It reaches 77.1% ID success on Reachy-2 and 63.8% on GR1T2. This lead persists un-der distribution shifts, with spatial OOD success rates of 55.9% and 47.9%, respectively, and 76.1% and 58.1% un-der initial-pose OOD. The gains over both VLA and world-action baselines suggest that jointly learning head-wrist ac-tions and future observations provides a transferable prior for coordinating active perception with manipulation across embodiments and distribution shifts. Human training and explicit head-action supervision both contribute to these gains, and we analyze their effects in Sec. 4.3. 

Results on the RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark. ActiveBridge achieves success rates of 92.3% in clean conditions and 90.5% under randomization on RoboTwin 2.0 (Tab. 2). The mean success rate across the two conditions is 91.40%, compared with 91.20% of LingBot-VA. Relative to direct adaptation, success increases by 0.7% in clean conditions and decreases by 0.3% under randomization. We examine the effect of head-action supervision relative to wrist-only human training in Sec. 4.3.

4.3. Ablation Study 

We compare ActiveBridge with two variants to evaluate the effects of the joint human video-action objective and explicit head-action supervision. LingBot-VA adapts the pretrained world-action model directly to each robot. The wrist-only variant retains joint prediction of future ego-centric video and wrist trajectories during human training, while removing head-action targets. 

Effect of the joint human video-action objective. Ac-tiveBridge outperforms LingBot-VA across both robot em-bodiments and all three TAVIS-Head evaluation conditions (Tab. 1). In ID evaluation, ActiveBridge reaches 77.1% suc-cess on Reachy-2 and 63.8% on GR1T2. These scores ex-ceed those of LingBot-VA by 0.3% and 2.0%, respectively. The largest gains occur under spatial OOD, where Reachy-2 success increases from 50.2% to 55.9%. On GR1T2, suc-cess increases from 44.5% to 47.9%. These improvements of 5.7% and 3.4% exceed the corresponding ID gains on both embodiments. Under initial-pose OOD, ActiveBridge also improves the success rate by 3.6% on Reachy-2 and 1.4% on GR1T2. These improvements demonstrate the value of the joint human video-action objective during the additional human-training stage before robot adaptation. The gains under spa-tial and initial-pose shifts show that this benefit extends be-yond familiar configurations. Together, the results support learning human active priors from synchronized egocentric video and head-wrist action trajectories as an effective ini-tialization for active manipulation across embodiments. 

Effect of head-action supervision. ActiveBridge also out-performs its wrist-only variant in every TAVIS-Head condi-tion, demonstrating the additional value of explicitly super-vising head motion (Tab. 1). On Reachy-2, ActiveBridge improves the success rate by 2.4%, 1.6%, and 1.3% un-der ID, spatial OOD, and initial-pose OOD, respectively. On GR1T2, the corresponding gains are 0.7%, 1.3%, and 0.8%. The improvements across both embodiments and all evaluation conditions support the benefit of including an ex-plicit head-action target alongside wrist supervision during human training. Although the wrist-only variant learns from egocentric video and wrist action trajectories, it does not fully exploit human active priors, as the head actions underlying view-point changes are not explicitly supervised. ActiveBridge additionally predicts the synchronized head-action trajec-tory to explicitly supervise the movement associated with visual observation (Eq. (5)). On Reachy-2 ID, this addi-tion raises success from 74.7% to 77.1%. The compari-son shows that explicit head-action supervision adds value beyond the visual and wrist-action signals retained by the wrist-only variant, supporting the joint representation of perception and interaction. The benefit of head-action supervision also extends to general bimanual manipulation on RoboTwin 2.0 (Tab. 2). ActiveBridge achieves 92.3% success in clean conditions and 90.5% under randomization. These scores exceed those of the wrist-only variant by 0.5% and 0.7%, respec-tively. The average across the two conditions increases from 90.80% to 91.40%, a gain of 0.60%. These results demon-strate that the contribution of human head-action supervi-sion transfers beyond benchmarks for active manipulation. 

# 5. Conclusion 

We present ActiveBridge, a training framework that learns human active priors from egocentric demonstrations and transfers them to robots through world-action modeling. By jointly predicting future video and synchronized head-wrist action trajectories, ActiveBridge explicitly supervises ac-tive perception alongside physical interaction. This formu-lation captures viewpoint motion and manipulation within a shared predictive framework, making their coordination an explicit part of human training. On TAVIS-Head, Ac-tiveBridge outperforms baselines and two ablated variants across both embodiments and all evaluation conditions. Head-action supervision also improves general bimanual manipulation on RoboTwin 2.0 compared with wrist-only training. Real-world experiments demonstrate coordinated 8perception and interaction with a single movable head cam-era and no wrist cameras. Together, these results highlight the value of explicit head-action supervision for transferring human active priors across embodiments and manipulation settings. These findings show that learning how humans acquire visual information and act on it can improve robot manipulation.
