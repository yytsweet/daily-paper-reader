Abstract
Collecting diverse real-world manipulation demonstrations
is costly, motivating the use of simulation to augment ex-
isting demonstrations for robot policy learning. When ob-
jects, layouts, or robots change, transferring motions from a
source demonstration can produce behavior that is semanti-
cally implausible or physically infeasible in the new setting.
We introduce RoboRecast, an agentic optimization frame-
work that preserves the interactions required by the source
task while jointly optimizing the simulated scene and robot
motion to make those interactions executable in the target
setting.
When an interaction fails, the agent uses simu-
lation feedback to diagnose the cause, revise the relevant
scene or motion parameters, and test whether the revision
resolves the failure. This flexibility allows RoboRecast to
generate synthetic data from both human and robot demon-

robot embodiments. When target demonstrations are avail-
able, adding RoboRecast data improves policy success on
both real-world single-arm and simulated bimanual tasks.
Even without demonstrations in the target configuration,
RoboRecast adapts source demonstrations to support pol-
icy learning with new objects or robot embodiments.

1. Introduction
Robot policies require diverse demonstrations to general-
ize across objects, layouts, and robot embodiments, yet col-
lecting such coverage in the real world is costly, motivat-
ing the augmentation of existing demonstrations. For the
resulting synthetic data to support policy learning, the gen-
erated interactions must preserve the source manipulation
intent and remain physically executable with the specified
objects, layout, and robot.
Image and video augmenta-

tion typically enriches observations around given robot mo-
tions [6, 18, 32, 39, 40], limiting its ability to accommo-
date changes in initial object poses, object assets, or robot
embodiments. Recreating interactions in simulation allows
scenes and motions to be varied together, with explicit con-
trol and execution feedback for checking and improving
physical plausibility. Such control and execution-based re-
finement make simulation a promising route to augmenting
real-world manipulation demonstrations.
The goal of simulation-based augmentation is to gener-
ate synthetic data in which the robot completes the demon-
strated task under the physical constraints of the target
setting.
Existing work provides two building blocks for
this goal:
real-to-sim methods reconstruct the environ-
ment [4, 11, 42], while demonstration transfer adapts robot
motions to new configurations. Errors in reconstructed ge-
ometry or physical properties can prevent the intended in-
teraction, and adjusting the motion alone leaves those er-
rors unresolved. On the motion side, demonstration trans-
fer adapts source motion segments [10, 14, 21] or keypoint-
trajectory constraints [17] to the target configuration. When
a new object or layout calls for a different grasp or ap-
proach, these inherited constraints can rule out feasible
motions or produce unnatural movements. These limita-
tions raise two questions: what must be preserved from the
source demonstration, and how should the scene and robot
motion be adapted to the target configuration?
Our key insight is that the same task intent can be real-
ized in different ways, so augmentation should preserve the
intended effects of the interaction while allowing the scene
and motion to adapt. We therefore propose expressing the
intended effects as interaction requirements for each phase
and jointly optimizing the scene and robot motion to satisfy
them under observation, target, and physical constraints. In-
ferring these requirements from a real-world demonstration
requires understanding what the demonstrator is doing and
what each phase is intended to achieve. Even with these
requirements identified, the search spans a large space of
object geometries, physical properties, and robot motions,
while evaluating a candidate requires simulation rollouts
that provide no direct gradient for improving it.
We introduce RoboRecast (Fig. 1), an agentic optimiza-
tion framework that uses an agent to infer interaction re-
quirements from demonstrations and uses simulation feed-
back to guide joint optimization of scenes and robot mo-
tions. An agent is well suited to this problem because its se-
mantic understanding and reasoning enable it to infer the in-
tent behind observed actions, diagnose likely causes of exe-
cution failures, and revise the scene or motion accordingly.
By interpreting object roles, contacts, and resulting state
changes in the source demonstration, the agent identifies the
essential interactions and their ordering, then maps these re-
quirements to the target configuration. When a phase fails
to meet one of these fixed requirements or violates a phys-
ical constraint, the agent examines visual and numerical
evidence from that phase to identify a likely cause in the
reconstructed scene or robot motion. This diagnosis nar-
rows each update to the relevant object parameters or mo-
tion waypoints, allowing the agent to test focused revisions
in simulation and reduce trial and error.
With target demonstrations available, RoboRecast recon-
structions yield 38.3% policy success across three real-to-
sim tasks, compared with 18.3% for RoboSnap [42] and
23.3% for AR2S [4].
Across four layout augmentation
tasks, RoboRecast data yields 53.8% policy success, com-
pared with 33.8% for MimicGen [21] and 40.0% for CP-
Gen [17]. On the same four tasks, adding robot trajectories
converted from human demonstrations yields 46.3% policy
success with RoboRecast and 32.5% with Ego2Robot [34].
Our synthetic data also improves policy success in biman-
ual simulation. Even without demonstrations in the target
configuration, RoboRecast adapts source demonstrations to
support policy learning with new objects or robots.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We formulate demonstration augmentation as joint scene
and motion optimization, preserving task-defining inter-
action requirements while allowing their realization to
adapt to target configurations.
• We introduce RoboRecast,
an agentic optimization
framework that extracts interaction requirements from
demonstrations and jointly optimizes scenes and motions
through execution-guided diagnosis and targeted updates.
• We demonstrate that RoboRecast generates effective pol-
icy training data both with and without demonstrations
available for the target configuration.
2. Related Work
Image and Video Augmentation. Image editing and video
synthesis expand the range of visual conditions covered
by robot demonstrations.
GenAug [6] and ROSIE [40]
edit demonstration images to vary objects, backgrounds,
and distractors. RoboVIP [32] and RoboTransfer [18] em-
phasize temporally consistent multi-view video generation,
while RoboDream [39] conditions synthesis on robot mo-
tion, object priors, and scene priors. Because these methods
primarily vary observations around a given motion, they of-
fer limited flexibility when changes in initial layout, object,
or robot embodiment require a new grasp or path. More-
over, their appearance and geometry controls do not estab-
lish physical executability.
Real-to-Sim for Manipulation Data Generation. Real-
to-sim methods turn real-world observations into interac-
tive environments for demonstration generation and pol-
icy learning[13]. RE3Sim [11] combines multi-view recon-
struction with neural rendering, while RoboSnap [42] builds

Demonstration Transfer and Augmentation. Demonstra-
tion transfer adapts source motions to new configurations,
reducing the need for additional demonstrations [1, 38].
MimicGen [21] transforms object-relative segments, Skill-

and DexMimicGen [14] extends transfer to bimanual and
dexterous manipulation.
CP-Gen [17] adapts keypoint-
trajectory constraints to object variations, accounts for
smoothness during trajectory optimization, and uses motion
planning to connect segments. When a target configuration
calls for a different grasp or approach direction, transferred
motion segments or adapted keypoint constraints may ex-
clude feasible motions or produce unnatural movements.
We instead fix interaction requirements—object roles, es-
sential contacts, and state changes—while allowing paths,
timing, and unconstrained contact choices to adapt.
Agentic 3D Modeling and Manipulation. Agentic meth-
ods coordinate specialized tools and feedback for 3D
modeling and robot manipulation.
For 3D asset mod-
eling, AgentSTAR [22] reconstructs and tracks objects
from video, while Articraft [43] generates articulated as-
sets.
At the scene level, Lucida [25] reconstructs ob-
served scenes, while SceneSmith [23] and SAGE [36] gen-
erate environments from language or task descriptions. In
concurrent work, ARSTAG [16] coordinates reconstruc-
tion and data generation through cross-stage feedback,
while Real2Gym [26] builds interactive environments from
demonstrations for skill acquisition and transfer. Our agent
extracts interaction requirements from the source demon-
stration and uses them to diagnose which scene parameters
or semantic waypoints need revision.
3. Method
Overview. Given a real demonstration D and a target con-
figuration c (objects, layout, and robot), RoboRecast seeks
a successful rollout ξ that preserves the demonstrated task
intent (Fig. 2). The agent infers the required interactions
from D, maps their roles to target entities, and initializes the
scene and trajectory. If a validity check fails or the rollout
misses a required interaction, the agent uses the evidence to
diagnose the failure and update the scene or motion.

Trajectory Initialization. Within the initial target scene
S0, the agent constructs the semantic waypoint sequence
W 0 for the target robot from the mapped interaction re-
quirements. For each requirement ei, the agent chooses a
contact region and approach direction based on the target
geometry, then places waypoints to establish or maintain
the required contact. For bimanual tasks, the agent assigns
roles to the two arms and coordinates their timing according
to the required order of interactions. As it builds W 0, the
agent records which waypoint segment corresponds to each
requirement ei, so it can evaluate that interaction over the
corresponding rollout interval Ti.
3.3. Diagnosis-Driven Optimization
The agent runs all scene and motion validity checks on
the initial candidate (S0, W 0) and repeats only the affected
checks after each edit. Once the required checks pass, the
agent simulates the motion to verify the interaction require-
ments phase by phase. If a check fails or the rollout reveals
an unmet requirement or physical violation, the agent di-
agnoses the likely cause and tests a targeted update to the
relevant scene parameters or waypoints (Table 1).
Validity Checks and Trajectory Execution.
Scene va-
lidity checks assess both the consistency of the candidate
scene S with the intended setting and its physical plausibil-
ity. The scene should reflect the changes specified by the
target configuration c, while rendered views of retained el-
ements should match observations in the source demonstra-
tion D. To assess physical plausibility, the tools compare
collision geometry with object surfaces and test whether ob-
jects settle stably and articulated parts move as intended.
To compile the motion, the compiler resolves each way-
point frame Fj(S) in the current scene and interpolates be-
tween the resulting world-frame poses. Inverse kinematics
converts the interpolated poses into joint commands, while
the gripper follows the timing specified by the waypoints.
Motion validity checks flag target poses for which no IK
solution is found and joint trajectories that exceed position
or velocity limits. Once the required checks pass, the tools
execute the motion in MuJoCo [31] and record the rollout
ξ, including rendered keyframes and numerical records of
object motion and contact.
Failure Diagnosis. When a validity check fails, the agent
uses the reported violation to identify the scene element or
motion segment to investigate. For an executed trajectory,

Targeted Updates and Verification. Based on its diagno-
sis, the agent specifies which object attribute or waypoint
to change and how to change it. The editing tool applies
the requested change to the object in the scene or modifies
the waypoint sequence for the selected phase, including in-
serting a waypoint when needed. The agent associates any
new waypoint with its interaction requirement and updates
the affected evaluation intervals Ti if the timing changes.
The tools recompile the motion as needed, repeat the af-
fected validity checks, and rerun the affected motion once
those checks pass. From the resulting evidence, the agent
determines whether the edit resolved the original failure and
whether it introduced any new violations.
Checkpoint Reuse. After verifying the interactions in a
phase, RoboRecast saves the simulation state at the end of
that phase as a checkpoint. After a local edit, execution re-
sumes from the latest checkpoint whose saved state is com-
patible with the edit and whose earlier interactions remain
verified. If no checkpoint meets both conditions, execution
restarts from the beginning. When a checkpoint is reused,
the agent retains the verification results for the preceding
phases and reviews only evidence relevant to the edit.
4. Experiments
With target demonstrations, we test whether real-to-sim
reconstruction, layout augmentation, and human-to-robot
conversion improve policy success on a physical UR5, and
whether cross-simulator augmentation improves bimanual
success on RoboTwin (Sec. 4.2). Without target demonstra-
tions, we evaluate real-world policies trained solely on tra-
jectories adapted to a new object or robot (Sec. 4.3). Abla-
tions measure how interaction requirements and diagnosis-
driven updates affect generation success and optimization
time (Sec. 4.4).

4.1. Experimental Setup
Platforms.
We evaluate single-arm policies on a phys-
ical UR5 and use a physical Piper-X for robot adapta-
tion. For bimanual evaluation, we use RoboTwin’s default
Aloha configuration [5] in a cross-simulator setting span-
ning SAPIEN [37] and MuJoCo [31].
Policy Training. We train all policies using Diffusion Pol-
icy [7] with external-camera images as visual input. At each
step of a successful RoboRecast rollout, we export the ob-
servations the policy requires and the corresponding action.
We compare methods by adding equal numbers of trajecto-
ries per task to a shared base dataset while holding policy
initialization, training steps, and hyperparameters fixed.
Evaluation. We report full-task success over 20 real-world
trials or 100 RoboTwin trials per task. For real-world tasks,
we also report stage success, such as grasping the object or
pushing it partway to the goal.
4.2. Augmentation with Target Demonstrations
We collected 50 demonstrations per task by teleoperating a
UR5 (Real-50). Across four tasks—stacking cubes, placing
a cube into a bowl, pushing a cube across a line, and open-
ing a microwave (Fig. 3)—we compare three ways to sup-
plement Real-50: real-to-sim reconstruction, layout aug-
mentation, and human-to-robot conversion (Table 2).
Real-to-sim reconstruction.
We first supplement Real-
50 with 50 trajectories per task reconstructed by Ro-
boSnap [42], AR2S [4], or RoboRecast. Adding RoboRe-
cast reconstructions to Real-50 raises mean full-task suc-
cess from 22.5% to 36.3% across all four tasks, with suc-
cess on the three rigid-object tasks reaching 38.3% for Ro-
boRecast versus 18.3% for RoboSnap and 23.3% for AR2S
(Table 2). Figure 4 suggests one reason for this gap. Ro-
boRecast more closely matches the relative sizes of the cube
and bowl and the distance from the cube to the target line, so
its demonstrations better reflect the placement tolerance and

pushing distance required on the real robot. For microwave
opening, AR2S cannot model the articulated microwave,
and RoboSnap’s open-source pipeline requires manual as-

Layout augmentation. Given the same 50 RoboRecast re-
constructions per task, MimicGen [21], CP-Gen [17], and
RoboRecast each generate 50 trajectories with varied initial
object positions. We then add each method’s augmented tra-
jectories to the same training set of 50 real demonstrations
and 50 RoboRecast reconstructions. Mean full-task success
reaches 33.8% with MimicGen, 40.0% with CP-Gen, and
53.8% with RoboRecast, up from 36.3% before augmenta-
tion (Table 2). MimicGen transfers object-relative motion
segments, while CP-Gen adapts keypoint trajectory con-
straints from the source demonstration. RoboRecast pre-
serves the required contacts and state changes while allow-
ing the robot to approach and move the object along a path
suited to its new position. Across both comparisons, gains
in full-task success exceed those in stage success, consistent
with more natural and coherent motions whose training ben-
efits may accumulate across successive interaction stages.
Human-to-robot conversion.
We collected 50 human
demonstrations per task and converted them into robot
trajectories using either Ego2Robot [34] or RoboRecast.
Adding these trajectories to Real-50 raises mean full-task
success from 22.5% to 32.5% with Ego2Robot and to
46.3% with RoboRecast (Table 2). Ego2Robot retargets the
observed hand motion to the robot and synthesizes the cor-
responding images. RoboRecast further tests whether the
gripper’s contact produces the intended object motion in
simulation, and revises the interaction when it fails. Adding
50 layout variations of the converted trajectories per task
(OurH2RAug-50) further raises mean success to 56.3%.

4.4. Ablation Studies
We examine how explicit interaction requirements and
diagnosis-driven updates to the scene and motion affect Ro-
boRecast’s ability to generate successful rollouts efficiently.
For each of 20 DROID [15] demonstrations, all variants
start from the same initialized scene and waypoints. Each
optimization run allows up to ten rollouts and stops early
if the agent reports that the task is complete. We report
mean optimization time across demonstrations, measured
from the end of initialization.
Explicit interaction requirements. We test whether the
agent benefits from explicit requirements for each stage by
comparing against a variant prompted only to reproduce the
source trajectory and correct its execution. Both variants
use the same tools, editable parameters, and rollout budget,
and we manually assess whether each final rollout com-
pletes the task. With explicit requirements, 20 of 20 roll-
outs succeed (100%) in 223 seconds on average, compared
with 13 of 20 (65%) in 348 seconds for trajectory repro-
duction. These results suggest that specifying the intended
effect of each stage helps the agent identify where execution
first falls short and focus its edits on that interaction.
Diagnosis-driven joint optimization. Once the agent iden-
tifies a failed interaction, it must determine whether the
cause lies in the scene or the motion and which parameters
to revise. We test how the choice of editable parameters
and the scope of each update affect optimization (Table 6).
From the same initialization, task success is 30% without
optimization and 75% when the agent can revise only the
trajectory. When both scene and trajectory parameters are
editable, allowing the agent to revise multiple blocks per
update yields 90% success in 388 seconds on average. Tar-
geting one block at a time based on the diagnosed failure
yields 100% success and reduces mean optimization time
to 223 seconds. This suggests that some failures require
scene corrections and that targeted edits can reduce the risk
of introducing new errors while resolving a failure.

5. Conclusion
RoboRecast generates synthetic data by preserving task-
defining interaction requirements while adapting scenes
and motions to target configurations. It extracts these re-
quirements from source demonstrations and uses simulation
feedback to diagnose failures and identify which scene pa-
rameters or robot motions to revise. Adding our synthetic
data to target demonstrations improved policy success on
real single-arm and simulated bimanual tasks. Without tar-
get demonstrations, it enabled training for new objects or
robots. These results support interaction requirements as a
useful abstraction for preserving manipulation intent while
allowing flexible adaptation across configurations. We dis-
cuss limitations and future work in detail in the appendix.

A. Implementation Details
A.1. Skills and Tools
Table S1 lists the skills and tools we provide to RoboRe-
cast, our agentic optimization framework for demonstration
augmentation. Skills guide the agent in interpreting demon-
strations, constructing candidate scenes and motions, and
deciding how to revise them when a check or rollout fails.
The agent invokes programmatic tools for modeling, motion
compilation, validity checks, and simulation, and interprets
their outputs to determine what to change.
A.2. Method Details
Parallel modeling of scene units.
The agent uses the
source scene graph to group parts that must fit together into
modeling units, while assigning independently movable ob-
jects to separate units. For example, the countertop, inset
sink, and faucet form a single modeling unit so that the sink
fits the countertop opening and the faucet is positioned over
the basin. Modeling these fixed assemblies together pre-
serves their attachment relationships, avoiding unintended
tipping or displacement during settling that can arise when
attached parts are treated as independent objects.
To reduce scene modeling time, subagents process dif-
ferent units in parallel, following AgentSTAR’s work-
flow [22] to alternate between reconstructing geometry and
appearance and refining pose estimates. Each subagent also
specifies its unit’s collision geometry, physical properties,
and joints where applicable, as described in Sec. 3.2. The
agent then assembles the units according to their poses and
the scene graph, and checks that the assembled scene pre-
serves the observed support and contact relationships.
Compiling semantic waypoints. The three motion modes
defined in Sec. 3.1—transit, dwell, and sustained contact—
share an interpolation scheme but differ in how their way-
points are placed. Transit waypoints connect poses in free
space, dwell waypoints hold the end effector at a fixed pose
for a specified duration, and sustained-contact waypoints
trace a path defined by the interaction geometry, such as
the arc of a door about its hinge. For the segment from
waypoint j −1 to waypoint j, let u = (t −tj−1)/∆tj de-
note normalized time. After transforming the waypoint po-
sitions into the world frame, denoted by ¯pj, the compiler in-
terpolates between successive positions using a quintic time
law [20],

poses and their respective grasp locations. During rollout,
the agent verifies contact timing, and collision checks flag
unintended contacts within each arm, between the arms, and
with the scene. For example, if an edit delays the receiv-
ing arm during a handover, the agent extends the delivering
arm’s hold and verifies that it releases the object only after
the receiving gripper has secured it.
Human-to-robot conversion.
Hand poses provide the
agent with reference information about how the demonstra-
tor interacts with objects, guiding its construction of seman-
tic waypoints for the target robot. These poses can come
from dataset annotations or off-the-shelf estimators such as
WiLoR [24], depending on the available input. Together
with the video, the thumb and index fingertip positions and
their relative motion help the agent infer contact regions,
grasping directions, and the timing of grasp and release
(Fig. S1). The agent uses these cues alongside the interac-
tion requirements, target object geometry, and robot reach-
ability to choose grasp poses, approach paths, and gripper
commands when initializing the waypoints.

A.3. Training Data Generation and Export
Once a MuJoCo [31] rollout is accepted, we convert it into
an observation–action sequence tailored to the downstream
policy. For the Diffusion Policies [7] used in our exper-
iments, we sample external-camera images, actual robot
joint positions, and gripper widths from the recorded rollout
at the policy’s control frequency. Each observation is paired
with the subsequent control command, which specifies the
target joint positions and gripper width. These observation
12


and action conventions match those of the real demonstra-
tions, allowing synthetic and real episodes to be used by the
same policy training pipeline.
To generate the visual observations, we restore the saved
simulation states and render each frame from the calibrated
external camera. The background can be represented using
Gaussian splats generated by InfiniSplat [33] or incorpo-
rated through 2D compositing. Geometry-based visibility
masks preserve the rendered robot and task objects when
integrating the background. The resulting images are re-
sized to the policy’s input resolution and stored alongside
the corresponding robot states and actions.
We trim inactive intervals at the beginning and end of
each episode while retaining pauses within the task, ap-
plying the same temporal crop to images, states, and ac-
tions. Episodes generated through reconstruction, layout
augmentation, or human-to-robot conversion share the same
data format, with joint ordering and gripper-width map-
pings adapted to the target robot. Before export, we check
that timestamps align, image and state counts match, and
action labels agree with logged control targets.

B.1. Evaluation Protocols and Metrics
Policy training.
We train Diffusion Policy [7] using
external-camera images and robot proprioception as obser-
vations. A ResNet-18 [12] encoder trained from scratch
encodes the images, and a conditional 1D U-Net predicts
joint-position and gripper-width target sequences. Across
all policy evaluations, we train each policy for 600 epochs
using the same architecture, random seed, and optimizer
settings within each comparison. Table S2 summarizes the
training configuration.
Policy evaluation. We evaluate each policy over 20 trials
per task on the physical robots and 100 trials per task in
RoboTwin [5], using the same predefined initial configu-
rations across methods. On UR5 and Piper-X, a trial fails
if the policy does not complete the task within 60 seconds
or triggers the robot’s protective stop.

object partway toward the goal. We report the arithmetic
mean of per-task success rates, specifying the evaluated task
subset whenever methods do not cover all tasks.
Demonstration generation. We evaluate generation suc-
cess by whether the synthesized rollout completes the re-
quired task interactions in simulation. The agent determines
when to stop refinement, and we manually review the fi-

C. Limitations and Future Work
Further improvements to RoboRecast concern generation
efficiency, support for mobile manipulation, and the visual
realism and diversity of synthesized observations.
Generation Efficiency and Experience Reuse. Although
model choice affects generation cost, processing each
demonstration still requires scene modeling and repeated
simulation to diagnose and correct interaction failures. Sim-
ilar failures can recur across demonstrations, motivating the
reuse of previously effective repairs. As a preliminary ex-
ploration, our Experience Library stores resolved failures
together with their diagnostic evidence and the modeling or
motion edits that addressed them. The agent retrieves these
records to guide subsequent revisions and verifies their ap-
plicability through simulation in the current scene.
During real-to-sim reconstruction of demonstrations we
collected on physical UR5 for four tasks, we build the li-
brary from the first 40 demonstrations per task and recon-
struct the remaining ten with and without reuse. Experience
reuse reduces mean end-to-end generation time by 4.9%–
21.0% across the four tasks. These preliminary results sug-
gest that repairs learned from earlier demonstrations can re-
duce repeated trial and error within the same task. Extend-
ing this benefit across tasks will require identifying which
aspects of a repair generalize and when they remain appli-
cable to new objects and interactions.

Our policy evaluations focus on
fixed-base single-arm and bimanual manipulation.

its simulated counterpart. This setting requires coordinat-
ing base and arm motion to maintain contact with the chair
as the robot moves. Future work could extend diagnosis-
driven optimization to jointly refine base trajectories and
