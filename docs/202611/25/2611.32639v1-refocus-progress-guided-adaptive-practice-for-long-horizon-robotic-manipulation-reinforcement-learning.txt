## Abstract 

Online reinforcement learning (RL) enables vision-language-action policies to improve through environmental interaction. However, in long-horizon manipulation, sparse success/failure feedback provides little information about where policies encounter bottlenecks. Moreover, repeated full-task rollouts also leave these bottlenecks weakly ana-lyzed and insufficiently practiced. To this end, we intro-duce ReFocus , a progress-guided multi-round RL frame-work that alternates Global with Local RL to identify task-specific bottlenecks and allocate additional practice to them. Specifically, during Global RL, we decompose each task into a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of subtasks. Then a DAG-based Progress Reward is leveraged for both pol-icy training and bottleneck diagnosis. Based on the iden-tified bottlenecks, ReFocus selects Adaptive Practice Win-dows, defining task segments from an entry stage to a target stage. It allocates additional interaction to these segments during Local RL while updating the entire policy. After that, it evaluates the policy to perform the full-task for the next round RL, forming an iteration between Global and Local RL. Experiments on LIBERO-Long and RoboCasa365 show that ReFocus achieves success rates of 97.8% and 51.3% respectively, compared with 90.6% and 39.3% for the π0.5

baseline. Furthermore, we evaluate a real-world adapta-tion on three long-horizon tasks. On BreadBaker, ReFo-cus achieves 95% success, compared with 30% for the π0.5.Project page: https:// refocus-robotics.github.io/ 

## 1. Introduction 

In long-horizon manipulation, the success of a subtask de-pends on both its execution and the state inherited from preceding stages. Completing one subtask therefore does not guarantee successful continuation. Imitation learn-ing gives vision-language-action (VLA) policies a strong initialization by supervising actions along expert trajecto-

> †Corresponding authors.

ries [5, 6, 34]. At deployment, however, successive subtasks begin from states produced by the policy itself, and demon-strations provide limited supervision for handling the devi-ations that accumulate along the way, resulting in failure. Online reinforcement learning (RL) enables VLA poli-cies to learn beyond demonstrations with further environ-mental interactions by themselves. However, most online RL methods, which learn from full-task rollouts using only binary terminal feedback, still struggle with long-horizon tasks. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), two episodes with different failures—one from bowl transport and the other from shelf placement—receive the same zero reward despite different amounts of completed work. Our diagnostic further reveals concentrations of failures at specific stages, as shown in Figs. 8 and 9 of Appendix A. Although some work recovers intermediate supervision by estimating progress between observations or rewarding verifiable subtask completions [41, 51], two challenges re-main: 1) Reward Imbalance. To characterize intermediate progress, we group verifiable subtasks with shared prereq-uisites into stages, within which subtasks can be completed in any order. Even with prerequisite gating and one-time credit, the total reward assigned to a stage can depend on its decomposition granularity. Uniform per-subtask rewards, for example, give a stage more weight simply because it contains more completion events. 2) Limited Bottleneck Practice. Under a fixed interaction budget, the stage lim-iting full-task success can receive few opportunities for practice and less comprehensive analysis. For example, in Fig. 1(a), each new attempt at shelf placement requires an-other grasp and transport, and an earlier failure prevents that attempt altogether. Moreover, there is no deeper analysis of the reason for the failure of shelf placement. To this end, we introduce ReFocus , a progress-guided RL framework for long-horizon manipulation. ReFocus al-ternates Global RL with Local RL to improve a shared VLA policy. During Global RL, we represent each task as a di-rected acyclic graph (DAG) of subtasks and their dependen-cies. Reinforcement Learning for VLAs. Reinforcement learn-ing improves pretrained VLA policies using offline data or online interaction. Offline methods use preferences, action values, or recovery data [18, 20, 56], while online methods optimize policies with actor–critic or group-relative objec-tives [11, 25, 29, 31]. Further work studies efficient training and real-world adaptation [1, 9, 13, 43, 45, 53]. For long-horizon tasks, terminal outcomes provide limited feedback on partially completed trajectories. To provide feedback beyond task outcomes, reward and critic models incorpo-rate intermediate progress [15, 24, 26, 41, 55], stage struc-ture [8, 16], or execution history [12, 49]. Building on these approaches, ReFocus uses DAG-based progress feedback to allocate equal reward budgets across stages and credit sub-task completions according to prerequisites. 

Long-Horizon Manipulation. Long-horizon tasks require policies to complete multiple subtasks while handling er-rors accumulated during execution. Prior work addresses long-horizon tasks by incorporating subtask prerequisites or downstream success into rewards [30, 51]. Other ap-proaches focus learning on difficult subtasks through spe-cialist policies [22, 28, 40], or allocate practice using budget-aware scheduling, reset curricula, and task selec-tion [4, 14, 17, 42]. Execution feedback has also been used to improve skills and policies across iterations [10, 44, 48]. In contrast, ReFocus adaptively selects task-specific prac-tice windows across rounds using subtask progress, full-task evaluations, and practice history. 

## 3. Method 

In this section, we first introduce Global RL with Progress Reward for full-task policy learning ( §3.1). We then present Local RL, which directs interaction toward stages requiring further improvement ( §3.2). Finally, we describe the op-timization paradigm that alternates between phases to im-prove a shared policy across training rounds ( §3.3). The overall pipeline of ReFocus is shown in Fig. 2. 

3.1. Global RL with Progress Reward 

We consider a set of long-horizon manipulation tasks T and a shared VLA policy πθ initialized through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). At each decision step k, the policy receives an input ok, comprising the visual observation and task in-struction, and generates an action chunk ak. Global RL optimizes this policy using full-task rollouts. To provide denser feedback than terminal rewards, we use a frontier-3LLM-driven Scheduler S to construct task DAGs and define progress rewards for valid subtask completions. 

Task DAG. Each task is represented by a directed acyclic graph G = ( V, E ), where nodes V denote subtasks and edges E specify prerequisites. Subtasks with the same pre-requisites form an ordered stage and can be completed in any order. For example, placing two vegetables in a basin forms a stage with two subtasks. Both placements must be credited before the next stage becomes eligible for reward. 

DAG-based Progress Reward. To balance progress re-wards across stages, we allocate an equal reward budget to each stage and divide it uniformly among its subtasks. Let 

zv,k ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether subtask v receives its first valid completion credit at action chunk k, after its prerequi-sites have been credited. 3.2. Local RL with Adaptive Practice 

Local RL focuses interaction on bottleneck stages that re-main difficult for the current policy. The Scheduler S uses validation rollouts and the History Buffer H to select task-specific practice windows and specify fine-grained mile-stone rewards for updating the shared policy. 

Bottleneck Diagnosis. To identify stages that require ad-ditional practice, we evaluate the policy on the valida-tion set every five updates during Global RL. Each vali-dation rollout provides a progress sequence p0: K , an ob-servation sequence o0: K , and the full-task outcome, where 

K denotes the final decision step. We summarize these records as D. The Scheduler S compares progress se-quences across unsuccessful rollouts to identify the earliest stage where progress repeatedly stalls. It then examines the corresponding visual observations to describe the stalled ac-tions and supporting evidence, reporting uncertainty when the evidence is insufficient. The resulting diagnosis guides practice-window selection. 

Adaptive Window Selection. The Scheduler combines the current diagnosis D with the History Buffer H, which stores information from previous practice rounds, to select a prac-tice window around the identified bottleneck. This informa-tion guides whether to retain the previous window, revise its scope, or skip further practice. The Scheduler specifies a window w = ( sentry , s target , B ), where sentry defines the starting progress, starget defines the stage to complete, and 

B limits the environment interaction within the window. Along with the window, it provides a set of milestones M

and their verifiable completion conditions to guide practice within the selected task segment. For example, shelf place-ment can be decomposed into secure holding, alignment, and placement with release. 3.3. Alternating Optimization 

We alternate Global RL and Local RL for several rounds to optimize the shared actor πθ and critic Vϕ. Each round comprises full-task training, bottleneck diagnosis and win-dow selection, and local practice. After Local RL, we eval-uate the updated policy from normal task starts to assess task progress and task-wise success rates. These results are stored in H alongside the selected windows and interaction costs. Together with fresh diagnoses from the next Global RL phase, this history guides whether to retain, revise, or skip practice windows. Repeated assessment keeps sub-sequent practice aligned with the shared policy’s remain-ing difficulties. Algorithm 1 summarizes this procedure. In Algorithm 1, rG and rL collect the scalar rewards of their respective rollouts. The vectors c, spre , and ∆s col-lect task-wise practice costs, pre-practice success rates, and post-practice success-rate changes, respectively. Both phases optimize the policy using PPO [39], while differing in their rollout distributions and rewards. ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks and Evaluation Settings. We conduct experiments on RoboCasa365 Composite-seen [33] and LIBERO-Long [27]. Compared with tasks centered on in-dividual skills, these benchmarks require sustained progress across multiple subgoals, making them suitable for study-ing intermediate feedback and targeted practice. LIBERO-Long contains 10 tabletop tasks involving multiple ob-jects and goal conditions. We also use 16 Composite-Seen tasks from RoboCasa365 that extend evaluation to diverse kitchen activities involving object transport and appliance interaction. For each benchmark, we initialize π0.5 through SFT on the official demonstrations and subsequently train a shared policy across all selected tasks. Validation rollouts are used to diagnose bottlenecks and guide practice-window selection. We evaluate each policy on 50 and 100 initial sce-narios per task for LIBERO-Long and RoboCasa365. 

Baselines. We compare ReFocus with the SFT-initialized 

π0.5 policy and four representative methods: Sparse-RL [53], SimpleVLA-RL [25], PolicyTrim [45], and Struc-tRL [51]. Sparse-RL PPO uses a binary success reward for PPO, while StructRL provides a subtask-level progress-reward baseline. All RL baselines use the same training configuration. More details about training and evaluation configurations are provided in Appendix C. 

4.2. Main Results 

Overall Performance. Table 1 compares ReFocus with SFT and representative RL post-training methods. ReFocus achieves the highest overall success rates on both bench-marks, reaching 51.3% on RoboCasa365 and 97.8% on LIBERO-Long, exceeding the SFT baseline by 12.0 and 7.2 points, respectively. Compared with Sparse-reward PPO [53], it improves by 10.2 and 5.2 points. ReFocus also outperforms StructRL [51] by 5.5 and 1.6 points, respec-tively. The gains over both sparse- and structured-reward baselines support the effectiveness of combining progress feedback with adaptive practice. 

Performance across Task Horizons. ReFocus achieves the highest success rate in all six horizon groups. Experimental Setup. We deploy the policies on a Unitree G1-D humanoid robot. ## 5. Conclusion 

In this work, we present ReFocus, a progress-guided multi-round RL framework for long-horizon manipulation that al-ternates Global and Local RL to improve a shared VLA pol-icy. Specifically, during Global RL, a DAG-based Progress Reward balances reward allocation across stages for pol-icy training and supports bottleneck diagnosis. After identi-fying bottlenecks, Adaptive Practice Windows concentrate additional interaction on the corresponding bottlenecks dur-ing Local RL. Experiments on LIBERO-Long and Robo-8Casa365 show that ReFocus improves the SFT baseline from 90.6% and 39.3% to 97.8% and 51.3%, respectively. A RECAP-based adaptation extends the framework to three real-world tasks, achieving 95% success on B READ BAKER

B.2. State Storage and Restoration 

Training Entry Collection and Storage. During Global RL, we collect a bounded bank of restorable entries at action-chunk boundaries. For each rollout, we retain the first eligible entry for each unfinished stage encountered. Entries are indexed by round, task, and stage, with up to four records per worker in each bank. Once a practice window is selected, we validate its task identifier and stage bounds, confirm entry availability, and retrieve matching records from the current round. Each entry stores the reset arguments needed to reproduce it: the episode seed, executed action prefix, flattened simulator state, and the reward checker’s latched completion flags and timers. Each record also includes the task and round identifiers, source update, stage-definition signature, and scene-XML fingerprint. These fields support entry matching and consistency checks during restoration.

B.3. Practice Scheduler: Inputs, History Buffer, and Prompt Templates 

For each task, the Scheduler receives the instruction, ordered stage definitions, progress records, and recorded environment outcomes, together with a 4 × 4 timeline montage of frames sampled along each rollout. Each cell contains synchronized external and wrist-camera views, when available, labeled with the control step and task stage. Figure 10 shows an example from DeliverStraw . The diagnosis output is then passed to the Scheduler together with the available training entries. Previous interventions are supplied through task-level History Buffer records containing the selected window and mile-17 stones, realized rollout modes, fresh-interaction and prefix-replay costs, and pre- and post-practice success counts with their evaluation denominators. Records also retain task, round, and checkpoint identifiers. The Scheduler retrieves records from up to two preceding rounds, including evaluations immediately before Local RL, after five Local updates, and at the end of the Local phase. Detailed prompts provided to the Scheduler are presented below. 

Task DAG Construction Prompt 

You are a robot task planner. Given the task instruction, scene information, and available completion checks, construct a Task DAG of verifiable subtasks and their dependencies. The DAG defines eligibility for progress rewards, and its completion records support bottleneck diagnosis and practice−window selection. Requirements: 1. Represent each subtask as a node and each prerequisite relation as a directed edge. The graph must be acyclic. 2. Encode the DAG as ordered stages. Subtasks within a stage must have no dependencies on one another and may be completed in any order. Every subtask in a stage must be credited before the next stage becomes eligible for reward. This specifies credit eligibility, not simultaneous execution. 3. Preserve meaningful dependencies. For example, placing chicken and vegetables in a bowl may share one stage, while placing the loaded bowl on a fridge shelf requires both placements to have been credited. 4. Describe each node as one atomic, physically verifiable event using a short verb phrase, such as grasp an object, place an object in a receptacle, open a fixture, or press a button. 5. Include meaningful intermediate progress. For continuous activities such as stirring, washing, or scrubbing, use verifiable milestones supported by the supplied completion checks. Do not invent simulator signals or thresholds. 6. Exclude redundant retreat motions and a generic task−finished node. The environment checks full−task success separately. Each valid subtask completion is credited once per episode. Output format requirement: Return only a valid JSON object matching the structure below. C.6. Reward Allocation Analysis 

We select three multi-stage RoboCasa365 tasks to examine how the distribution of subtasks affects reward allocation across stages. Stage 1 contains two subtasks in each task. In StoreLeftoversInBowl , the robot puts chicken and vegetables into the bowl. In LoadDishwasher , it loads two dishes. In StirVegetables , it adds two vegetables to the pot. Each later stage has one subtask. With uniform per-subtask rewards, Stage 1 receives 50% of the progress budget in the first two tasks and 40% in StirVegetables , while each later stage receives only 25% or 20%, respectively. This imbalance means the policy receives a stronger training signal for early-stage completions and a weaker signal for the stages that remain difficult — precisely where additional practice is most needed. Stage-balanced rewards give each stage an equal share of the budget regardless of how many subtasks it contains, preventing any single stage from dominating the reward signal. Table 15 summarizes progress in archived normal-start rollouts. For StirVegetables , 21 of 25 rollouts completed Stage 1, but only 10 completed the final tracked stage, illustrating how uniform per-subtask rewards can allocate more credit to an early stage even when progress frequently stalls later. The reward-feedback ablation on LIBERO-Long (Table 4 in the main paper) further corroborates this: stage-balanced allocation improves overall success by 2.6 points over structured per-subtask rewards, with the gap widening to 9.0 points on the hardest task-horizon group (400–550 steps).

## F. Limitations and Future Work 

Some Local RL rollout modes rely on restoring intermediate simulator states. Since comparable mid-episode resets are impractical on physical hardware, the real-world experiments of ReFocus employ RECAP-based post-training on recorded rollouts as an alternative. In current version, ReFocus employs a frontier LLM as the Scheduler, which operates between training rounds to diagnose execution bottlenecks and allocate practice windows accordingly. A direction for future work is to extend from an inter-round advisor to an online Co-Worker that remains active throughout task execution. Under this paradigm, when the VLA policy stalls at a bottleneck subtask, the Co-Worker would intervene by selecting and triggering an appropriate recovery skill, temporarily assuming control until task progress is verified, at which point execution would be returned to the policy. 27
