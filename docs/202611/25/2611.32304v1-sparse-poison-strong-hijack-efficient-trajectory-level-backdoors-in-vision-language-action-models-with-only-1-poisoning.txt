Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models advance robotic ma-nipulation, but their reliance on demonstrations exposes them to backdoor threats. Among these threats, trajectory-level targeted backdoors are particularly challenging and may pose greater safety risks than behavior-disrupting at-tacks: rather than merely causing task failures or local ac-tion deviations, they must sustain closed-loop redirection to complete an attacker-specified multi-step task. To reliably in-duce such behaviors, existing methods typically rely on high poisoning rates to obtain sufficient target-trajectory supervi-sion. However, high poisoning rates may compromise clean-task performance, whereas low rates leave target-trajectory supervision scarce and backdoor learning vulnerable to in-terference from dominant clean-task updates. To resolve this trade-off at low poisoning rates, we propose a two-stage co-optimization framework guided by vision, language, and action. Stage I freezes the policy and uses differentiable ren-dering and multimodal supervision to optimize a 3D trigger’s texture, translation, and scale, strengthening trigger–target associations from limited poisoned demonstrations. Building on the optimized trigger, Stage II applies source-selective dual-LoRA adaptation to decouple clean and backdoor up-dates and mitigate their interference, followed by learned column-wise calibration and analytic fusion into a unified policy. Experiments on OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π0, and 

π0.5 across four LIBERO suites show consistent gains over all evaluated attack baselines. With only 1% poisoned data, our method achieves 86.4% target success and 95.2% clean-task success on OpenVLA-OFT in LIBERO-Spatial. Real-robot trials also demonstrate trajectory-level hijacking.Our code is available at https://github.com/vla-attack/Sparse_Poison_Strong_Hijack .

This need for sustained control across successive action steps intensifies the trade-off among poisoning rate, attack reliability, and clean-task performance in trajectory-level hijacking. As Fig. 1(a,b) shows, increasing the poisoning rate improves existing attacks’ target success rate (TSR), but often at the expense of clean success rate (CSR). At low poi-soning rates, attacks face two fundamental bottlenecks. First, 

scarce target-trajectory supervision weakens trigger–target associations, making it difficult to learn reliable multi-step redirection. Second, interference from dominant clean-task updates hinders backdoor learning: in conventional mixed training, clean demonstrations dominate shared parameter updates, and conflicting clean and backdoor gradients can further suppress learning from rare poisoned samples, as confirmed by our preliminary gradient analysis in Fig. 3(a). To address these complementary bottlenecks, we propose a two-stage co-optimization framework guided by vision, language, and action, targeting reliable trajectory-level hi-jacking under sparse poisoning while preserving clean-task performance. 2. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models learn instruction-conditioned robot control from demonstrations, enabling policies to map multimodal observations and language in-structions into executable actions. Early work explored vision–language knowledge transfer through action tokeniza-tion (RT-2 [29]) and large-scale cross-embodiment training (Open X-Embodiment [19]), establishing foundations for general-purpose robotic manipulation. Recent VLA models differ substantially in their action representations and pre-diction mechanisms. OpenVLA [13] predicts discretized ac-tions autoregressively, whereas OpenVLA-OFT [12] adopts continuous action chunks and parallel decoding to improve manipulation efficiency. π0 [3] generates continuous ac-tions through flow matching, while π0.5 [2] further enhances generalization through heterogeneous training data. These ar-chitectural differences influence how policies represent and execute multi-step manipulation behaviors. Nevertheless, all four models rely on demonstration-based learning, ex-posing their training and adaptation processes to potentially poisoned data. This shared dependency motivates our cross-architecture evaluation of trajectory-level backdoor risks, spanning both discrete and continuous action generation. 

2.2. Attacks on VLA Models 

Attacks on VLA models fall into inference-time perturba-tions and training-time backdoors. Related work examines decision-level attacks on LLM-based embodied agents [17], LLM jailbreaking [4], and reasoning-model red teaming [6]. Inference-time attacks manipulate observations or instruc-tions during deployment without modifying parameters. Methods exploit adversarial visual patches [18, 22, 24], text [11], or 3D object textures [5, 8]. These approaches expose vulnerabilities to manipulated inputs, but differ from backdoors embedded during policy adaptation. Training-time backdoors associate specific triggers with malicious behaviors through poisoned demonstrations, al-lowing policies to behave normally on clean inputs while deviating when triggered. Existing attacks induce task failures, action manipulation, or stealthy trajectory devia-tions [1, 9, 23, 25, 27]. Unlike such disruptions, trajectory-level targeted backdoors require sustained redirection to com-plete an attacker-specified manipulation task. Such comple-tion entails coherent approach, grasp, transport, and place-ment across multiple closed-loop control stages. GoBA [28] and BackdoorVLA-VT [15] pursue goal-directed trajectory hijacking, yet reliable completion remains difficult under sparse poisoning: scarce supervision weakens trigger–target associations, while dominant clean-task updates interfere with backdoor learning. Their use of fixed triggers and shared parameter updates motivates optimizing both the trig-ger and adaptation process. Related to our second stage, ATAAT [7] addresses gradient conflicts through feature or-thogonalization or parameter isolation. In contrast, our framework combines differentiable multimodal 3D trigger optimization with source-selective dual-LoRA adaptation, conflict-aware updates, and calibrated fusion, targeting re-liable trajectory hijacking under limited poisoning budgets while preserving clean-task performance. 

3. Method 

Our two-stage multimodal framework induces trajectory-level targeted backdoors under sparse poisoning (Fig. 2). Stage I optimizes a 3D trigger; Stage II mitigates update in-terference and fuses the learned adaptations into one policy.

4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Models and benchmarks. We evaluate four VLA models: OpenVLA [13] with discrete action tokens, and OpenVLA-OFT [12], π0 [3], and π0.5 [2] with continuous actions. Four LIBERO [16] suites— Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long (LIBERO-10)— cover spatial relations, object diversity, goal-conditioned behaviors, and long-horizon tasks. 

Attack setting. We study white-box offline adapta-tion with a fixed 1% demonstration-level poisoning budget. When a physical trigger object appears, the backdoored pol-icy should grasp it and place it stably on a plate despite an unchanged instruction. Suite-specific target demonstrations follow this tabletop pick-and-place structure (Sec. B). 

Baselines. We compare against the targeted backdoors GoBA [28] and BackdoorVLA-VT [15], and the untargeted BadVLA [27]. For a controlled comparison, both targeted baselines are adapted to all four models with the same base trigger object, target trajectories, and data sampling protocol (Sec. D). GoBA and our method preserve the original in-structions, whereas BackdoorVLA-VT adds a textual trigger. OpenVLA-OFT adaptation starts from Clean-150k. 

Evaluation protocol and metrics. Each setting uses 

500 clean and 500 triggered closed-loop episodes ( 50 per task). We report clean success rate (CSR; original-task suc-cess without the trigger), target success rate (TSR; attacker-specified task completion with the trigger), and untargeted attack success rate (UASR; original-task failure with the trig-ger). TSR is primary; CSR measures clean-task preservation. UASR includes failures that never reach the target, so it is not complementary to TSR: the two need not sum to 100% .Clean-only CSR comes from published evaluations; TSR and UASR are evaluated with the trigger present.

5. Conclusion 

We investigate trajectory-level targeted backdoors in VLA models under sparse poisoning. Our framework combines multimodal 3D trigger optimization with conflict-aware dual-LoRA adaptation and calibrated fusion to strengthen target-trajectory learning while preserving clean-task performance. Experiments across four VLA models and four LIBERO suites, alongside real-robot trials, demonstrate sustained trajectory-level hijacking with only 1% poisoning. Stage-wise analysis shows improvements in grasping, transport, and placement beyond initial target approach. Post-hoc de-fenses only partially suppress the backdoor, often degrad-ing clean-task performance. Limited to white-box offline adaptation, our study leaves generalization to unseen envi-ronments and object configurations unexplored. Future work will investigate such generalization and effective defenses. These findings highlight the need to safeguard VLA models throughout training, adaptation, and deployment. 6. H. Real-Robot Setup 

Physical system configuration. The system comprises an AgileX PiPER 6-DoF arm, a parallel gripper, and an Intel RealSense D435i camera, capturing RGB observations at 

640 × 480 and 30 fps. The policy processes visual obser-vations, proprioception, and language instructions, issuing continuous action commands at 20 Hz, while the low-level controller tracks the commanded robot motion at 200 Hz. We adapt π0.5 to the physical platform. Clean-task suc-cess requires successful completion of all instructed objec-tives, including both sequential manipulation steps in T4.
