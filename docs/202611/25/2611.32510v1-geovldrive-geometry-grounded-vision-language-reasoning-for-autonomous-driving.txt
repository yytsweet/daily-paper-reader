Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) planners inherit semantic
knowledge from pretrained vision-language models, but
their reasoning is rarely grounded in 3D scene structure
and its evolution over time. We introduce GeoVLDrive, a
geometry-grounded VLA framework for 3D spatiotemporal
reasoning and planning. Multi-layer features from a 3D
foundation model augment the vision-language backbone
through gated cross-attention. A structured latent chain
of thought (CoT) organizes future scene geometry, key-
agent motion, and meta action into ordered, separately
supervised states. An action expert generates trajectory
proposals from these states and multi-depth scene features,
and ranks them with scene-grounded, interacting score
tokens. We also introduce NAVSIM 3DQA to train and
evaluate metric 3D spatial and motion understanding.
GeoVLDrive achieves 91.8 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 and 90.9
EPDMS on NAVSIM v2. On NAVSIM 3DQA, the geometry-
grounded backbone scores 89.2% overall, 4.6 percentage
points above the fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B baseline. On
LingoQA, it reaches 68.6% Lingo-Judge accuracy.
1. Introduction
End-to-end autonomous driving has advanced through joint
optimization of perception, prediction, and planning [15,
16, 20, 21]. Building on the semantic knowledge of
pretrained vision-language models, vision-language-action
(VLA) frameworks use visual observations and language in-
puts to support driving trajectory generation [9, 18, 40, 52,
54]. Driving requires reasoning about traffic participants,
scene geometry, and future scene evolution [6, 7, 16, 21].
This motivates grounding such reasoning in 3D geometry
and scene semantics to connect current spatial relationships
and their future evolution with ego planning.
Representative approaches differ in how they incorpo-
rate semantic reasoning and geometric representations into
trajectory planning (Fig. 1). VLA policies (Fig. 1(a)) build
on pretrained vision-language models for scene understand-
ing and reasoning to guide trajectory generation [9, 18, 52].
Geometry world action models (Fig. 1(b)) encode cur-
rent scene geometry and predict its future evolution, con-
ditioning planning on geometric representations of the
observed and anticipated scene [49]. Geometry-grounded
VLAs (Fig. 1(c)) use observed-scene 3D geometric features
or reconstruction supervision to support spatial understand-
ing and trajectory planning [42, 47].
A central challenge is to connect scene-wide geomet-
ric forecasts with agent-specific motion reasoning and ego
maneuvers. Recent VLAs incorporate geometric and dy-
namic knowledge into latent representations used for plan-
ning [17, 19, 33]. We focus on structuring this connection
through distinct, separately supervised latent groups for fu-
ture scene geometry, the motion of a selected key agent, and
meta action. Directed dependencies link scene forecasts to
agent motion and maneuver selection.
We propose GeoVLDrive, a geometry-grounded VLA
framework for 3D spatiotemporal reasoning and planning
(Fig. 1(d)).
Multi-layer geometric features augment the
vision-language backbone with 3D spatial information. A
structured latent chain of thought (CoT) organizes future
scene geometry, key-agent motion, and meta action into
separately supervised latent states. Its attention mask lets
future-motion states read future-geometry states and meta-
action states read both. An action expert uses the latent CoT
and multi-depth geometric and semantic features to jointly
generate and score trajectory proposals. Dedicated score to-
kens incorporate scene and reasoning context and exchange
information across proposals, allowing each candidate to
be evaluated in relation to the alternatives. We construct
NAVSIM 3DQA to train and evaluate box-conditioned met-
ric 3D and motion understanding on NAVSIM scenes.

2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models for Driving
VLA models use pretrained semantic knowledge for
trajectory planning. DriveLM [38] frames driving un-
derstanding as graph-structured question answering, and
EMMA [18] incorporates textual reasoning into action gen-
eration. Textual chains of thought [45] expose intermedi-
ate reasoning but add autoregressive decoding overhead.
AutoVLA [54] and AdaThinkDrive [32] adapt the use of
such reasoning to driving scenarios. Geometry-grounded
models add 3D information. VGGDrive [42] injects fea-
tures from a frozen geometry backbone via multi-layer
cross-attention. VLGA [47] supervises a geometry expert
with dense pointmap reconstruction, and DVGT-2 [56]
jointly reconstructs current geometry and predicts trajecto-
ries. Selection-based planners, including Hydra-MDP [27],
WoTE [25], and iPad [12], use learned scores to select
candidate trajectories. GeoVLDrive combines geometric
grounding with structured reasoning about future scene evo-
lution for ego planning. It uses iPad’s proposal formulation
with dedicated score tokens.
2.2. Latent Reasoning and World Modeling
Latent CoT methods replace explicit reasoning traces with
continuous intermediate states. Coconut [14] recurrently
propagates hidden states, CCoT [5] learns compressed
traces, CODI [37] uses hidden-state self-distillation, and
SIM-CoT [46] adds step-level supervision through an aux-
iliary decoder. In driving, LaST-VLA [33] and CoWorld-
VLA [17] transfer geometric and dynamic priors into
separately supervised latent groups.
PerceptDrive [29]
routes distilled perception priors into world-action model-
ing; DriveWorld-VLA [19] shares latent features between
world modeling and planning. OneVL [31] uses language
and future-frame decoders to supervise shared latents. Dyn-
VLA [36] predicts compact dynamics tokens, LCDrive [39]
interleaves action proposals with predicted outcomes, and
ColaVLA [35] derives meta-action representations for par-
allel planning. Other approaches support planning through
video prediction [4, 10, 44], latent forecasting [24], oc-
cupancy [51], occupancy-flow prediction [30], and future
geometry prediction [49]. SGDrive [22] is closest to our
design.
It supervises scene, agent, and goal queries in
a VLM with occupancy, safety-critical agents, and a 4-s
goal pose, learning geometry through occupancy supervi-

3. Method
GeoVLDrive grounds a vision-language backbone in 3D
geometry and introduces a structured latent CoT connecting
future scene geometry, key-agent motion, and meta action
(Fig. 2). An action expert jointly generates and scores tra-
jectory proposals conditioned on the latent CoT and multi-
depth geometric and semantic features.
3.1. Geometry-Grounded Vision-Language Back-
bone
We combine a pretrained Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct back-
bone [2] with a frozen StreamVGGT geometry encoder [49,
55]. The VLM encodes the visual input, driving prompt,
and current ego state, while StreamVGGT aggregates four
front-camera frames to extract current-frame geometric fea-
tures {Gℓ}ℓfrom multiple layers.

Qwen3-VL decoder layers [6, 13, 20, 26], using zero-based
layer indices (Fig. 2(b)). For each paired depth, the decoder
hidden states query the projected geometry tokens through
a gated cross-attention residual [1]:

Spatial alignment of semantic and geometric features.
To spatially align the VLM’s visual representations with ge-

(u, v) using a shared 2D sinusoidal positional encoding.
The resulting encodings are added to the visual queries and
the geometry keys and values (Fig. 2(c)). This provides
a common image-plane reference across feature grids of
different resolutions, helping cross-attention associate fea-

jection and spatial encoding improve the backbone’s 3D
scene understanding and metric localization, as shown by
the NAVSIM 3DQA ablations in Table 4.
3.2. Structured Latent Chain-of-Thought
We organize future scene geometry, key-agent motion, and
meta action into three supervised groups of latent states that
form the latent CoT. The annotations select at most one key
agent per scene (Sec. B.4 of the supplementary material).
At inference, these states are computed in one backbone
forward pass. During training, textual CoT provides autore-
gressive language supervision.
We predict scene geometry over 2 s and key-agent mo-
tion over 4 s, using residual representations relative to the
current state. The action expert reads both these hidden
states and scene features directly (Sec. 3.3).
Structured attention mask.
The mask in Fig. 3 orders
the latent CoT. Future key-agent motion states attend to
future geometry states. Meta-action tokens attend to both
groups.
The mask blocks reverse connections between
groups. Geometry tokens attend to their own and earlier
prediction horizons. Future-motion tokens attend to one an-
other bidirectionally. Their direct access to geometry states
is limited to the same or earlier horizons.

3.3. Action Expert
Our action expert jointly generates and scores trajectory
proposals conditioned on the latent chain and multi-depth
geometric and semantic features (Fig. 4). Causal joint atten-
tion directs information from reasoning to actions and from
actions to scores. Dedicated score tokens combine scene
context with proposal-specific action states and exchange
information across candidates for trajectory selection.
Multi-depth joint attention.
The expert is a dedicated
transformer operating on latent reasoning states, ego-state
tokens, R × J action tokens, and R score tokens. Geo-
metric and semantic features from multiple depths are con-
catenated with these states at corresponding expert layers.
At each selected depth, Qwen features are compressed to
64 tokens by attention pooling with learned queries. All
VLM-derived inputs are detached before the trainable pool-
ing and projection layers, preventing planning gradients
from updating the VLM. Masked self-attention and an FFN
jointly update the resulting sequence under the causal joint-
attention mask in Fig. 4(b), which enforces the token-group
visibility order while allowing bidirectional attention within
each group. The refined reasoning, action, and score states
propagate across S refinement stages, with stage s decod-
ing R trajectory proposals τ (s)

4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation Details
Datasets.
We use LingoQA [34] and NAVSIM 3DQA for
visual question answering.1 NAVSIM-WM-CoT (Sec. B.4
of the supplementary material) provides chain-of-thought
annotations and supervision for future geometry and key-
agent motion. For planning, we train on navtrain and eval-
uate on the 12,146 scenes in navtest [3, 7]. Inference uses
front-camera images and ego state without LiDAR.
NAVSIM 3DQA. We derive NAVSIM 3DQA from NAVSIM
annotations to train and evaluate the backbone on ob-
ject geometry and motion.
We will publicly release
NAVSIM 3DQA when we submit the camera-ready ver-
sion. Each sample comprises four consecutive front-camera
frames at time offsets [−1.5, −1.0, −0.5, 0] s relative to the
current frame, ordered from oldest to newest, and a ques-
tion about an object identified by a normalized 2D bound-
ing box in the current frame. Questions cover four task
types: coarse object category, ground-plane position in me-
ters, vehicle dimensions, and motion behavior. The dataset
contains approximately 1.28M QA pairs from 95K anchor
frames, including 1.12M training pairs and 165K validation
pairs. The training and validation sets follow NAVSIM’s
official partition. Dataset construction is detailed in Sec. A
of the supplementary material.
Metrics.
We report the Predictive Driver Model Score
(PDMS) on NAVSIM v1 and the Extended PDMS
(EPDMS) on NAVSIM v2 for planning evaluation. Both
aggregate safety, comfort, and progress; v2 includes ad-
ditional evaluation criteria.

Training details.
We use Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [2] as
the vision-language backbone and StreamVGGT [55] as the
geometry encoder, which remains frozen throughout train-
ing. Training proceeds in four phases. The first aligns se-
mantics and geometry on the question-answering mixture
for 3 epochs. The second supervises future geometry and
key-agent motion for 5 epochs. The third trains the super-
vised latent chain for 10 epochs, and the fourth trains the
chain and the action expert jointly for 30 epochs. Training
uses NVIDIA H20 GPUs, with phase-specific GPU counts
and batch sizes listed in supplementary Table S3. Sec. B
of the supplementary material describes the latent interface,
loss definitions, and training schedule.

5. Conclusion
GeoVLDrive combines multi-layer geometry injection with
latent-state supervision for future scene geometry, key-
agent motion, and meta action. The action expert uses these
states and multi-depth scene features to generate and score
trajectory proposals. The latent CoT and geometry injec-
tion raise NAVSIM v1 PDMS from 90.1 to 91.8, and the
full model reaches 90.9 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2 with one
front camera and supervised training only. Scene-grounded,
interacting score tokens add 1.6 PDMS over an MLP read-
out. Geometry injection improves box-conditioned metric
localization and motion recognition on NAVSIM 3DQA.
Limitations and future work.
GeoVLDrive uses a single
front camera and models at most one key agent per scene.
Future geometry is supervised over 2 s, while key-agent mo-
tion and planning extend to 4 s. Training targets follow the
recorded future. We evaluate with NAVSIM’s non-reactive
pseudo-simulation.
Future work includes surround-view
8


input, multi-agent interactions, longer geometry horizons,
multimodal futures, and closed-loop evaluation.

A.4
Comparison with SURDS
NAVSIM 3DQA complements SURDS [13] by emphasizing metric geometry and temporal motion. SURDS evaluates
orientation, depth ranges, pixel localization, and pairwise spatial relations from individual nuScenes images, using
categorical correctness and a centerness-based localization score. NAVSIM 3DQA uses four consecutive front views
and a normalized 2D box to specify the queried object. It adds numerical estimation of ground-plane position and
vehicle dimensions in meters, together with temporal motion classification.
The geometric diagnostics assess physical location and scale. Position accuracy at L2 ≤1 m jointly constrains
longitudinal and lateral errors in the ego frame; joint dimension accuracy requires length, width, and height all to
be within 0.5 m of their targets. These criteria distinguish metric accuracy from correct depth-range or relative-order
predictions, which can remain correct despite substantial coordinate errors. Estimating physical dimensions further
probes object scale, which image-plane localization alone does not measure. Both tasks require numerical answers
without candidate values supplied in the question.
Motion classification extends the evaluation to temporal observations, with labels derived from ego-motion-
compensated tracks and local road geometry. Categories such as stationary, crossing, and cut-in are therefore defined
15


by object motion relative to the ground and the road rather than by apparent image motion. Together, the tasks pro-
vide complementary diagnostics of metric spatial grounding and temporal motion understanding relevant to planning,
alongside SURDS’s orientation and multi-object relation tests.
B
Implementation and Training Details
B.1
Architecture and Latent Interface
Backbone configuration.
StreamVGGT features from layers [4, 11, 17, 23] are injected into Qwen3-VL layers
[6, 13, 20, 26], respectively. The VLM and geometry streams use image resolutions of 416 × 672 and 280 × 518
(height × width), with a 20×37 patch grid in the geometry stream. Features across the selected geometry layers differ
in representation depth while sharing the same spatial resolution.
Latent token interface.
The observed visual and language prefix is followed by an ego-state token, future-geometry
tokens, key-agent motion tokens, and meta-action tokens. Group-specific embeddings replace the reserved-token
embeddings at these positions in the VLM input sequence. With δ = 0.5 s, future geometry uses K = 4 horizons and
32 tokens per horizon (128 in total). Agent motion uses ten tokens for category, current position, and eight current-
relative future offsets. Two tokens encode longitudinal and lateral meta actions. Future-geometry and future-motion
tokens receive sinusoidal encodings of their prediction times, with attention governed by the latent CoT mask in Sec. 3
of the main paper. Textual CoT targets follow the latent block during training. At inference, planning uses the latent
states without autoregressive text generation.
Coordinate frames.
Agent positions and ego trajectories are expressed in meters in the ego frame at the observation
time, with x forward and y left. Future agent annotations are transformed to this fixed frame before computing the
current-relative position offsets ∆pj = pj −p0, where p0 is the agent’s current position.
Action expert.
The expert comprises four refinement stages, each containing four independently parameterized
attention blocks conditioned on features from different depths. The corresponding Qwen3-VL readout indices are
[8, 16, 22, −1] (zero-based), with −1 selecting the final hidden state. It maintains R = 64 proposals, each represented
by J = 8 action tokens and one score token. Reasoning, action, and score states are propagated between blocks, while
scene features are supplied at each depth. Action queries incorporate ego-state and meta-action context. VLM features
and latent states are detached before the expert’s trainable projections, blocking expert gradients from updating the
VLM while allowing refinement within the expert.

Key-agent annotations.
For each scene, we select at most one key agent from front-camera-visible objects with
complete future tracks and verified spatiotemporal interaction with the ego route. Agent labels describe realized
behavior based on future ground truth and map-based spatial relations. The decision_causal flag identifies
agents whose behavior provides evidence supporting the recorded ego action. The selected agent is labeled as vehicle,
pedestrian, cyclist, or other; none denotes the absence of a selected agent. A valid motion target comprises the
current position and eight future positions sampled at 0.5 s intervals in the current ego frame. Future offsets follow the
coordinate convention in Appendix B.1. Regression requires a valid track, while category supervision includes none.
The validation annotations contain 5,087 scenes with a selected agent and 13,092 without one. The none label refers
only to agent selection and does not exclude other planning constraints.
Meta-action labels.
Lateral meta-action labels are refined using the original decision, navigation command, route
geometry, and future ego trajectory, while preserving the original longitudinal label and eight-point ego trajectory.
Longitudinal labels are maintain speed, accelerate, decelerate, stop, and reverse. Lateral labels are keep lane, turn
left, turn right, change left, change right, nudge left, and nudge right. These labels supervise both the backbone
meta-action tokens and the expert’s auxiliary meta-action readouts.
Textual CoT.
We generated initial textual CoT annotations from templates using route-control information, the ego-
action rationale, and the selected key agent when present. Other agents provided scene context. GPT checked the
drafts against the structured scene annotations and proposed corrections. Human annotators reviewed a sample of the
revised annotations.
