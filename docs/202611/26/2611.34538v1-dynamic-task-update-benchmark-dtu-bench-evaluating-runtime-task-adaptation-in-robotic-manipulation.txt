# Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies perform well on fixed-instruction robotic manipulation benchmarks, but their ability to adapt to instruction changes during execu-tion remains understudied. We introduce the Dynamic Task Update Benchmark (DTU-Bench), evaluating 40 task revi-sions across seven update types and three execution stages using LIBERO, a simulated robotic manipulation bench-mark. We compare performance when a new instruction is given before execution starts versus during execution, and examine whether the new task instructions are followed. Across 13 representative VLA configurations, average task success is 95.8% on the original object-manipulation tasks, compared with only 1.6% when the instruction changes which object to manipulate during execution. Controlled diagnostics show that policies often complete the original task before the updated one, and that their responses de-pend on the robot’s physical state. We further test whether a simple scripted controller that releases the grasped object and moves the arm back helps robots respond to instruction changes. This improves success from 2% to 38% for π0.5

and from 8% to 48% for DM0.5, but can cause unwanted object motion. These findings reveal limitations that fixed-instruction benchmarks and task-success metrics alone can overlook in evaluating robotic manipulation. Project page: 

https://dtu-bench.github.io/ .

# 1. Introduction 

Robots operating alongside people may need respond to changes in instructions [22, 24]. A user may change where an object should be placed, request a different object, or withdraw part of an instruction after the robot has already begun acting. Unlike starting a new task, such updates arrive while the robot is executing and may have already made some progress on the initial task. When an instruc-tion change arrives, some actions associated with the initial task may still be useful, while others may need to be aban-doned or undone [15]. Recent advances in robot learning have greatly improved language-conditioned manipulation. Generalist vision-language-action (VLA) policies such as OpenVLA [13] and 

π0 [1] have demonstrated increasingly capable manipula-tion across tasks and environments. Benchmarks such as CALVIN [20] and LIBERO [19] have supported evaluation of VLA policies on long-horizon task execution, general-ization, and knowledge transfer. However, success on tasks specified before execution does not necessarily mean a pol-icy can adapt when the task changes during execution. A policy may successfully execute the original or the updated tasks independently, yet fail to transition between them once execution has begun. Recent studies have addressed different challenges in dy-namic robot execution. DynamicVLA [26] focuses on ma-nipulating moving objects, while TIC-VLA [12] addresses reactive control. SwitchVLA [16] and ReSteer [6] develop training-based approaches to task switching. However, how existing VLA policies respond when different parts of a task are changed during execution remains insufficiently under-stood. Moreover, reaching the updated goal does not nec-essarily indicate that the robot has responded to the new in-struction. This motivates a systematic evaluation of runtime task adaptation and post-update behavior. We introduce Dynamic Task Update Benchmark ( DTU-Bench ) to systematically evaluate how robot policies re-spond to task instructions that change during execution. As illustrated in Fig. 1, DTU-Bench introduces new instruc-tions while the robot is acting, without resetting its state. Built on LIBERO, the benchmark covers 40 task revisions across seven types of instruction changes and three execu-tion stages. We evaluate each policy under three condi-tions: executing the original task, performing the revised task from the initial state of the original task, and switching to the revised task during execution. These comparisons al-low us to assess whether policies can complete a revised task after making progress towards a different objective. Beyond task success, we examine whether robots maintain the requested outcome and how their behavior changes fol-1arXiv:2611.34538v1 [cs.RO] 25 Nov 2026 Figure 1. Overview of DTU-Bench. 

lowing an instruction update. Our main contributions are: • We introduce DTU-Bench, a systematic, policy-agnostic framework for evaluating runtime task updates in robotic manipulation across seven task-revision categories and three execution stages. The benchmark and evaluation code will be released. • We evaluate 13 VLA configurations and identify limita-tions that fixed-instruction evaluation can overlook, in-cluding differences across update types, delayed satisfac-tion of updated goals, and substantial gaps between tem-porary and final goal satisfaction. • We conduct controlled experiments to examine how the robot’s execution state affects responses to new instruc-tions, including whether simple scripted interventions can help recover from failed task updates. 

# 2. Related Work 

Embodied Foundation Models. Vision–language–action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation map visual obser-vations and instructions to actions through autoregressive token generation [13, 21, 25], continuous regression [14], or flow matching [1, 2]. WorldVLA and RynnVLA-002 jointly model actions and future visual observations [4, 5], while VLA-JEPA and Light-WAM incorporate predictive representations or supervision [17, 23]. Hierarchical mod-els such as τ0-VLA further integrate world-model-guided planning [3]. Despite this architectural diversity, adaptation to mid-execution task changes remains insufficiently evalu-ated. 

Dynamic Execution and Task Adaptation. Dynam-icVLA [26] addresses moving-object manipulation, while TIC-VLA [12] combines asynchronous reasoning and con-trol. These methods primarily respond to changing ob-servations rather than task objectives. More directly, SwitchVLA [16] trains a contact-aware policy for task switching, while ReSteer [6] improves policy steerability through generated training data. Rather than developing a new switching policy, DTU-Bench focuses on evaluat-ing different types of task revisions, distinguishing revised-task competence from runtime performance and examining whether updated-goal success reflects a response to the new instruction. 

Benchmarking VLA Robustness and Adaptation. 

LIBERO [19] provides a benchmark for language-conditioned manipulation tasks; LIBERO-Plus [10] evaluates robustness to visual, spatial, and linguistic per-turbations; and LIBERO-Safety [7] examines physical and semantic safety. DTU-Bench instead focuses on changes to task requirements during execution. It investigates original tasks, updated-from-start controls, and runtime updates, assessing whether updated goals are reached and maintained, alongside diagnostics of post-update behavior. 

# 3. DTU-Bench 

DTU-Bench (Fig. 1) evaluates runtime task adaptation in robotic manipulation through structured task updates, execution-stage interventions, and measures of updated-2goal attainment, persistence, and post-update behavior. 

3.1. Task Update Formulation 

In conventional language-conditioned manipulation, a pol-icy executes a fixed instruction I0 toward a goal G0. In DTU-Bench, execution begins under I0, and a revised in-struction I1, specifying goal G1, is delivered at time t = τ

while the system is in an intermediate state sτ . The in-struction conditioning the policy changes at the intervention time:

This distinguishes runtime adapta-tion from executing the revised task from the initial state: earlier actions may still be useful, may need to be redi-rected, or may have created conflicts with the new goal. 

3.2. What Changes: Task Update Taxonomy 

We ground our taxonomy in the task representation of clas-sical planning. Given a fixed planning domain, a planning problem can be described by a set of objects O, an initial state s0, and a goal specification G [11]: 

P = ( O, s 0, G ). (2) Here, G specifies the conditions required to complete a task. In DTU-Bench, instruction updates change the target object, the required task outcome, or both, while preserving the ob-jects and scene at the time of the update. We include seven types of task updates, grouped into four broader categories (Table 1): Entity (2 types) changes which object the robot must manipulate (Object identity) or how the target object is identified (Spatial referent); Goal (3 types) changes where an object should be placed (Des-tination), its required relation to another object (Placement relation), or the required final state of the environment (Ter-minal state); Entity/Goal (1 type) replaces one required sub-goal with another while leaving the remaining subgoals unchanged (Subgoal replacement); and Multiple (1 type) 

changes two or more independent task requirements within the same instruction (Compound goal). Each task-update scenario is assigned to one of the seven types, although some changes may involve more than one aspect of the task. Additional experiments on task cancel-lation and unrelated instructions are conducted on a subset of policies, with results reported in the supplementary ma-terial.

3.4. When It Changes: Execution-Stage Interven-tions 

We introduce instruction changes at three stages of execu-tion: early , middle , and late . These stages are defined by task-progress events rather than fixed action counts. For example, in pick-and-place tasks, they correspond to ap-proaching an object, grasping or lifting it, and moving it towards its destination. If the robot does not reach the re-quired stage, the update is recorded as not delivered. When the instruction changes, we discard any remaining actions predicted under the original instruction and generate new actions using the revised instruction and current obser-vations. The robot is then given 300 action steps to complete the revised task, regardless of how many steps it has already ex-ecuted. We continue execution even after the revised task is completed to check whether the required outcome is main-tained. We additionally evaluate selected continuations over 600 actions to examine how a longer execution horizon af-fects task completion and maintenance. 

3.5. What Remains: Progress Compatibility and Execution State 

The effect of a task update depends not only on what changes in the goal, but also on the progress made and the physical state reached during execution prior to the update. We consider two complementary aspects of compatibility with the updated goal G1.

Task-level progress compatibility concerns which parts of the original task remain useful, which are no longer 3needed, and what must be done to complete the revised task. In multi-stage tasks, completed subgoals may remain useful even when other requirements change. For example, if both tasks require opening a drawer, the robot does not need to open it again. 

Execution-state compatibility concerns how the robot’s physical configuration affects subsequent behavior. At the intervention state sτ , robot posture, existing grasps, and ob-ject configurations may facilitate or constrain execution of the updated goal. For example, changing the destination of a grasped object preserves useful progress, whereas chang-ing the target object may require releasing the grasped ob-ject and selecting another. These two aspects motivate our analysis of post-update behavior (Sec. 4.2). 

3.6. Evaluation and Diagnostic Analysis 

DTU-Bench evaluates runtime task adaptation through complementary measures of task competence, update de-livery, goal attainment, and post-update behavior. Table 2 summarizes the evaluation components and the evidence each provides. The benchmark protocol applies to all eval-uated policies, while controlled diagnostics are performed on selected tasks and configurations. 

Task competence and update delivery. For each task and initial state, we evaluate three conditions: (1) an orig-inal control executing I0 from the initial state s0; (2) an 

updated-from-start control receives I1 before any task ac-tions are executed and starts from the initial state s0; and (3) runtime updates that begin with I0 and switch to I1 at the intervention state sτ . The first two conditions evaluate performance on each task separately, while the third evalu-ates performance when the instruction changes during exe-cution. 

Task Success and Goal Maintenance. We evaluate the updated-goal predicate G1 after each executed action using three metrics: Ever , whether the goal is satisfied at least once; Held-10 , whether it remains satisfied for ten consec-utive observations; and Final , whether it is satisfied at the end of the 300-step post-update execution. Held-10 mea-sures sustained goal attainment over a short interval, while Final measures whether the goal remains satisfied at the end of execution. Their difference helps identify cases where the updated goal is temporarily attained but not maintained until the end of execution. 

Matched-state behavioral diagnostics. Goal attain-ment alone does not establish whether a policy changes its behavior in response to an updated instruction. For selected intervention states, we restore the same recorded state and compare two continuations: one retaining the original in-struction I0 and the other receiving the updated instruc-tion I1. # 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmark and evaluation protocol. We evaluate DTU-Bench using all four LIBERO suites: Spatial, Object, GOAL, and Long. The full evaluation design includes 40 source tasks, 50 official initial states per task, and five ex-ecution conditions: original-from-start, updated-from-start, and runtime updates at early, middle, and late stages. The full evaluation design comprises 10,000 scheduled trials per policy configuration, with the two from-start controls shared across the three intervention stages. Each delivered update is evaluated for 300 executed actions. We report the evaluation coverage, valid trials, and ex-clusions for each policy in the supplementary material. Tri-als with simulator errors are excluded from the success-rate calculations. 

VLA baselines. We evaluate a series of publicly released VLA configurations covering different action-generation mechanisms (autoregressive action tokens, direct contin-uous regression, and flow-based action prediction). The 4models also span conventional vision-language-action ar-chitectures, action-expert policies, and approaches incor-porating world-model or predictive video supervision: (1) OpenVLA [13], (2) OpenVLA-OFT [14], (3) π0 [1], (4) 

π0.5 [2], (5) DM0.5 [8], (6) WorldVLA [5], (7) RynnVLA-002 [4], (8) UniVLA [25], (9) VLA-JEPA [23], (10) In-structVLA [27], (11) τ0-VLA [3], (12) MolmoAct2 [9], and (13) FlashVLA-π0.5 [18]. We use released LIBERO checkpoints without additional training. Each configuration retains its native observation inputs, action representation, and inference interface while following the same task-update protocol and goal evalua-tion. For models with additional planning or world-model components, we evaluate only the released policy configu-ration supported by the tested interface, rather than assum-ing that the full planning system is active. Checkpoint de-tails, execution settings, and evaluation coverage are pro-vided in supplementary material. 

Statistical analysis. We estimate pointwise 95% con-fidence intervals by resampling initial-state blocks within each source task, keeping all conditions and intervention stages paired. Task-cluster bootstrap and leave-one-task-out analyses assess sensitivity to task composition. Full results are reported in the supplementary material.

5.1. Responses Differ by Task Update Types 

Table 3 compares the final task success of original-task and runtime updated-goal across seven update categories. Because these scores measure different task requirements, their difference cannot be attributed solely to the instruc-tion change. Across the reported configurations, destina-tion updates resulted in 36.4–68.8% runtime Final success, compared with at most 13.5% yielded by object-identity updates and 1.2% for spatial-referent updates. Destina-tion changes often preserve manipulation of the same ob-ject, whereas object updates require selecting another tar-get. However, scene layout and manipulation task diffi-culty also vary across categories, preventing attribution of the observed performance difference solely to the type of task update. We confirm this gap in a same-scene compari-son (Sec. 5.5). 

Finding 1: The VLAs achieved substantially higher task success rates when encountering destination updates com-pared to object-identity and spatial-referent updates. How-ever, this difference may be caused by factors in addition to the update type.

5.4. Reaching the New Goal ̸ = Adaptation Success 

Runtime success measures whether the updated goal is achieved, but does not capture how or when it is reached. 

Instruction-dependent goal attainment. We compare 94 matched pairs of continuations from identical interven-tion states, with one branch following the original instruc-tion and the other receiving the update. Both branches are evaluated against the updated goal. Under Held-10, 37 pairs succeed with both instructions, three succeed only with the update, and 54 fail under both. All 37 both-success cases involve terminal-state updates, indicating that the updated goal can be temporarily satisfied even when following the original instruction. However, at the end of execution (Fi-nal), only five updated-instruction continuations still satisfy the updated goal, while none of the original-instruction con-tinuations do. 

Delayed goal completion. In a LIBERO-Object task where the instruction changes from placing alphabet soup to placing cream cheese, DM0.5’s Held-10 success increases from 4/15 at 300 actions to 15/15 at 600 actions. All 15 trajectories complete the original goal before the updated goal (Fig. 6). This behavior extends across multiple VLAs: among successful Object updates, the original goal is com-pleted first in 95.8% of DM0.5 cases (184/192), 90.8% for MolmoAct2 (59/65), 94.7% for FlashVLA (18/19), and 90.0% for π0.5 (9/10). These results show that achieving the updated goal does not necessarily indicate a prompt re-sponse to the instruction change. 

Temporary goal satisfaction. Terminal-state updates provide another example of why goal attainment alone can be misleading. For terminal-state updates, DM0.5 achieves 64.9% Held-10 success but only 0.22% Final success, while 

π0.5 achieves 62.9% and 0.67%, respectively. For example, a robot may temporarily leave a drawer open before contin-uing to close it. Such outcomes distinguish temporary goal attainment from sustained satisfaction. 

Preserving useful progress. Runtime updates do not always require abandoning previous work. In a LIBERO-Long example, the instruction changes from placing cream cheese and butter in a basket to placing cream cheese and soup in the basket. DM0.5 preserves the already completed cheese placement and adds the newly requested soup with-out placing the obsolete butter. 

Finding 4: Reaching the updated goal does not necessar-ily mean the robot changed its behavior promptly or main-tained the goal afterward.

Both policies succeed in all 10 destination-update continuations, including after grasping the original object. In contrast, neither succeeds in the five object-update continuations after stable grasp, although all updated-from-start controls succeed. Finding 6: Simple physical assistance can mitigate some task-update failures without policy retraining, although its benefits and physical consequences depend on the update type and execution stage. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We introduced DTU-Bench to evaluate how VLA policies respond to instruction changes during execution. Across 13 configurations, we found substantial differences across up-date types and execution stages, while success on fixed tasks did not guarantee runtime success. Behavioral diagnostics revealed delayed completion and temporary satisfaction of updated tasks. Training-free assistance improved some fail-ures but could also disrupt useful progress and introduce undesirable object motion. These findings highlight the im-8portance of evaluating task success alongside post-update behavior and physical execution.
