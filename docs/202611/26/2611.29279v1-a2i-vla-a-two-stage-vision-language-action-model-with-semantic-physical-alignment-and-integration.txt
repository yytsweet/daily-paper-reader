## Abstract 

Force and tactile feedback provide essential information about physical interactions in contact-rich manipulation that visual observations alone cannot reliably capture. However, integrating these signals into vision–language–action (VLA) models remains challenging owing to the representational gap between visual–language semantics and physical mea-surements. To address this challenge, we propose A2I-VLA, a two-stage VLA framework that first learns semantically 

Aligned force–tactile representations and then Integrates them into a VLA model. In Stage I, a cross-modal alignment loss is introduced to promote correspondence between se-mantic and physical representations. The tactile and force feature streams are then integrated through a hierarchical multimodal fusion network, and the resulting representations are jointly supervised by future wrench and action predic-tion losses. The former encourages the model to anticipate physical responses, while the latter associates force–tactile observations with task-relevant manipulation behaviors. In 

1arXiv:2611.29279v1 [cs.RO] 22 Nov 2026 Stage II, a gated cross-attention bridge module adaptively integrates the hierarchical physical features learned in Stage I into the VLA model to guide action generation. Real-world experiments demonstrate that the proposed A2I-VLA signif-icantly outperforms the baseline π0.5 across three contact-rich manipulation tasks, improving the success rate from 70% to 95%. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in vision-language-action (VLA) models [1, 3, 7–9, 13, 19–23, 27] have demonstrated considerable potential across diverse robotic manipulation tasks by con-necting visual observations and task instructions with action generation. However, contact-rich manipulation requires robots to perceive and respond to physical interactions that vi-sual observations alone cannot reliably capture [4, 6, 11, 24]. In such settings, visually similar configurations may corre-spond to different contact conditions and require different actions. Force and tactile feedback can help resolve this ambiguity by providing complementary information about interaction forces and local contact, making their effective integration important for contact-rich manipulation. Existing methods [5, 6, 11, 24, 25] typically encode force or tactile measurements through dedicated branches and di-rectly integrate the resulting tokens with visual–language representations from pretrained backbones [2, 14, 18]. For example, Tactile-VLA [6] introduces tactile tokens into the VLM input sequences, while ForceVLA [24] fuses force tokens with VLM outputs through a force-aware Mixture-of-Experts module. Experimental results of these methods demonstrate the benefits of incorporating physical feedback into action generation. However, a challenge lies in the het-erogeneous information encoded by these modalities. Force measurements capture interaction forces and torques, while tactile signals characterize local contact state. In contrast, visual features encode object appearance and spatial relation-ships, whereas language features convey task instructions and goals. Although these modalities provide complemen-tary information for manipulation, the force–tactile features capture physical interactions, whereas the visual–language features encode semantic information. Existing direct inte-gration frameworks [6, 11, 24, 25] overlook the fundamental differences between these heterogeneous representations, in-troducing conflicts during training. As a result, force–tactile feedback cannot be effectively leveraged to conduct contact-rich manipulation. To address this challenge, we propose A2I-VLA, a two-stage framework that first aligns semantic and physical rep-resentations and then integrates the learned physical features into VLA models for action generation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the force-tactile representation learning stage , separate semantic and physical branches encode visual–language ob-servations and current wrench and tactile measurements, re-spectively. A cross-modal alignment loss promotes semantic-physical correspondence between the two streams. The se-mantic and physical features are then integrated through a hierarchical multimodal fusion network, producing multi-level physical features enriched with visual–language con-text. These features are subsequently concatenated to form a unified representation for future wrench and action predic-tion. The former encourages the model to anticipate physical responses, while the latter links force–tactile observations to task-relevant manipulation behaviors. Together, the align-ment and prediction losses guide the learning of physical representations. In the force-tactile feature integration stage , A2I-VLA transfers the learned feature extractor and retains the hierar-chical tactile and wrench features. A gated cross-attention bridge module utilizes the action expert’s output features as queries to retrieve the two types of physical context through separate cross-attention branches. Learned weighting factors modulate the retrieved features before adding them to action features through residual connections. This design enables A2I-VLA to adjust the contribution of physical context dur-ing action generation. During training, a two-phase strategy is adopted to progressively optimize the overall architecture. Specifically, A2I-VLA first trains only the bridge module while keeping all other components frozen. Subsequently, all components are jointly fine-tuned. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose A2I-VLA, a two-stage VLA model for contact-rich manipulation that incorporates force and tactile feed-back to guide action generation. • We develop a two-stage framework for force–tactile fea-ture alignment and integration into VLA models. In the first stage, A2I-VLA integrates semantic–physical feature alignment with future wrench and action prediction to guide the learning of hierarchical force–tactile features. In the second stage, A2I-VLA integrates physical features learned in the first stage into the VLA through a gated cross-attention bridge module. • We evaluate A2I-VLA on ring-on-shaft insertion, black-board wiping, and ethernet connector insertion tasks. Com-pared with π0.5, A2I-VLA improves the task success rate from 70.00% to 95.00%. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models [1, 3, 7–9, 13, 19– 22, 27] integrate visual perception, language understanding, and action prediction within a unified end-to-end framework. OpenVLA [9] formulates robot control as the autoregressive generation of discretized action tokens and fine-tunes a pre-trained vision–language model [15] using a next-token pre-2diction objective. Unlike OpenVLA, π0 [3] combines a pre-trained vision–language backbone [2] with a flow-matching technique to generate continuous action chunks. This con-tinuous action generation paradigm is widely adopted in subsequent VLA studies. Building upon π0, π0.5 [7] intro-duces hierarchical subtask inference to enable long-horizon manipulation in unseen environments. As reinforcement learning continues to improve performance on dexterous manipulation tasks, π0.6 [1] extends flow-based continuous action generation with RECAP, a reinforcement learning framework that improves policies through real-world deploy-ment. To improve cross-embodiment generalization, X-VLA [27] employs embodiment-specific soft prompts to accom-modate diverse robotic embodiments. LingBot-VLA series [19, 20] integrates vision-language understanding and contin-uous action generation through a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture, with depth-feature distillation to enhance spa-tial perception. ABot-M0 [21] combines unified pretraining on heterogeneous robot data with action manifold learning, predicting action sequences rather than velocity targets. 

2.2. Physical Modalities in VLA Models 

Force and tactile feedback play essential roles in contact-rich robotic manipulation, providing physical interaction in-formation that cannot be reliably inferred from visual obser-vations alone. Recent studies [4–6, 11, 12, 17, 24–26] have explored integrating these modalities into the VLA models. For force-based methods, ForceVLA [24] introduces a force-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module to adaptively fuse six-axis force-torque measurements with visual-language features for contact-aware action generation. ForceVLA2 [11] further incorporates force-based prompts into task rea-soning and employs a MoE module to enable force-position control. ForceDelta-VLA [4] adopts a distillation framework that utilizes a lightweight policy to refine reference actions. PhaForce [17] introduces a slow-fast policy that coordinates visual-force planning and high-frequency residual correc-tions according to predicted contact states and task phases. For tactile-based methods, TLA [5] and VTLA [25] incor-porate sequential tactile observations into the encoder of LM or VLM to predict actions. Tactile-VLA [6] combines tactile feedback with hybrid position-force control to enable language-guided physical interaction. AT-VLA [10] intro-duces adaptive tactile injection and a dual-stream strategy to selectively incorporate tactile feedback while preserving pretrained VLA capabilities. However, most existing methods adopt input-level integra-tion without explicitly addressing the representational gap between force–tactile measurements and visual–language semantics. This gap introduces conflicts between heteroge-neous features, limiting the effective use of physical feed-back in contact-rich manipulation. 

## 3. Method 

3.1. Overall Architecture of A2I-VLA 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, A2I-VLA consists of a force-tactile representation learning stage and a force-tactile fea-ture integration stage . Stage I realizes the semantic-physical alignment and scene-conditioned force-tactile understanding. Stage II integrates the hierarchical physical features learned in Stage I into the VLA model. At time t, A2I-VLA receives two RGB images It =

{Ig , I r }, a language instruction ℓ, robot state pt, a current wrench value wt ∈ R6, and a current tactile value ct ∈{0, 1}. The wrench wt contains three force and three torque components, while ct is a binary scalar taking values of 0 or 1. Given these inputs, the policy predicts an action chunk

F phy t from wt and ct, while a parallel semantic branch em-ploys pretrained VLA components to extract semantic fea-tures F sem t from It, ℓ, and pt. A cross-modal alignment loss 

Lalign promotes correspondence between the two streams before fusion. A hierarchical multimodal fusion network then jointly processes the semantic and physical features, producing multi-level physical features { ¯Zk}Lk=1 , where L

denotes the number of fusion layers. At each layer, an atten-tion selector processes the ¯Zk into a descriptor ¯Hk. Future wrench prediction loss Lw guides the model to anticipate physical responses, while action prediction loss La asso-ciates the learned features with task-relevant behaviors. In Stage II, the learned physical encoder and hierarchical multimodal fusion network form a feature extractor Eϕ. Sep-arate projectors map the tactile and wrench features from all fusion layers into Fc and Fw. A gated cross-attention bridge uses the action expert’s output as queries and these context features as keys and values to produce action features eFact .The action output projection then predicts flow vectors for action generation.

3.3. Force-Tactile Feature Integration Stage 

Stage II integrates the hierarchical force-tactile features learned in Stage I into the VLA model to guide action gen-eration. We combine the physical encoder and hierarchical multimodal fusion network learned in Stage I as a physical feature extractor Eϕ. The attention selectors and prediction heads are discarded. 

Gated Cross-Attention Bridge Module. At the k-th fusion layer, we extract the tactile feature zkc and wrench feature zkw from ¯Zk according to their token positions. These features incorporate semantic context, enabling the network to interpret similar physical measurements in accordance with the visual context. Then, separate linear layers map 

zkc and zkw into compact features mkc and mkw. For L fusion layer, we obtain Fc ∈ RL×C and Fw ∈ RL×C

Subsequently, we employ the VLM and action expert of π0.5 to process input images, language and noisy action chunk. Let Fact denote the final output features of the action expert. We then utilize Fact as queries to independently aggregate tactile and wrench features through two separate cross-attention branches. Subsequently, a learnable gating strategy adaptively modulates the output features, which are integrated into Fact through a residual connection to guide action generation. This process can be formulated as follows: 

Dc = CrossAtt( Fact , F c, F c),Dw = CrossAtt( Fact , F w, F w),

[Gc, G w] = Sigmoid (Linear(Cat( Fact , D c, D w))) ,

eFact = Fact + Gc ⊙ Linear c(Dc) + Gw ⊙ Linear w(Dw)

(6) The updated action features eFact are processed by the flow matching technique to generate action chunk. Notably, the linear layers Linear c and Linear w are initialized to zero so that eFact = Fact at initialization. 

Loss Function of Stage II. Following π0.5, we adopt the same loss function to minimize the mean squared error between the predicted flow vector and the target flow vector.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Architecture Details. The hierarchical multimodal fu-sion network consists of L = 4 Transformer layers, each with a feature dimension of d = 256 and eight atten-tion heads. The semantic–physical alignment loss employs 

K = 4 shared queries, with loss weights set to λalign = 0 .1

and λw = λa = 1 .

Training Strategy. We train the A2I-VLA on eight A100 GPUs using the AdamW optimizer for both stages. In Stage I, we freeze the semantic encoder pretrained by the π0.5 and train 1 × 10 4 iterations with a batch size of 512 and a weight decay of 1 × 10 −4. The learning rate is gradually increased to 1 × 10 −4 over the first 1000 warmup iterations, followed by the cosine decay strategy. During Stage II, A2I-VLA is trained for 5 × 10 4 iterations with a batch size of 64. During the first 1000 iterations, only the gated cross-attention bridge module is trained, with the learning rate increasing from 0

to 5 × 10 −5. The base VLA and feature extractor are then unfrozen for an additional 1000 iteration warmup. Afterward, the learning rate is maintained at 5 × 10 −5.

Data Augmentation. During Stage II, we apply ran-dom brightness, contrast, and saturation jitter to input im-ages. Additionally, we augment the raw force components 

(Fx, F y , F z ) before normalization. For each sample, we ran-domly apply one of three force augmentation strategies. (1) Keeping the original force measurements unchanged with a probability of 0.3; (2) Adding uniform noise within ±0.25 N

for Fx and ±0.4N for Fy and Fz with a probability of 0.5;(3) Adding stronger uniform noise within ±0.5N for Fx and 

±0.8N for Fy and Fz with a probability of 0.2.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduce A2I-VLA, a two-stage VLA framework that integrates force–tactile feedback to enable more accurate contact-rich manipulation. In Stage I, seman-tic–physical alignment, hierarchical multimodal fusion, and 8future wrench-action prediction are employed to learn task-aware force–tactile representations. Building upon the repre-sentations learned in Stage I, Stage II integrates hierarchical tactile and wrench features into the VLA model through a gated cross-attention bridge module, enabling physical context to adaptively guide action generation. We evaluate A2I-VLA on three real-world manipulation tasks, includ-ing ring-on-shaft insertion, blackboard wiping, and ethernet connector insertion. Experimental results demonstrate that A2I-VLA consistently outperforms the baseline across all three tasks.
