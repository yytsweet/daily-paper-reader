# Integration of Vision–Language–Action Control for Autonomous Forklift Unloading with Limited Real-World Data 

Koshi Oishi 1,∗ and Seigo Ito 1

This work has been submitted to the IEEE for possible publication. Copyright may be transferred without notice, after which this version may no longer be accessible 

Abstract — Forklifts play a critical role in warehousing and distribution, but existing automation systems remain limited in handling complex tasks that require sequential decision-making. Recently, vision–language–action (VLA) models have emerged as a promising approach for multi-step decision-making by directly generating control actions from visual observations and language prompts. Although VLA-based con-trol has been extensively researched for manipulators, to the best of our knowledge, its application to forklifts remains unexplored. Therefore, this study demonstrates the integration of VLA control for an autonomous forklift unloading task with limited real-world data. Moreover, training VLA models typically requires large-scale datasets. As there is currently no public dataset for forklift operations, we use a VLA model pretrained primarily on robotic manipulator data and construct a training dataset via simulation, which can be scaled up at low cost. We then adapt the pretrained VLA model to forklift control using 9.38 h of simulation data and 0.8 h of real-world demonstrations, with the latter accounting for approximately 8% of the collected forklift data by duration. The integrated system was validated through 72 real-world trials across four model variants. The results show that the proposed system can accomplish an unloading task even with limited real-world data. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Forklifts are among the most widely used industrial ve-hicles in warehousing and distribution. In the context of rapidly expanding global logistics, forklifts are indispensable material-handling equipment, and their automation has be-come increasingly important. Forklift automation has already been implemented in restricted environments, using system designs broadly similar to those reported by Walter et al. [1] and Teller et al. [2]. However, these approaches rely heavily on accurate state estimation using multiple sensors and hard-coded logic tailored to constrained spaces [3]. In real-world deployments, where forklifts operate under diverse and changing conditions, hard-coding decisions for all possible situations is prohibitively costly to engineer and maintain. This motivates the need for an alternative approach to con-ventional systems that depend heavily on human-engineered design. Recently, with the rapid progress of vision–language mod-els (VLMs), there is a growing interest in vision–language– action (VLA) models that extend VLMs to directly output ac-tions [4], [5], [6], [7]. VLA models address decision-making scenarios that are challenging for conventional approaches,

This capability can reduce reliance on hard-coded logic, which is inflexible and costly to maintain as operating conditions vary. Applying VLA models can potentially automate decision-making in complex forklift operations. However, most VLA research has focused on manipulators, and forklift-specific studies remain limited. In this study, we integrate a VLA model into a forklift unloading system and automate the unloading sequence by directly generating actions from language and visual observa-tions (Fig. 1). Our integrated system can handle task goals in natural language and generate action sequences while jointly predicting the current task phase, eliminating the need to manually enter numeric targets or implementing extensive hard-coded logic. Moreover, training VLA models requires large-scale datasets. However, to the best of our knowledge, no public dataset currently exists for forklift operations. Therefore, we employed a VLA model pretrained primarily on robotic manipulator data and constructed a simulation dataset so that the embodiment gap between manipulators and forklifts could be bridged without large-scale real-world forklift data. We performed real-world evaluation of our approach on a 1/14-scale forklift platform by conducting 72 trials, including baseline comparisons. Although full-scale arXiv:2611.33880v1 [cs.RO] 25 Nov 2026 forklifts are the intended target, the 1/14-scale platform en-ables safer, real-world iterations during early-stage learning-based development. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-world integration study of VLA-based end-to-end action generation for a forklift unloading task. The main contributions are as follows:  

> •

We integrate a VLA model into a forklift unloading system, enabling phase inference and action generation from onboard sensor observations while supporting low-latency execution.  

> •

We present a cross-embodiment adaptation recipe that combines simulation-based mid-training with post-training on 0.8 h of real-world forklift demonstrations.  

> •

We evaluate the proposed integrated system through 72 real-world trials across four model variants and demonstrate that it can accomplish the unloading task even with limited real-world data. R ELATED WORK 

Recent forklift studies have combined advanced sensing technologies with deep learning to improve the robustness of environmental perception for autonomous forklift oper-ation [12], [13], [14]. Nguyen et al. [15] used Florence-2 [16], a VLM, for pallet detection and target selection of forklifts. While these studies improve perception robust-ness and reduce part of reliance on hard-coded logic, the transition from perception to task execution still requires manual system design. Reinforcement-learning (RL)-based action generation has also been studied for forklift tasks [3], [17]. RL learns actions in a human-like manner, allowing the system to rely only on onboard sensors such as cameras and velocity. However, RL-based studies primarily focused on short-horizon skills, such as pallet approach. Applying RL to the entire unloading task is difficult owing to the increased complexity of reward design for long-horizon behaviors with multiple conditional branches. Considering these challenges, this study introduces recent VLA techniques to forklift control, thereby aiming to reduce hand-coded logic and reward design while retaining a minimal onboard sensing setup comparable to prior RL-based studies. VLA models are commonly developed by first acquiring visual understanding and decision-making capabilities from large-scale non-robot web data, then transferring these ca-pabilities to robot control using large-scale robot datasets, and finally fine-tuning the model on data from the target robot [7]. The first two stages require large amounts of data, and collecting real-world robot data is costly and time-consuming. Existing robot datasets are biased toward robot arms [5], [9] and autonomous driving [18]; therefore, directly applying existing VLA models to industrial machinery, such as forklifts, is challenging. Recent advances in simulation have enabled automatic generation of high-quality robot data, and sim-to-real transfer has been demonstrated in some robotic tasks [19], [17]. Although simulation data can be less diverse than real-world data and cannot cover all real-world conditions, it can provide useful supervision for transferring action generation to a new embodiment. Based on this observation, we use digital-twin-generated data to adapt a VLA model pretrained on web and robot-arm data to forklift action generation, and then fine-tune it with a small amount of real-world data. VLA models have also been developed for robot navigation [20]. Because forklift unloading requires accurate pallet alignment, pickup, and placement in addition to navigation, we use π0.5 [21], which is pretrained on robotic manipulation data. Additionally, simulation-based data generation automatically provides most of the subtask labels. We demonstrate that this training recipe enables a VLA policy to perform a complex forklift unloading task even when large-scale forklift operation data are unavailable. Moreover, the simulation environment and the 1/14-scale forklift platform are based on Oishi et al. [17]. Recent VLA studies have proposed techniques for long-horizon control and real-time deployment. For tasks con-sisting of multiple subtasks, methods that jointly learn task progress or semantic states with action generation have been explored [21], [11]. We integrate a simplified version of the semantic prediction approach of Black et al. [21] by jointly training subtask prediction with action generation. In addition, because VLA inference is often slower than the control cycle of industrial machinery, asynchronous action chunking has been proposed to perform inference while previously predicted actions are being executed [10], [22]. Our system implements a simplified version of this idea for real-time execution on a physical forklift platform. III. S YSTEM OVERVIEW 

The proposed integration combines an online VLA-based forklift control system with an offline adaptation pipeline, as shown in Fig. 2. As a target task, we address pallet unloading from a wing truck using a 1/14-scale forklift platform for safe and repeatable evaluation. 

A. Task setting 

The unloading task consists of subtasks, including picking up the pallet, backing out, searching the right side for the target color while backing out, and placing the pallet at the specified color location. Fig. 3 illustrates the subtasks that compose the primary task. The forklift starts approximately 0.15 m in front of the pallet. The operator only provides the prompt; all other operations are executed autonomously. 

B. Hardware platform 

We used the 1/14-scale forklift platform introduced by Oishi et al. [17] (Fig. 4). Most hardware and sensing speci-fications follow those reported by Oishi et al.; therefore, we describe only the components and any deviation relevant to this study. This platform is a counterbalance-type forklift, which is the most common forklift configuration, with rear-wheel steering and front-wheel drive. The visual data were captured using two pinhole cameras mounted on the left Fig. 2: Integrated system. In a full-scale forklift, these speeds are typically available directly from the vehicle. Fork height was measured using a rotary encoder mounted near the top of the fork assembly, which estimated the lift height based on the amount of chain winding. For actions, the throttle command was specified in the range 0–100%, the steering command was defined as the servo motor angle, and the fork-height command specified the target height. The forklift was equipped with a Raspberry Pi 4, which communicates with the inference PC via robot operating system (ROS) [23] to exchange action commands and sensor observations. 

C. Integrated architecture 

Fig. 2 shows the overall architecture of the proposed system. The offline pipeline first trains a VLA policy on web-scale vision–language data and large-scale robot manip-ulation data, and then adapts it to forklift control. Simulator-generated forklift data serves as a mid-training stage that bridges the embodiment gap between robot manipulators and the forklift, while a short post-training stage on a small amount of real-world data specializes the policy to the target platform and its operating environment. The use of robot manipulation data is also motivated by its structural similarity to forklift operation. Our pallet unloading task is essentially a pick-and-place problem: the forklift lifts a pallet and places it at a designated location. For observation data acquisition, we used cameras mounted on the left and right sides of the forklift, similar to the wrist-mounted cameras on robot arms. In online operation, the adapted policy receives camera images, vehicle-state observations, and a language prompt, and outputs an action chunk over a future horizon. Let tinf 

denote the maximum time required for one VLA inference. Because VLA inference is computationally expensive, the system runs inference every tinf and executes forklift actions asynchronously. The policy is trained to predict actions up to 

2tinf after the observation time, so that the controller can skip the portion corresponding to inference latency and execute the future part of the predicted action chunk. This design reduces idle waiting time and maintains temporal consistency between observation, inference, and vehicle motion. IV. M ODEL AND TRAINING STRATEGY 

We construct the forklift policy based on the adaptation pipeline described in Section III-C. Recent progress in robot manipulation has led to several publicly available VLA models trained on large-scale web and manipulation data. Fig. 5: VLA model reconfigured for forklift operation. In this work, we use π0.5 [21] as the base model, which allows us to bypass the initial construction of a VLA policy and focus on adapting it to forklift control. 

A. Model architecture 

π0.5 is a VLA built on a PaliGemma-style VLM [24] that combines SigLIP [25] and Gemma [26], where images are encoded by a vision transformer backbone [27] and actions are generated via flow matching [28]. Ilt and Irt are the left and right camera images, respectively, 

ℓt is the prompt, and qt is a vector of the vehicle’s speed, yaw rate, and fork height. These observations are compatible with onboard sensing on a full-scale forklift. The camera images were captured at 352 × 288 RGB and resized to 

224 × 224 before being fed into the VLA model. Given the observation ot as an input, the VLM branch classifies the current subtask, while the action-expert branch generates an action sequence at:t+H := ( at, . . . , at+H−1) over a horizon 

H. at ∈ R3 includes commands throttle, steering angle, and fork height, which matches the control interface used by human operators. The state vector qt is quantized into 256 bins and provided to the model as text tokens. Following the BERT design, we added a <CLS> token and used its feature representation for subtask classification via a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) [29]. We defined seven subtasks 

ct ∈ { 1, . . . , 7}, as shown in Fig. 3. Using the same notation as π0.5, our model can be described as follows:

The base-camera input is masked because our platform lacks a head camera.

A. Simulation data 

We built the simulation environment in Isaac Sim using CAD models [19]. For the background, we used a 3D-scanned model of the actual environment. We matched the relative configuration of the wing truck, pallet, drop-off location, and the forklift’s initial pose to the real task setup. We then randomly placed this task set within the background model and collected ot and at. To increase data diversity, we randomized the lighting intensity, the colors of the pallet and the load, the load arrangement, and the camera pose [31]. Fig. 6 shows an example of the simulation environment and camera images. During the simulation, we controlled the forklift using a hard-coded subtask manager and non-learning-based meth-ods that leveraged privileged information available in the simulation environment (e.g., absolute pose). We first ran-domly selected the target color for the drop-off location and determined the corresponding subtask sequence. The current state of the subtask manager provided the ground-truth subtask label. Transitions were triggered by predefined criteria, such as fork height and reversing distance, and the subtask sequence progressed without backward transi-tions. Each subtask primarily involved movement toward a designated target pose. To perform this movement, we used Clothoid-based path planning and Pure Pursuit for trajectory tracking [32]. For simple behaviors such as straight “backout” and backing up with left steering during “search right,” we used a fixed steering angle. B. Real data 

We collected real-world data from the two environments (A and B) shown in Fig. 7. Operators teleoperated the forklift using a joystick while directly observing the scene, whereas the learned policy used onboard camera images. Because the task does not require visual information beyond the onboard cameras’ field of view, we adopted this teleoper-ation method for ease of operation. Additionally, operators manually annotated the current subtask using button inputs during teleoperation. The recorded button-input times defined the boundaries between successive labels. The control period was 20 ms, which matched the simulation. All the data were logged at this rate. In total, we collected 0.8 h of real-world data. VI. R EAL -WORLD EXPERIMENT

This study focuses on the integration of a VLA policy into a forklift unloading system under a controlled real-world setting. Although the experiments demonstrate that the proposed system can complete the target unloading task with limited real-world data, they do not establish open-ended robustness to arbitrary environments, pallet poses, or target layouts. Extending the evaluation to wider variations in target placement, pallet position, lighting conditions, and language instructions remains an important direction for future work. Additionally, the high success rate highlights the potential of adapting pretrained VLA models to forklift unloading, although the specific contribution of pretraining remains to be clarified. Although the tasks performed included a complete unload-ing sequence, several recovery actions required in practical logistics operations were not performed. For example, during truck unloading, when a pallet is deep inside the truck, operators often lift the pallet while maintaining clearance from the truck, temporarily place it near the truck’s edge, and then reinsert the forks more deeply to stabilize the load before extraction. In addition, when pallets or loads interfere, fine manipulation, such as nudging the pallets with the forks to remove the interference, is often performed. Handling such complex, contact-rich tasks that require high precision remains an important direction for future work. For such precise operations, offline reinforcement learning is reported to be effective [33]. Finally, the experiments were conducted on a 1/14-scale forklift platform to enable safe and repeatable evaluation of the integrated system. Deployment on full-scale forklifts will require further validation under full vehicle dynamics, stronger sensing noise, safety constraints, and collecting data. Addressing these issues is beyond the scope of this study, however, the proposed integration provides a basis for investigating these challenges in future full-scale systems. VIII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This study demonstrated the integration of VLA control into a forklift unloading system. Motivated by the challenges in automating sequential forklift operations with hard-coded logic and the lack of large-scale forklift operation data for pretraining, we adapted a pretrained VLA model to forklift control using simulation-generated and limited real-world forklift data. The proposed integrated system connects VLA inference, phase prediction, asynchronous action-chunk execution, and low-level vehicle control. Through 72 real-world trials, we validated that simulation-generated data sup-port model adaptation, while subtask prediction helps align generated actions with task instructions. The study findings demonstrate the feasibility of constructing a VLA-based forklift unloading system using simulation data, limited real-world demonstrations, and system-level integration.
