Abstract
Latent reasoning lets Vision-Language-Action (VLA) mod-
els deliberate at a fraction of the cost of explicit chain-
of-thought, increasingly through visual foresight, which we
show to be indispensable for predicting actions. Yet this
paradigm is inherited from Vision-Language Models, where
it serves understanding rather than action: each latent is
aligned with an isolated future frame, often enriched with
further modalities of that frame. We find that this binds the
policy to the appearance of its training scenes, so that it
degrades sharply once the scene changes. We argue that
visual latents for action should capture not what a future
frame looks like, but how the scene evolves toward the goal.
We therefore propose Motion-Oriented Latent Reasoning,
which redirects visual latents from the whole frame to the
interaction that the action must bring about. Semantic la-
tents, following the stages of embodied chain-of-thought,
first establish what to accomplish and how. Conditioned
on them, motion-oriented latents anticipate each future step
from coarse to fine: from the global scene, to the interaction
region spanned by the manipulated object and its destina-
tion, to the robot state. Relative to the state-of-the-art latent
VLA LaST0, our method raises the mean success rate on
SimplerEnv-WidowX from 37.5% to 55.2% in distribution
and from 22.4% to 30.2% under out-of-distribution shifts,
and on an AgileX Piper dual-arm platform from 60.8% to
90.8% in training scenes and from 6.7% to 37.5% under
environmental changes.

Building

els [4, 18, 56] inherit web-scale visual-semantic priors
and translate visual observations and language instructions
directly into robot actions.
Yet such reactive policies
lack explicit deliberation over subgoals and action conse-
quences, which is indispensable for complex, long-horizon

verbalizing sub-task plans [12, 24, 37, 48] or anticipating
future observations [49, 50, 52] before acting, and thereby
attain stronger generalization.
Despite these gains, explicit reasoning suffers from two

These limitations have spurred a shift
toward Latent Reasoning [55], which unfolds reasoning
in continuous hidden states [46] and endows compact la-
tent tokens with task-relevant content, from verbalizable
textual rationales in Heima [35] to visual evidence such
as trajectory maps in Mirage [47], regions of interest in
LVR [20], 3D geometric priors in 3DThinker [8], and per-

brought this paradigm into VLAs [2, 12, 13, 28–30, 38, 44]:
Fast-ThinkAct [13] distills lengthy textual reasoning into
compact verbalizable latents, whereas LaST0 [28], LaRA-
VLA [2], and, in autonomous driving, LaST-VLA [29] fur-
ther ground their latents in future visual dynamics, 3D struc-
ture, and robot proprioception. Such grounding proves es-
sential for embodied control: our oracle analysis reveals
that, added to the current observation, ground-truth future
visual evidence attains an action predictability1 of 0.678,
against 0.616 for textual latents (Figure 2). Visual latents
thus constitute an indispensable component of latent rea-
soning in VLAs.
In VLMs, latent visual reasoning serves to comprehend
the image at hand: its latents encode the current input across
diverse modalities, from RGB features [20, 47] and 3D
geometry [8] to segmentation and depth maps [33], and
the reasoning culminates in a single answer rather than
an action sequence.
Latent VLAs, however, inherit this
understanding-oriented paradigm largely intact: each latent
is aligned with the features of an isolated future frame, and

richer foresight is pursued by stacking further modalities of
that same frame [28]. This raises a pivotal question: does a
paradigm built to understand a frame suit a policy that must
decide how to act? We find that it does not. Reproducing
what a future frame looks like rewards the backbone for
encoding the appearance of the scene, and each additional
modality of the same frame supplies further supervision on
appearance rather than on how to move. For example, when
only the workspace background changes while the task re-
mains the same, the backbone of LaST0 [28] recognizes the
task in only 2% of the observations (Figure 1), mistaking
a change of scene for a change of task. Bound to the ap-
pearance of its training scenes, the latent VLA overfits the
training distribution: under the same kind of change on a
real robot, it succeeds in only 6.7% of trials, against 37.5%
for our method (Figure 1).
In this paper, we argue that visual latents for action
should capture not what a future frame looks like, but how
the scene evolves toward the goal. Textual reasoning un-
folds step by step, with each step conditioned on its prede-
cessors and the goal. Visual latents should likewise follow
a logic of their own: within each future step, they should
progress from the global scene to the interaction region and
the robot state, i.e., from coarse to fine; across steps, each
should build on the one before, so that successive steps
chain into a motion path.
Such a derivation supervises
what changes, where, and in which order, which transfers
across scenes, rather than the appearance that distinguishes
one training scene from another. Based on this insight, we
propose Motion-Oriented Latent Reasoning, which orga-
nizes latent reasoning as a logically structured derivation.
Semantic latents, aligned with the semantic stages of em-
bodied chain-of-thought [48] and trained under random tex-
tual prefix substitution, first establish what to accomplish
and how. Conditioned on them, motion-oriented latents an-
ticipate each future step from coarse to fine, zooming from
the global scene into the interaction region spanned by the
manipulated object and its destination before specifying the
robot state. Without altering the inference procedure of la-
tent reasoning, the policy thereby learns how to move rather
than what the training scenes look like, and still recognizes
the task in unseen scenes (Section 4.2).
Our contributions are threefold: (1) We reveal that la-
tent VLAs inherit an understanding-oriented paradigm of
latent visual reasoning from VLMs, and show that align-
ing latents with isolated future frames and their modalities
binds the policy to the appearance of its training scenes and
impairs its generalization. (2) We propose Motion-Oriented
Latent Reasoning, which organizes visual latents as a logi-
cally structured derivation, from coarse to fine within each
step and chained across steps into a motion path, scaf-
folded by semantic latents. (3) Extensive experiments on
SimplerEnv-WidowX, LIBERO, LIBERO-PRO, and an Ag-

matches or improves in-distribution performance while sub-
stantially improving out-of-distribution generalization.
2. Related Work
Reasoning in VLA models.
Vision-Language-Action
models adapt pretrained VLMs to robot control by map-
ping observations and instructions directly to actions [4, 18,
21, 31, 32, 34, 56]. To equip such reactive policies with
deliberation, a line of work interposes explicit reasoning
before acting: textual approaches verbalize subtasks, mo-
tions, and grounded object locations as embodied chain-
of-thought [6, 24, 37, 48], while visual approaches syn-
thesize a subgoal image [49, 52] or forecast dynamic re-
gions, depth, and semantics [50]. Others condition the pol-
icy on motion through predicted point tracks [3, 43], object
flow [45], or tracked visual traces [53], improving gener-
alization across objects, scenes, and embodiments and cor-
roborating that motion transfers where appearance does not.
In both lines, however, the intermediate representation is de-
coded or tracked at every control step, incurring substantial
latency at inference.
Latent reasoning. Latent reasoning relocates deliberation
into continuous hidden states [55]. In language models, the
chain of thought is replaced by continuous thoughts [10],
compressed into fewer latent steps [39], interleaved with
discrete text tokens [36], or condensed into verbalizable
hidden tokens [35]; VLMs extend it to visual evidence,
aligning latent tokens with image features [47], regions of
interest [20], 3D geometry [8], or segmentation and depth
cues [33], although recent analyses question how effectively
such latents are exploited [23, 51].
These latents serve
to comprehend the image at hand rather than to act.
In
VLAs, RD-VLA [40] scales test-time computation through
latent iteration, Fast-ThinkAct [13] compresses reasoning
into verbalizable latents, and ThinkAct [12] distills rein-
forced reasoning into a visual plan latent. Closer to our set-
ting, implicit visual chain-of-thought [30], LaST0 [28], and
LaRA-VLA [2] ground latents in future visual features, 3D
structure, and robot proprioception, as do driving models
that forecast future scenes in latent space [15, 29, 38, 44].
These methods inherit the understanding-oriented design
of VLMs, supervising latents with snapshots of future
frames or further modalities thereof, without organizing
them around the interaction that the action must bring about.

3.1. Semantic Latents as a Logical Scaffold
We derive the scaffold from embodied chain-of-thought an-
notations [48], which decompose the reasoning at each step
into M semantic stages: Subtask, Why, Movement, Move-
ment reasoning, and Plan. The stages progress from the
sub-goal and its rationale to the movement that realizes it
and the plan that follows, so that each stage presupposes the
ones before; each is represented by a semantic latent cm.
Textual prefix.
Aligning each latent with its own stage
alone would let the latents memorize their targets indepen-
dently, without deriving one stage from another. Inspired
by Token Assorted [36], we therefore condition each latent
on the annotations of the stages before it. For each training
sample, we draw κ ∼U{0, . . . , M} and replace the in-
puts at the first κ semantic latent positions with ¯c1, . . . , ¯cκ,
where ¯cm is the averaged token embedding of the annota-
tion of the m-th stage (e.g., turn on the stove for Subtask
in Figure 3). Since each latent is read out at the position
preceding it, every cm in this prefix (m ≤κ) is predicted
from the averaged annotation embeddings ¯c1, . . . , ¯cm−1 of
the preceding stages and is trained to match ¯cm,

At inference, no annotations are provided and all semantic
latents are generated by the backbone itself.

3.3. Training and Inference
Two-pass training. Following LaST0 [28], training pro-
ceeds in two forward passes over the same sequence. In the
first pass, the inputs at the motion-oriented latent positions
are replaced by their targets, and those at the first κ seman-
tic latent positions by ¯c1, . . . , ¯cκ. The remaining M−κ se-
mantic latent positions keep their placeholder inputs. Each
latent is read out from the hidden state at the preceding po-
sition, and Lsem (Equation 3), Ldec, and Lmot (Equation 5)
are computed on these predictions. In the second pass, all
K latent positions are filled with the latents predicted in the
first pass, and the action expert is optimized with Lact con-
ditioned on them. The action expert is thus trained on the
latents that the backbone itself produces, as at inference,
and the action loss is back-propagated through these latents
into the first pass. The overall objective is
L = Lact + Lmot + λsem Lsem + λdec Ldec.

4.2. Ablation Study and Analysis
What does each latent group contribute? Table 3 ab-
lates the two latent groups against a baseline whose la-
tents are removed at inference, in distribution on LIBERO
and out of distribution on the Spatial suite of LIBERO-
PRO. The twelve motion-oriented latents alone raise the
in-distribution mean from 77.3% to 91.4%; the gain con-
centrates on Goal, which rises from 41.2% to 91.2%. Out
of distribution, they improve all five perturbations, by 6.8 to
10.4 points, raising the mean from 40.8% to 50.2%. Adding
the five semantic latents improves every suite in distribu-
tion, most notably Spatial and Long (by 9.2 and 8.4 points),
lifting the mean to 97.2%. Out of distribution, it further
raises manipulated objects from 78.4% to 89.8% and modi-
fied tasks from 29.0% to 36.4%, lifting the mean to 53.3%.
The two groups are thus complementary: motion-oriented
latents furnish most of the gain both in and out of distribu-
tion, whereas the semantic scaffold extends it to the long-
horizon suite and to changes of the manipulated object and
the task.
Does the backbone recognize the task under a new
scene? We probe whether the training target determines
what the backbone encodes.
On the ten LIBERO-Long
tasks, each rendered from 20 initial states in the orig-
inal scene and under the environmental perturbation of
LIBERO-PRO [54], we take the hidden states of the third-
person image tokens at the middle layer of the backbone.
As these tokens precede the latent segment under causal
attention, their states are independent of the generated la-
tents and reflect only what training has instilled. Each per-
turbed observation is assigned to the task whose original-
scene centroid is nearest. On the five tasks whose scene
visibly changes, the backbone of LaST0 [28] recognizes the
task in only 2% of the observations, mistaking a change of
scene for a change of task, whereas ours recognizes it in
66% (Figure 1). Over all ten tasks, the rates are 30% and
74%, and Figure 4 shows that LaST0 [28] assigns most per-
turbed observations to the living-room tasks T5–T7, i.e., it
identifies the scene rather than the task. Predicting isolated
future frames thus binds the backbone to the appearance of
the training scenes, whereas the motion-oriented derivation
steers it toward what transfers across them.
What do the latents represent? We further inspect the pre-
dicted latents in the space where they are supervised. On
80 LIBERO-Long training states with annotated interaction
regions, we compute the cosine similarity between each la-
tent and the patch features of the future frame it anticipates.
As shown in Figure 5, the snapshot latent of LaST0 [28]
resembles patches scattered over the scene, whereas our re-
gion latent concentrates on the interaction region. Over all
320 latent–frame pairs, 38.5% of our top-10% patches fall
inside the region, against 15.7% for LaST0 [28] and 19.4%
by chance.

Data collection.
We deploy our method on an AgileX
Piper dual-arm platform equipped with three external RGB
cameras. We record 14-dimensional joint-space states and
control targets covering both arms and grippers. The pol-
icy uses the right arm only, predicting 7-dimensional end-
effector actions comprising translation, rotation, and grip-

and robot control are provided in Appendix B. We design

Training and evaluation details. We train task-specific
policies using the same architecture and two-pass training
strategy as in simulation, with real-world-specific hyperpa-
rameters detailed in Appendix B, and compare them with
LaST0 [28] and π0.5 [32]. Beyond the training scene (ID),

Object (Obj.), which replaces the manipulated object or
container, e.g., a silver bell with a red one or a handle-
less cup with a handled mug; Environment (Env.), which
overlays colored background paper on the workspace; and
Language (Lang.), which paraphrases the instruction, e.g.,
“activate the bell by pressing it”. Specifically, object per-
turbations in the red-cup task replace the target cup while
retaining the blue distractor cup and white placement area;
in the pouring and block-placement tasks, they replace the
receiving cup with a handled mug. Each task–condition pair
is evaluated over 30 trials.
Quantitative and qualitative analysis. As shown in Ta-
ble 4, our method achieves a mean success rate of 90.8%
in the training scenes, surpassing LaST0 [28] and π0.5 by
30.0 and 33.3 points, respectively. The advantage persists
under perturbations: relative to LaST0 [28], our method
improves the mean success rate from 52.5% to 81.7% un-
der object changes, from 6.7% to 37.5% under environmen-
tal changes, and from 52.5% to 89.2% under paraphrased
instructions. Selected qualitative examples illustrate these
differences. When the original red cup is replaced with a
handled mug, our method completes the transfer, whereas
LaST0 [28] fails to achieve a stable lift and π0.5 misses the
grasp. The failure modes of the baselines are telling: given
a paraphrased instruction, LaST0 [28] grasps the blue dis-
tractor cup instead of the red one, and under environmental
changes, both baselines topple the cup or fail to complete
the transfer. These failures accord with the analysis in Sec-
tion 4.2: once the scene or instruction departs from training,
a policy bound to the appearance of its training scenes falls
back on memorized patterns, whereas our motion-oriented
latents ground the action in the intended target (Figure 6).
5. Conclusion
We have revisited the visual latents of latent VLAs, which
inherit from Vision-Language Models the role of pictur-
ing an isolated future frame. Such snapshot targets bind
the policy to the appearance of its training scenes.
We
have therefore proposed Motion-Oriented Latent Reason-
ing, which redirects the visual latents from the whole frame
to the interaction that the action must bring about. Scaf-
folded by semantic latents that establish what to accom-
plish and how, these latents anticipate each future step from
the global scene through the interaction region to the robot
state. Our method improves on its latent counterpart both in
distribution and under perturbations, in simulation and on a
real robot. We hope these findings encourage latent reason-
ing for robotics to be designed around what the action must
accomplish, not borrowed from image understanding.
8


Limitations
Our method relies on offline annotations, namely the stage
descriptions of embodied chain-of-thought and the interac-
tion regions obtained with open-vocabulary detection and
segmentation, so that annotation errors carry over to the la-
tent targets. Its region latent localizes the interaction of each
future step with a single two-dimensional box, which can-
not separate several interactions that occur at once, as in bi-
manual manipulation, and carries no depth, on which dex-
terous manipulation relies. Moreover, although the global
and region targets are built from video frames without ac-
tion labels, we have so far trained on robot demonstrations;
learning them from large-scale human video, far more abun-
dant than robot data, is left to future work.

A. Action Predictability
This section details the oracle analysis in Section 1 and Fig-
ure 2, which measures how much action-relevant informa-
tion a set of features carries. On timesteps t sampled from
LIBERO-90 demonstrations, we compare four feature sets:
the hidden states of the latent tokens of a Coconut-style [10]
latent ECoT [6, 48] policy built on MiniVLA-1B (textual
latents); the visual features of the current observation (ini-
tial state); the ground-truth visual features of the frames at
t+8, t+16, t+24, and t+32 (visual foresight); and those
of the same number of frames drawn from other timesteps
of the same task (random frames). Each set is standard-
ized, reduced to 64 dimensions by PCA, and fed to a ridge
regression that predicts the ground-truth actions over the
next 8 steps, with the sets concatenated when combined.
Action predictability is the R2 on the held-out 30% of the
episodes, averaged over action dimensions. The initial state
alone attains 0.523; adding textual latents raises it to 0.616,
whereas adding visual foresight raises it to 0.678. Replac-
ing the foresight with random frames yields only 0.610, so
the gain stems from the correspondence to the actual future
rather than from the additional appearance.
B. Real-World Experimental Setup
This section describes the data collection, annotation, and
deployment procedures for the four real-world tasks in the
main paper. Figure 7 presents the real-world experimental
setup.
Platform and data collection.
We use an AgileX Piper
dual-arm platform with three external RGB cameras and
collect demonstrations through leader–follower teleopera-
tion. Each arm has six joints and one gripper channel. At
each time step, we record the three RGB views, joint and
gripper feedback, control targets, and the task instruction.
The recordings use a sampling rate of 30 Hz and an image
resolution of 640 × 480. Visual observations and numer-
ical records are associated through episode indices, frame
indices, and timestamps, and stored in the LeRobot for-
mat. We collect approximately 60–90 teleoperated demon-
strations for each of the four tasks: bell pressing, red-cup
pickup, water pouring, and block placement.

Temporal supervision.
Each sample contains a 16-step
action chunk starting at the current frame. Future visual and
proprioceptive targets are taken at offsets {8, 16, 24, 32},
corresponding to approximately {0.27, 0.53, 0.80, 1.07}
seconds. All three RGB views are used as current visual
inputs to the policy, and future visual targets are taken from
the main camera.
Targets extending beyond an episode
are padded with its final frame or action. When no valid
interaction-region box is available, including for future
frames beyond the episode boundary, the regional target
falls back to the global visual target. No supervision crosses
episode boundaries.
Reasoning and spatial annotations.
We organize offline
reasoning supervision into five fields: Subtask, Why, Move-
ment, Movement reasoning, and Plan. These describe the
current operation, its purpose, the intended motion, the mo-
tion rationale, and the remaining steps. Descriptions are
conditioned on the task, the manipulation phase, and the
recorded action, with visual observations and robot motion
providing the context. Movement descriptions refer to the
current control target, and gripper descriptions distinguish
closing, holding, and releasing.
For spatial supervision,
we associate task-relevant objects with their interaction or
placement regions in the main view. When both object and
destination boxes are available, their enclosing rectangle de-
fines the region of interest. Box coordinates are normalized
by image width and height and matched to the correspond-
ing future frame. When no valid interaction-region box is

Training.
We train a separate policy for each real-world
task.
We initialize the vision–language backbone with
Janus-Pro-1B weights and the action expert with pretrained
weights from LaST0 [28]. The visual encoders and visual
projection module remain frozen, while the language and
action components are fine-tuned. Each policy uses 17 la-
tent tokens: five reasoning tokens followed by four groups
of global visual, ROI visual, and proprioceptive tokens, cor-
responding to the future offsets defined above. The training
objective combines a masked flow-matching action loss, co-
sine alignment of future visual and proprioceptive latents,
reasoning-embedding alignment, and autoregressive text re-
construction.
The latent alignment objectives are super-
vised in the first pass using teacher-forced inputs, while the
action loss in the second pass is conditioned on the latents
predicted in the first pass, rather than on ground-truth latent
targets.
Demonstrations are split by episode into approximately
90% training and 10% validation data using seed 42. Joint
angles and the six end-effector displacement components
are normalized to [−1, 1] using the 1st and 99th percentiles
computed exclusively from the training episodes. We op-
timize with AdamW on eight GPUs using bfloat16 preci-
sion, a peak learning rate of 5 × 10−5 for both the language
and action components, linear warmup over the first 5% of
training steps, and cosine decay to zero. Weight decay is
disabled, and the gradient norm is clipped to 1.0.
Closed-loop deployment.
At inference time, the policy
receives the task instruction, the three current RGB views,
and the right-arm state, using the same view ordering, units,
and normalization statistics as during training. The flow-
based action expert generates a 16-step chunk with 10 in-
tegration steps. We execute the first 8 actions before ac-
quiring fresh observations and predicting the next chunk.
Execution uses a nominal 30 Hz control loop; this is the
command rate rather than the rate of full policy inference.
Before each command, the predicted action is denormalized
and applied to the latest measured end-effector pose. Joint-
limit-constrained numerical inverse kinematics, initialized
from the current joint feedback, converts the target pose into
joint commands. Per-command joint changes and cumula-
tive displacement within a chunk are bounded. Infeasible
inverse-kinematics solutions trigger a hold, with repeated
failures stopping execution. The left arm is not commanded.
The right gripper follows the predicted opening for grasping
tasks and remains fixed for the bell task.
Evaluation protocol.
Each model is evaluated on the four
real-world tasks in the training scene and under object, en-
vironment, and language perturbations. For each model, 30
trials are conducted for every task–condition pair, and suc-
cess rates are computed separately for each pair.

C. Interaction-Region Annotation
The region latent (Section 3.2) requires, for every training
frame, the box Bj enclosing the manipulated object and its
destination. We construct these boxes offline as follows;
they serve only to build the training targets and are never
used at inference.
Object tracks. For the manipulated object, per-frame box
tracks are obtained by prompting SAM 3 [5] with Ground-
ing DINO [27] detections and are verified step by step.
Destinations. Destinations remain static within an episode,
e.g., the plate in LIBERO-Spatial and the basket in
LIBERO-Object.
We therefore detect each destination
once, on the first frame of the episode, with Grounding
DINO (Swin-T) prompted by the name of the destination,
using box and text thresholds of 0.25 and 0.20 and non-
maximum suppression at an IoU of 0.5, and reuse the box
for all frames. On BridgeData V2, the destination phrase is
parsed from the instruction; when it names a location rather
than an object, e.g., the lower right corner, the correspond-
ing region of the workspace serves as the destination.
Gripper. For operations without grasping, such as turning
on the stove or opening a drawer, the gripper takes the place
of the object. We detect it with Grounding DINO, prompted
by white gripper on robot arm with a box threshold of 0.12,
and track it with SAM 3 prompted by the detected box. A
box prompt is preferred over a point prompt, which tends to
expand the mask to the entire arm.
Merging and quality control. The interaction region of
each frame is the tightest box enclosing its component
boxes, expressed on the 224×224 input image. We visu-
alize the boxes of every episode and correct failures manu-
ally or by re-detection with revised prompts, e.g., missed or
duplicated destinations and unparsable destination phrases.
Frames without a valid box, including future frames beyond
the end of an episode, fall back to the global target ¯gj.
