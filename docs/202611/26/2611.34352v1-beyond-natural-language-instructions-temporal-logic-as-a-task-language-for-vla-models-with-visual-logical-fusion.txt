# Abstract 

Robotic tasks combine object relations, logical dependencies, and temporal requirements, which robot planning and indus-trial control commonly specify in temporal logic (TL). We introduce TL-VLA, a vision–language–action framework that replaces natural-language task instructions with temporal logic, making this spatiotemporal structure explicit. A typed graph encoder and visual–logical fusion condition continuous ac-tions on the task specification and the current observations. To prepare a general vision–language backbone for this inter-face, we develop TL-aware robot pretraining with separate semantic and structural readouts: a frozen language teacher aligns task semantics, the structural readout encodes TL syntax to support complex, long-horizon tasks, and autoregressive action prediction connects the graph representation to robot behavior. Downstream policies inherit the pretrained backbone and TL encoder and learn new fusion and action modules. On CALVIN ABC →D, our TL-aware initializer reaches 3.800 

average chain length and 62.3% five-task success, compared with 3.578 and 54.5% for a FAST initializer pretrained on the same data and 3.485 average chain length for a Qwen+NL baseline. On LIBERO-10, TL-aware initialization improves success from 91.2% to 95.8% . On LIBERO-Mem, our policy reaches 65.0% success without observation history, compared with 45.0% for a Qwen+NL reference. On held-out Panda planning specifications, it reaches 59.6% at Success@256 over three generation seeds, versus 48.4% for Qwen+TL and 37.0% for Qwen+NL. These results support explicit logical task conditioning and transferable TL-aware representations for robot action learning. 

# 1 Introduction 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models connect broad visual and linguistic knowledge to robot control. RT-2, OpenVLA, and π0 show the value of this connection for learning diverse manipulation behaviors [1–3]. A central design choice is how a task enters the policy. Beyond naming an action, a task may specify which objects participate, how they relate, and which conditions must hold in a particular order. A short natural-language instruction often leaves some of this spatiotemporal structure implicit; we instead make it the explicit task input of the policy. Consider placing an object in a drawer and then closing the drawer. Temporal logic (TL) represents semantic intent together with spatial relations, temporal dependencies, and compositional syntax. Predicate arguments bind participants; operators specify how requirements relate. This exposes richer, rule-governed structure than a short benchmark instruction, although natural language can express the same requirements. TL is less broadly used than natural language but is well estab-lished in robotics, control, and industrial systems. Its precise semantics provide a practical foundation for specifying task and system requirements and supporting systematic planning, control, and monitoring [4–7]. This motivates bringing TL into VLA models: can an explicit logical task interface, supported by suitable pretraining, improve learned visuomotor behavior? 

We introduce TL-VLA, which conditions actions on a TL graph, camera observations, and robot state. The graph repre-sents task intent, and perception supplies the current scene. A typed graph encoder preserves operator structure and predicate arguments, and bidirectional visual–logical fusion connects them to visual features. The resulting continuous-action policy takes the graph, rather than an instruction, as its task input; training instructions only supply semantic supervision through a frozen teacher. Because TL conveys both semantic intent and explicit spa-tiotemporal syntax, our pretraining splits graph representations into semantic and structural streams. The semantic stream aligns with task-matched training instructions, while the struc-tural stream retains ordered graph information beyond that alignment target. Joint action-token prediction trains a Qwen3-VL backbone [8] to use these representations on a robot-data mixture. # 2 Related Work 

Vision–language–action learning. RT-2 and OpenVLA transfer visual–language knowledge to robot control [1, 2]; RoboFlamingo adds history modeling through imitation learn-ing [9]. π0 and GR00T N1 generate continuous actions [3, 10]. 

π0.5 and RDT2 improve generalization through heteroge-neous co-training and scaled UMI data, respectively [11, 12]. UniVLA learns task-centric latent actions for cross-embodiment transfer [13], while Moto learns motion tokens from videos [14]. VLAct develops transferable representa-tions through continued robot pretraining [15]. We build on StarVLA [16]. 

Logic-based policies and planning. For nested STL, Yu et al. synthesize continuous-time controllers using temporal-logic trees and control barrier functions [17]. SDT conditions offline Decision Transformers on STL robustness [18], while STL-conditioned diffusion policies generate diverse, rule-compliant trajectories [19]. TeLoGraF learns TL-conditioned trajectories with graph encoding and flow matching [20]. Vision-TL-Action grounds coordinate-free TL graphs in im-ages [21], while S-MSP combines visual observations and STL through a structured mixture of experts [22]. Logic-VLA adds STL requirements to the NL instruction of a quadcopter-navigation VLA, with a pretrained STL graph encoder, su-pervised adaptation, and preference optimization [23]; STeP uses STL for language-based planning, monitoring, and re-planning [24]. We instead replace the instruction with TL as the sole task input and pretrain the VLA backbone and TL encoder jointly on robot data. To our knowledge, no prior work evaluates this interface across manipulation and planning 2benchmarks against language conditioning. 

Task structure and long-horizon evaluation. CALVIN evaluates manipulation chains, LIBERO studies knowledge transfer, and LIBERO-Mem tests history-dependent manipu-lation [25–27]. PDDL describes action models and planning problems [28], while BDDL specifies activity objects and log-ical initial/goal conditions [29]. LIBERO uses BDDL task definitions to connect language instructions, scene initializa-tion, and success checks [26]. For symbolic model construc-tion, pix2pred selects VLM-proposed predicates to learn world models from demonstrations [30], while PDDL-ART gener-ates PDDL domains and problems from a demonstration, lan-guage, and action names [31]. Connections include finite-trace LTL-to-PDDL compilation [32], BDDL grounding within an LTL interface [33], and NL-to-STL translation for cooperative robots in Seeing, Saying, Solving [34]. Our TL graphs expose benchmark-defined goals and temporal structure to the policy. 

# 3 Temporal Logic as the Policy Task Language 

## 3.1 Specification and observation interfaces 

At control time t, let ot = ( I1: Vt , s t) contain the available camera views and proprioceptive state. A task is given as a bounded temporal-logic formula φ. The policy predicts an action chunk 

At = ( at, . . . , a t+Ha−1) ∼ πθ (· | ot, Gφ), (1) where Gφ is a typed syntax graph. The environment executes a prefix of this chunk before the policy receives a new ob-servation. The task graph specifies the desired behavior, and observations provide current evidence. We use predicates such as on( oi, o j ) or closed( ok), logical composition, and bounded temporal operators. A representa-tive grammar is 

φ ::= p(o) | ¬ φ | φ ∧ φ | φ ∨ φ

An ordered eventuality expresses attaining one condition before another. Benchmark adapters define the concrete predicates and intervals of each task.

## 3.3 What the interface makes explicit 

This formulation separates task intent from any particular lan-guage string. Logical edges expose conjunction, alternatives, and temporal structure; predicate arguments expose the partic-ipants of each relation. Changing a destination, for example, changes an argument binding rather than the operator struc-ture; Panda tests such recombination on held-out specifications (Sec. 5.4). In deployment, specifications can come from for-mal requirements that robotic and industrial systems already 3maintain [4, 7], planners for temporally extended goals [32], or NL-to-TL translation [34]. Here, benchmark adapters build them from task definitions and native monitors score success, so task construction, policy input, and evaluation share one specification. 

# 4 TL-Aware Pretraining and Action Learning 

## 4.1 Pretraining data and multimodal condition-ing 

The pretraining backbone is Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct. The robot mixture samples uniformly from Panda, CALVIN, LIBERO, and LIBERO-Mem. Each adapter supplies current observa-tions, a task graph, native actions, and a source-specific control identity. Every source contributes only training data: CALVIN uses the ABC split and holds out environment D, and Panda excludes the evaluation specifications. Images occupy two view slots, and states are padded to 32 dimensions; validity masks mark the available inputs. Actions use source-specific normalization and an eight-step horizon. Embodiment/control and horizon tokens expose these conven-tions to the autoregressive model. The graph encoder produces complementary semantic and structural readouts, Sφ and Lφ, to retain task meaning and formal spatiotemporal structure. The latter adds ordered pool-ing of graph features, preserving the graph sequence inde-pendently of semantic alignment. After visual–logical fusion, the autoregressive prefix concatenates the visual features, the two graph-derived streams, state, control identity, and horizon. These features are projected to the Qwen hidden width and receive type embeddings. The action output is encoded with FAST [35], a widely used action tokenization for VLA pretrain-ing [11]. FAST represents action chunks as discrete tokens, so action learning keeps the backbone’s next-token prediction ob-jective. To separate the effect of our objectives from exposure to embodied data, we also pretrain PI-Fast-pretrain with FAST action prediction alone. It uses the same data mixture, number of updates, task conditioning, and checkpoint-selection rule as TL-pretrain, and adds no semantic or structural objective.

# 5 Experiments 

We evaluate four downstream capabilities: sustained execu-tion of consecutive subtasks on CALVIN, long-horizon task execution on LIBERO-10, sequential manipulation without observation history on LIBERO-Mem, and logical satisfaction of held-out specifications on Panda (Figure 3). 

Task information and comparison scope. Benchmark adapters build each TL graph automatically from the task’s NL description and native symbolic metadata (BDDL where available; examples in the appendix). The graph is fixed per episode: it states goal conditions, bindings, and order, not which conditions already hold, so the policy infers progress from observations. NL instructions come from the same task definitions but may leave bindings and order implicit; mak-ing them explicit is the property under test, so TL-versus-NL rows compare complete pipelines. The graph alone is no ora-cle: with original Qwen, TL trails NL on CALVIN (Table 1). Rows sharing identical graphs isolate TL-aware pretraining. CALVIN and LIBERO evaluate training tasks in new environ-ments or initial states and were not designed to vary formula structure; Panda tests held-out formulas [20]. 95% intervals for key differences use evaluation counts (normal approximation) and cover evaluation sampling only; each policy is trained once (seed 42). 

## 5.1 CALVIN: main long-horizon comparison 

Comparison design of task and evaluation. CALVIN ABC →D tests whether a policy can sustain manipulation across five consecutive subtasks in an unseen environment [25]. The official evaluator issues one subtask at a time and ad-vances once its oracle reports success, so NL rows receive the current instruction and TL rows the current subtask’s graph. CALVIN thus measures sustained execution from the states ear-lier subtasks leave, not parsing of a five-task formula; LIBERO-Mem and Panda supply complete specifications. We compare Qwen+NL, S-MSP [22], Logic-VLA [23], Qwen+TL, PI-Fast-pretrain+NL, PI-Fast-pretrain+TL, and TL-pretrain+TL (Ta-ble 1). The three TL-conditioned initialization controls share the task interface, action head, and downstream recipe: 30k updates, global batch 32, and training seed 42. They differ only in initialization: original Qwen or PI-Fast-pretrain, each with a new TL encoder, or TL-pretrain, which supplies both Qwen and the TL encoder. The NL and external rows are whole-method comparisons; we adapt S-MSP and Logic-VLA to CALVIN, keeping Logic-VLA’s instruction and STL inputs (appendix). The table is a controlled study of task interfaces and initializers, not a leaderboard; methods that redesign the visual and action pathways reach 4.28–4.53 ACL [37–39], and those designs are orthogonal to the task interface studied here. All TL controls use the same 1,000 official sequences, eval-uation seed 0, and at most 360 simulator steps per subtask. At each replanning step, the policy predicts eight actions and exe-cutes five before receiving a new observation. ## 5.3 LIBERO-Mem: sequential manipulation 

On LIBERO-Mem [27], we test whether the TL-pretrained policy executes multi-stage goals without observation history. TL-pretrain+TL receives the complete, fixed task graph, dual-view RGB, and joint state, without observation history or on-line memory. The published memory-free methods, whose results we take from their papers, also score low on these two rearrangements. Task 6 swaps two bowls through an empty plate via either of two three-stage routes. Task 7 rotates three bowls from left to right through the empty plate in a fixed four-stage sequence. ## 5.4 Panda: spatiotemporal-logic satisfaction 

A logic-centered benchmark. We adapt the Franka Panda benchmark from TeLoGraF [20] to visual task conditioning. Its four specification families, I. single-goal , II. multi-goal , III. sequential , and IV. partial order , cover timed goal reaching, Boolean goal combinations, ordered visits, and partial-order dependencies, all with obstacle-avoidance constraints. Beyond 

F and ∧, the templates use G¬ for avoidance, ∨ for alterna-tive goals, and ¬ U for precedence [20]. The 128 evaluation specifications are held out from training, so they require new combinations of operators, bindings, and intervals. Each policy gets the whole formula and orders subgoals itself under partial orders. We compare Qwen+NL, Qwen+TL, and TL-pretrain+TL on all 128 tasks with three generation seeds. For these three mod-els, dual-view images are re-rendered from each candidate’s current generated joint state every eight actions. Success@ K is an offline oracle : a task–seed pair succeeds if any of its first K

candidates satisfies the native STL evaluator. The three models share the candidate-noise bank and a 21,399-update training budget, and both TL models share the downstream recipe. The appendix details the NL comparison and the archived planner references. 

Pretraining and logical task structure.
