# Why Does Sensor Depth Fail Learned Manipulation Policies? Finding the Failure Mode with ACT 

Inayat Rasool 1, Chulwoo Pack 2 and Shabbir Ahmed 1

Abstract — Learned manipulation policies, from task-specific imitation policies to vision-language-action (VLA) models, rely primarily on RGB images. While some studies report gains from sensor depth, others avoid depth cameras because of measurement noise. Depth cameras introduce both noise and missing depth values (holes), whose relative effects remain unclear. In this study, we calibrate a sensor model using a real wrist-mounted stereo camera and separately evaluate noise and holes on RGB-D imitation policies with three visual encoders in simulation. We found that complete depth improves success by approximately ten percentage points, and most of this gain persists even at twenty times the camera’s noise level. In contrast, holes covering about one-sixth of the wrist image eliminate the detectable benefit of depth. The validity mask strongly influences depth reliance: marking a small fraction of pixels as invalid reduces reliance, while filling holes and marking them valid restores most of the benefit. Policies trained on clean depth also lose performance when holes appear only at deployment, sometimes falling below RGB-only performance. These findings suggest that depth cameras for learned policies should therefore be judged by depth coverage more than measurement precision. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have emerged as a prominent approach to robot manipulation and are pretrained on large-scale images and texts datasets, which gives them a strong sense of what objects are and what an instruction asks for [1], [2], [3]. However, robotic manipulation is also a geometric problem, and a robot must know about the position of an object relative to its gripper to grasp or place it precisely. While an RGB image provides indirect cues about the pose and relative distance of a three-dimensional geometry, a depth sensor can directly measure distances in metric units. As a result, VLA models and task-specific manipulation policies are increasingly incorporating depth maps or point clouds as additional inputs. However, existing studies differ on whether depth in-formation should be acquired directly from sensors or inferred through learning-based policies. Studies such as 3D Diffusion Policy [4], RISE [5], iDP3 [6], FP3 [7], 3D Diffuser Actor [8], Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) and Vision Language Action (VLA) policies [9], [10] etc. incorporates depth maps or point clouds through direct sensor measurements and report improved performance. A common motivation for these approaches is the susceptibility of depth sensors to measurement noise and missing depth information [18], [19], [12], [13], [17]. One possible explanation for these differing findings is that “sensor quality” encompasses two distinct limitations: measurement noise and missing depth values (holes). Measure-ment noise affects the accuracy of valid depth measurements, whereas holes occur when the sensor fails to return a depth value. A camera or sensor can be very precise where it provides a measured value, and at the same time, may leave a large part of the image without any depth information (holes). Consequently, a sensor may provide accurate measurements while exhibiting substantial missing data. Previous studies have extensively characterized measurement accuracy of depth and associated noise [20], [21], [22],however, the impact of missing depth or holes on robotic manipulation remains less understood. We therefore ask three questions: (1) Does complete depth information improve manipulation performance? (2) How do measurement noise and missing depth values (holes) independently affect this benefit? (3) What causes the performance degradation, and how can it be mitigated? To address these questions, we characterize the distance-dependent noise and missing-depth rate of a real wrist-mounted stereo camera on a teleoperated robot. Using noise-free depth rendered in simulation, we independently introduce measurement noise and missing values at the measured levels and under scaled perturbations. We evaluate Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policies [23] using three visual encoders: ResNet-18 trained from scratch, DINOv2 [24] with 2D pretraining, and MoGe-2 [25] with geometric pretraining. We quantify the contribution of depth using two metrics: 

benefit , defined as the improvement in task success relative to an RGB-only policy with the same encoder, and reliance ,defined as the reduction in success when depth input is removed at deployment. This paper makes four contributions: (1) a controlled evaluation of depth noise and holes, with a sensor model fitted to a real wrist camera; (2) provide evidence that the holes, not noise, remove the benefit of sensor depth for the studied policies; (3) provide evidence that validity masks cause policies to disregard incomplete depth information; and (4) provide practical guidance for depth sensor selection and preprocessing, along with a metric ( reliance ) to quantify whether a trained policy uses depth information. II. S TUDY DESIGN 

To isolate the effects of individual depth input qualities, experiments need to be designed such that one property varies at a time while holding all others constant. Since measurement noise and missing depth values cannot be independently controlled on a physical camera, we render ground-truth depth in the ManiSkill3 simulator [26] and introduce con-trolled degradations using a model calibrated to real camera measurements. The experimental setup consists of a 7 degrees-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda robotic arm equipped with a wrist-mounted camera and a third-person camera. We employ a factorial design across depth conditions and visual encoders, maintaining identical policy architectures, demonstrations, action spaces, evaluation episodes, and training schedules across all configurations. 

A. Sensor Model Fitted to a Real Wrist Camera 

We recorded 706 teleoperated episodes at 15 Hz on a Franka Emika Panda driven by a GELLO leader arm [27] (Fig. 2a). The episodes cover seven task families, such as pick-and-place, stacking, pushing and drawer opening. The wrist camera is an Intel RealSense D405, a short-range stereo camera with an 18 mm baseline and a rated range of 7–50 cm. The third-person camera is a RealSense D435. To quantify depth measurement noise, we select a fixed 6×

6,cm 2 patch on the table and record its depth measurements across successive video frames. For each frame, a best-fit plane is estimated from the valid 3D points within the patch, and the noise level σ is defined as the root-mean-square distance of these points from the fitted plane. As we move the wrist camera every 5 cm distance, σ increases from 0.17 mm at 12.5 cm to 2.71 mm at 62.5 cm. This trend is approximated by the power law σ(d) = 0 .35 , (d/ 20 , cm) 1.79 mm (Fig. 2b), with an exponent close to 2, consistent with the expected quadratic dependence of stereo-triangulation depth uncertainty on distance. The depth hole rate is defined as the fraction of pixels in each frame without valid depth measurements (Fig. 2c). For the wrist camera, the hole rate ranges from 17.5% in the nearest distance to 3.4% in the farthest, reaching a maximum of 22.1% at intermediate distances. The rate also depends on surface characteristics, as stereo matching relies on visual texture and can fail on reflective or transparent surfaces. At the simulated robot’s median working distance, the calibrated model predicts a depth noise level of 0.19 mm and a hole rate of 16.4% for the wrist camera, compared with 2.86 mm and 2.1%, respectively, for the third-person camera. Thus, the wrist camera provides approximately 15 times lower depth noise but exhibits about 8 times higher proportion of holes. Using the calibrated sensor model, we independently introduce depth noise and missing values into the simulated depth maps. Measurement noise is modeled as zero-mean Gaussian noise applied independently to each pixel, with the standard deviation σ determined by its depth. This model excludes systematic bias, spatial noise correlation, and artifacts near depth discontinuities, such as flying pixels. B. Depth Conditions 

Table I summarizes the depth conditions used in this study. To investigate the effect of missing depth values (holes), we compare two conditions: sensor depth, which contains both noise and holes based on the calibrated camera model, and hole-free sensor depth, which contains the same noise but no holes. During training, the noise is generated using a fixed seed for each frame, ensuring identical noise values in both conditions. Across evaluation rollouts, the wrist-camera depth RMSE is 0.22mm without holes and 0.25mm with holes, while the corresponding hole rates are 0.0% and 16.1%, respectively. Two additional experiments independently vary noise and hole severity. The noise sweep scales measurement noise by factors of 5, 20, 50, and 100 without introducing holes, reaching a wrist-camera RMSE of 22.37mm. The hole sweep scales the calibrated hole rate from 1/8 to 4, producing missing-depth rates of 2.0% to 64.3%. Additional levels of 1/16 and 1/32 are used for ResNet-18, while all encoders are evaluated across all levels at deployment (Sec. III-D). Perfect depth uses the simulator’s ground-truth measurements, whereas constant depth assigns a fixed value to each camera to retain the depth input pathway without providing geometric information. 

C. Policy and Depth Input 

We use the ACT policy [23] implemented in LeRobot [28], modifying only the visual feature extraction module (Fig. 2d). Each camera provides two depth channels: metric depth, normalized over fixed ranges of 0–2 m for the wrist camera and 0–10 m for the third-person camera, and a binary validity mask indicating missing measurements. Fixed normalization preserves the magnitude of depth errors, while the validity mask distinguishes missing values from valid measurements. A lightweight trainable depth encoder comprising three con-volutional blocks (approximately 0.2,M parameters) extracts depth features, which are concatenated with RGB features before the policy projection layer, allowing each visual token to incorporate both color and depth information. To evaluate the influence of visual pretraining, we consider three image encoders: ResNet-18 trained from scratch, DI-NOv2 [24], a frozen ViT-B/14 pretrained on 2D imagery, and MoGe-2 [25], a frozen ViT-B/14 further trained from DINOv2 weights for geometric understanding. Comparing DINOv2 and MoGe-2 isolates the effect of geometry-oriented pretraining, while ResNet-18 provides a baseline without pretraining. 

D. Tasks, Training and Evaluation 

STACK CUBE , which requires stacking one cube on an-other, is evaluated using ResNet-18 across the main depth conditions to assess generalization to a second task. Motion-planning demonstrations are used for both tasks, with 150 for PICK CUBE and 1000 for S TACK CUBE , selected to maintain moderate RGB-only success rates. Images from both cameras are rendered at 256 × 256 pixels and resized to 128 × 128 

before depth corruption. The policy predicts 16-action chunks, executes 8 actions per cycle, and controls changes in end-effector pose. Training uses AdamW for 30,000 steps with a batch size of 64 and learning rates of 10 −4, or 10 −5 for trainable visual encoders and the depth stem. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected using 10% held-out demonstrations. Each configuration is trained with 8 random seeds and evaluated over 200 episodes per seed using identical scenes. An episode is considered successful if the task’s success criterion is met before the step limit.

Benefit measures improvement over RGB-only performance, includ-ing any effect of the additional depth-processing parameters, whereas reliance measures sensitivity to depth removal using the same trained policy and evaluation episodes. The blank map assigns the camera’s median depth for that frame to all pixels, marked as valid. To reduce the influence of out-of-distribution blank inputs, we also evaluate scene reliance by replacing depth with a corrupted depth map from a randomly selected step in another demonstration scene. The halving point is the hole rate at which reliance falls to half its hole-free value, estimated by linear interpolation with bootstrap confidence intervals over seeds. Statistical comparisons use training runs as independent replicates. For each seed, success differences are computed on matched evaluation episodes and assessed using a one-sample t-test across 8 seeds, with Holm correction for multiple comparisons addressing the same question. This avoids treating evaluation episodes from the same trained policy as independent training replicates. III. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS 

A. Complete Depth Helps 

We first evaluate the benefit of complete depth information (Table II). Perfect depth improves mean success across all policies, with gains of 8.2, 8.6, and 8.8 pp for ResNet-18, DINOv2, and MoGe-2 on P ICK CUBE , respectively, although none is individually significant ( p = 0 .064 to 0.078 ). On STACK CUBE , the gain reaches 17.1 pp ( p = 0.004 ) is consistent across all 8 seeds, and remains significant after Holm correction. Hole-free sensor depth yields comparable improvements of 9.9–10.0 pp on P ICK CUBE significant for each encoder before correction, and 19.3 pp on S TACK CUBE .To distinguish the contribution of geometric information from that of additional network capacity, we compare perfect depth with constant depth, which preserves the depth-processing pathway without providing scene geometry. Perfect depth improves success by 15.3, 5.2, and 5.9 pp on PICK CUBE , and 7.9 pp on S TACK CUBE . Only the ResNet-18 comparison on P ICK CUBE is significant ( p = 0 .003 ); the other P ICK CUBE comparisons yield p = 0 .081 to 0.153 .Relative to RGB-only, constant depth changes success by 

−7.1, +3.3, and +2.8pp on P ICK CUBE , and +9.1pp on STACK CUBE , indicating that the depth pathway itself can affect performance. In particular, the unexplained performance reduction for ResNet-18 with constant depth ( p = 0 .06 )makes its estimated information gain larger than its overall benefit. On S TACK CUBE , approximately half the observed benefit is associated with the additional pathway rather than geometric information. Overall, the results suggest depth-related improvements of approximately 5–15 pp, consistent with prior studies [29], [4], [5]. Stronger evidence for the contribution of depth information comes from the controlled hole comparisons (Sec. III-B) and reliance analysis. 

B. Holes, Not Noise, Remove the Benefit 

It was found that the measurement noise has little effect on manipulation performance. Hole-free sensor depth performs comparably to perfect depth across all encoders and tasks, with no significant differences. Even at 20 times the calibrated noise level (wrist RMSE of 4.52mm), depth provides benefits of 11.0, 7.3, and 5.6 pp for ResNet-18, DINOv2, and MoGe-2, respectively, with statistically significant improvements for two encoders before correction for multiple comparisons. At 100 times the noise (22.37mm), none of the benefits remain significant, although policies continue to use depth, with reliance at least 29.4 pp throughout the sweep (Fig. 3a). Policies trained on perfect depth also show limited sensitivity to noise introduced only at deployment, with success changes of −1.1, +0.6, and −0.8pp at 20 times the noise. These findings indicate that depth precision is not the primary limitation within the tested range, although the noise model excludes spatially correlated and structured errors. In contrast, missing depth values substantially reduce performance. At the calibrated wrist-camera hole rate of 16.1%, sensor-depth policies achieve improvements over RGB-only policies of −1.3, +1.8, and +0.3pp on P ICK CUBE ,and +6.4pp on S TACK CUBE , none of which is statistically significant. However, a direct comparison with hole-free sensor depth reveals performance reductions of 11.2, 8.1, and 9.7 pp on P ICK CUBE , and 12.9 pp on S TACK CUBE ,demonstrating the adverse effect of missing depth information. All four differences remain significant after Holm correction (p ≤ 0.034 ), although their magnitudes vary across seeds. To identify the source of this degradation, we introduce holes only in the wrist-camera depth. Performance is statistically indistinguishable from introducing holes in both cameras ( p ≥

0.39 ), while comparisons with hole-free depth yield changes of −13.2, −6.2, and −10.9pp ( p ≤ 0.022 ). This indicates that the observed degradation is primarily associated with missing wrist-camera depth, despite its higher measurement precision. Whether the resulting loss reflects misleading depth information or reduced reliance on depth is examined next. Replacing depth with a blank map can overestimate a policy’s reliance on depth information, as the unfamiliar input may itself degrade performance. For policies trained on hole-free sensor depth, blanking reduces success below the RGB-only baseline by 30.5, 24.9, and 41.8 pp on P ICK CUBE ,suggesting that approximately three-quarters of the measured reliance may reflect sensitivity to out-of-distribution inputs. We therefore emphasize scene reliance , which replaces depth with measurements from another scene while preserving realistic input characteristics. For hole-free sensor depth, scene reliance is 8.1, 29.8, and 33.2,pp on P ICK CUBE ,and 14.5,pp on S TACK CUBE , all statistically significant. ResNet-18 exhibits substantially lower scene reliance than the pretrained encoders, indicating weaker use of depth geometry. In contrast, policies trained with sensor-depth holes exhibit scene reliance of only +1.9, −0.4, and +2.4pp on P ICK CUBE , and +1.4pp on S TACK CUBE . These results suggest that policies trained with missing depth rely primarily on RGB information rather than exploiting the available depth measurements. Consistently, across 69 depth-trained configurations, 17 show significant benefits ( p < 0.05 ), of which only two exhibit blank-depth reliance below 10 pp. The hole sweep further quantifies how reliance decreases with missing depth (Fig. 3b). Scene reliance falls to half its hole-free value at hole rates of 0.9%, 1.9%, and 6.1% for ResNet-18, DINOv2, and MoGe-2, respectively, with corre-sponding 95% bootstrap intervals of [0.4, 7.6], [1.5, 2.9], and [1.5, 9.5]%. Blank-depth reliance reaches the same threshold at higher hole rates: 0.9%, 6.2%, and 12.6%, respectively. On S TACK CUBE , the scene-reliance and blank-depth halving points are 1.1% and 1.2%. All point estimates fall below the calibrated wrist-camera hole rate of 16.1%, indicating that the measured level of missing depth is sufficient to substantially reduce depth reliance. These thresholds are specific to the simulated random-hole model, and their uncertainty is limited by the 8 training seeds. The findings motivate a closer examination of which depth-input components cause policies to disregard geometric information.

Averaged over the two hole values, a mask that marks every pixel valid increases reliance by 22.9, 24.7 and 34.1 pp for ResNet-18, DINOv2 and MoGe-2 ( p ≤ 0.004 ). Their reliance is 5.3, 4.4 and 23.2 pp, against 40.4, 34.9 and 51.8 pp for depth that never had holes, and no benefit differs measurably from zero. The policy ignores filled depth that the mask marks, as it ignores depth with holes. Empty holes that are marked valid make the policy use depth, and reliance increases to 16.0, 22.9 and 56.6 pp. However, no benefit is significant, and ResNet-18 changes by −5.1 pp ( p = 0 .054 ), a possible cost, because the depth now says that a sixth of the wrist image is a surface at zero distance. Thus a policy stops using depth when the mask marks part of the image as missing, and it benefits from depth when every pixel is marked valid and the values are plausible. Multimodal networks are known to ignore one of their inputs [30], [31], [32]. Our result gives a cause for depth, which is a mark that tells the network in every frame that the input is incomplete. The result holds for random hole placement, and a fill on hardware can put invented depth on the object to grasp (Sec. III-F). 

D. A Policy Keeps the Decision It Made in Training 

In the above analysis, each policy has the same holes in training and at deployment. A policy might also be trained on complete depth, from simulation or from cleaned data, and then deployed on a real sensor. We therefore deploy each policy trained on hole-free sensor depth at every level of the hole sweep (Fig. 4c). With 0.5% holes, success changes by −3.6, −6.4 and −10.1 pp for ResNet-18, DINOv2 and MoGe-2 ( p ≤ 0.049 ). With 2.0% holes the change is −10.9, 

−17.1 and −28.4 pp, and with 64.3% it is −16.8, −24.1 and 

−39.0 pp. These policies stay better than the same weights with blank depth by at least 10.0 pp, so they still use the depth that is left. The important comparison is with a policy that never had depth. At the camera’s hole rate, success changes against the RGB-only policy by −27.1 pp for MoGe-2 ( p = 0 .002 ) and by −14.2 pp for DINOv2 ( p = 0 .013 ), both significant before correction, while ResNet-18 changes by −4.1 pp ( p = 0 .258 ). A policy trained on clean depth and deployed on a real sensor can thus be worse than a policy with no depth. The pretrained encoders, which take the most scene information from depth, lose the most. The reverse case gives no gain. We deploy each policy trained with holes on depth with the holes removed. In 26 such cases, no change is larger than 4.1 pp, and none is significant after a Holm correction. These results suggest that, during training, the policy learns how much to rely on depth and largely retains this behavior at deployment. Depth noise has little effect on this reliance, whereas a validity mask indicating missing regions in the wrist image substantially reduces it. This interpretation is consistent with the observed results, although we did not examine the network’s internal representations to verify the underlying mechanism. 

E. Outlook for VLA Models 

Our experiments use ACT rather than a VLA model, so the implications discussed here remain hypotheses that require direct validation. Nevertheless, several architectural components relevant to our findings are shared with existing VLA models. The frozen DINOv2 encoder is one of the two visual encoders used in OpenVLA [2], while recent VLA fine-tuning approaches adopt ACT’s chunked, regression-based action head [33]. When depth is introduced into a pretrained VLA during fine-tuning, the new depth pathway must learn to contribute alongside an RGB pathway that already provides substantial task-relevant information. Based on our findings, we therefore expect that a VLA that receives depth as image tokens will learn to ignore these tokens if its wrist camera marks a sixth of the view as invalid, as the camera in this study does. 

F. Limitations 

Although the depth corruption model is calibrated using measurements from a real camera, all policy evaluations are conducted in simulation, with demonstrations generated by a motion planner. The corruption model also simplifies several characteristics of real depth measurements. Specifically, holes are represented as square blocks, placed independently of the scene and resampled at every frame. In contrast, real depth holes often form larger connected regions, occur more frequently on flat or nearby surfaces, and persist on the same surfaces over time. To assess the effect of hole placement, we conducted an additional experiment in which holes followed the spatial pattern observed in the real camera, while keeping the overall hole rate unchanged. Compared with random placement, success changed by −2.2, +0.5, and −1.0,pp for ResNet-18, DINOv2, and MoGe-2, respectively, with none of these differences statistically significant. Reliance changed by at most 2.6,pp. However, these findings do not rule out an effect of hole placement. The confidence interval for ResNet-18, [ −9.4, +4.9],pp, includes a possible performance loss. Furthermore, the comparison was performed at the camera’s observed hole rate, where the policies already showed little reliance on depth regardless of hole placement. Therefore, the estimated halving points and the observed benefits of hole filling remain specific to random hole placement. Holes concentrated on the object being grasped could lead to different outcomes. The noise model introduces independent errors at each pixel and therefore does not capture spatially or temporally correlated depth errors. It also does not account for the camera’s minimum sensing distance of approximately 7,cm, below which valid depth measurements are unavailable. Beyond these modeling assumptions, the study is limited to a single policy class, one camera model, and two grasping tasks. We additionally evaluated a third task involving planar pushing, but the policies showed neither measurable reliance on depth nor a performance benefit from it across three seeds. These limitations motivate further evaluation with real robotic hardware, more realistic depth corruption patterns, and a broader range of manipulation tasks. IV. C ONCLUSIONS 

This study reconciles two competing perspectives on the value of depth sensing in learned robotic manipulation. While complete depth information improves policy performance, depth from a real wrist-mounted camera may provide little or no benefit. By separating sensor noise from missing depth measurements, we show that the loss of benefit is primarily as-sociated with depth holes rather than measurement noise. Even when noise is increased to twenty times the camera’s measured level, most of the benefit remains, with a performance cost bounded by 3.6pp (one-sided 95% bound). In contrast, depth holes substantially reduce the policy’s reliance on depth, particularly when the validity mask identifies missing regions. Filling these holes and marking the corresponding pixels as valid restores most of the benefit. These findings suggest that sensor noise alone is not a compelling reason to avoid depth sensing in the setting studied here, although we do not directly compare sensor depth with alternative depth-estimation approaches. The overall trends are consistent across all three visual encoders, although their sensitivity to missing depth differs. The estimated halving points range from 0.9% to 12.6%, while the pretrained encoders extract more task-relevant information from depth and experience larger performance losses when holes appear only at deployment. Whether an explicit 3D prior improves the usefulness of sensor depth remains unresolved. The measured difference between DINOv2 and MoGe-2 is 

−1.5pp, with a 95% confidence interval of [ −12.7, +9.7] pp, which is too wide to establish or rule out an effect. Detecting differences smaller than approximately 11.2pp would require additional seeds. These results have several practical implications for in-tegrating depth sensors into learned manipulation policies. First, camera selection should prioritize depth coverage at the robot’s working distance rather than measurement precision alone, since the fraction of missing depth can be more consequential than sensor noise. Second, missing depth values should be filled before being passed to the policy, with the corresponding pixels marked as valid. Although the original validity mask remains necessary to identify holes during preprocessing, retaining these invalid markings in the policy input can suppress depth utilization even after filling. Third, training and deployment should use consistent depth preprocessing, including realistic missing-depth patterns and the same hole-filling procedure, to reduce the mismatch between simulated or recorded training data and real sensor measurements. Finally, depth utilization should be evaluated explicitly, for example by replacing the depth input with depth from another scene, rather than inferring its contribution solely from task success. These recommendations apply to policies receiving depth as an image input and require validation on physical robotic systems.
