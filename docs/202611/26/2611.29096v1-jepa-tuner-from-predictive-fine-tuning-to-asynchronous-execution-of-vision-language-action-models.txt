## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models generate sequences of actions from visual observations, proprioceptive states, and language instructions. In embodied interaction, ex-ecuting these actions influences subsequent observations and robot states, providing a natural predictive signal for policy learning. However, standard action-supervised fine-tuning does not explicitly capture these action-conditioned temporal dependencies in the guidance consumed by the action predictor. We introduce JEPA-Tuner , a predictive fine-tuning framework that regularizes the VLA’s native pol-icy guidance through action-conditioned future prediction. During fine-tuning, a predictor in JEPA-Tuner learns to match guidance encoded from a future observation given the current guidance and committed actions. This objec-tive encourages a cross-modal guidance adapter to cap-ture how the scene and robot state evolve under those ac-tions, while jointly training the action expert on both ob-served and predicted guidance keeps the representation in-formative for control. In addition, when deploying the VLA model with asynchronous action-chunk inference, the pre-trained predictor will anticipate the guidance needed at the next chunk boundary. The policy can thus generate the next chunk in advance and execute it from its first action without overlap correction or additional predictor train-ing, enabling efficient and high-quality asynchronous exe-cution. Experiments on LIBERO and Meta-World show that JEPA-Tuner improves task success under synchronous in-ference with better multi-modal guidance embeddings. For asynchronous control, JEPA-Tuner improves performance and smoothness across different latency settings on both LIBERO simulator and multi-size real robot demonstra-tions. ## 1. Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models integrate visual ob-servations, language instructions, and proprioceptive states to generate robot action chunks [4, 5, 16, 28]. Many recent VLAs couple pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) [1, 2] with action experts through multimodal pol-icy guidance (vision, language, robotic states, etc.). Struc-turing this multimodal guidance is important for both pol-icy adaptation and asynchronous execution. When a robot executes an action on an object, it changes both the arm configuration and the object’s position. Prior works used this relationship to anticipate input to a policy. For in-stance, VLASH [31] estimates future proprioceptive states using current states and committed actions, while Futur-eRTC [14] predicts visual representations and propriocep-tive states to correct a frozen VLA policy. These exam-ples suggest that multimodal control signals are coupled through learnable predictive dependencies. However, stan-dard action-supervised fine-tuning does not enforce these dependencies in the guidance consumed by the action ex-pert. We therefore use the observations of executed actions as an additional training signal for the guidance that condi-tions the action expert. Specifically, we encourage action-conditioned predictive consistency by jointly training a pre-dictor to predict future guidance and proprioception from the current guidance, and further align the policy on it. Action-conditioned prediction also offers a natural way to address inference latency in asynchronous control. As Fig. 1 illustrates, the robot continues to move while the next chunk is computed, shifting execution toward later pre-dictions conditioned on an earlier observation. Real-Time Chunking (RTC) [6] corrects the overlap with committed actions through inference-time inpainting, while training based method (e.g., T-RTC [7], Legato [23]) learn smooth-ness during training to continue the committed trajectory. Although conditioning on the committed action prefix can smooth transitions between chunks, a strong bias toward continuing the previous trajectory can limit the action ex-1arXiv:2611.29096v1 [cs.RO] 21 Nov 2026 pert’s ability to respond to state changes, reducing closed-loop task success under inference delay [20, 23]. To capture action-conditioned dependencies in multi-modal guidance and better balance trajectory smoothness with action accuracy during asynchronous inference, we introduce JEPA-Tuner , a fine-tuning framework inspired by joint-embedding predictive architectures for robot con-trol [24, 30, 33]. Specifically, a cross-modal adapter mixes visual, language, and state information at the VLA’s native input prefix. Given the adapted guidance at step t, the robot state, and the next d demonstrated actions, a predictor es-timates the future guidance and proprioceptive state at step 

t + d. In VLA model finetuning, we further train the ac-tion expert to generate action chunks from both observed and predicted guidance, while jointly training the predic-tor to match the guidance encoded from the observation at 

t + d. This joint objective encourages the predicted guid-ance to capture the future scene and state while retaining the information needed for action generation. During asyn-chronous inference, we reuse the same predictor with the actions committed before the handoff, allowing the expert to generate the next chunk in advance and execute it from its first action without overlap correction. Our main contributions are as follows: • We identify action-conditioned temporal dependencies in multimodal guidance that standard action-supervised VLA fine-tuning ignored. Motivated by this observa-tion, we propose JEPA-Tuner , which improves VLA fine-tuning by regularizing native multimodal guidance through future prediction while jointly training the action expert on both observed and predicted guidance. • To address latency-induced conditioning mismatch dur-ing asynchronous control, we develop a predictive exe-cution scheme that reuses the predictor learned by JEPA-Tuner during fine-tuning to anticipate guidance at the next chunk handoff. This enables each new chunk to directly execute from its first action with anticipated guidance without additional training or overlap correction. • Experiments on LIBERO and Meta-World demonstrate improved VLA fine-tuning performance. Asynchronous evaluations further demonstrate a better trade-off between task success and action smoothness, both in simulation and on robot platforms of different scales, SO-101 and ALOHA, under standard and severe inference latency. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and generalist robot policies integrate visual observations and language in-structions for robot control. RT-2 [39] represents actions as tokens, while OpenVLA [16] and Octo [13] advance generalist policy pretraining and adaptation. OpenVLA-OFT [15] studies efficient action decoding and fine-tuning. Recently, diffusion [12] and flow matching [18] play an important role in generative modeling across multiple data modalities [26, 27, 35]. These frameworks also support continuous action generation: π0 [5] introduces a flow-matching action expert, π0.5 [4] extends this paradigm through heterogeneous co-training, and SmolVLA [28] em-phasizes compact, affordable action-chunk policies. Unless otherwise specified, we consider VLA policies that generate continuous action chunks using diffusion or flow matching. 

2.2. Predictive Learning for VLA Policies 

VLA-JEPA [30] and JEPA-WAM [17] impose temporal supervision through visual targets defined by pretrained V-JEPA encoders, while JEPA-VLA [24] augments exist-ing VLAs with pretrained video-predictive embeddings. WoG [29] learns compact future-observation conditions for action generation, and SLIM [33] models action-conditioned transitions within its own learned observation-latent space. Their future-representation targets or trans-ferred features are primarily visual, even when actions and proprioception provide conditioning. These formula-tions do not address the joint problem of shaping an exist-ing VLA’s native multimodal guidance and forecasting that guidance at execution boundaries under inference latency. JEPA-Tuner addresses this joint problem by learning action-conditioned predictive consistency within an exist-ing VLA’s native policy guidance space. As a plug-in fine-tuning framework, it couples future visual-guidance and proprioceptive-state prediction with action supervision, di-rectly regularizing the temporal evolution of the condition-ing consumed by the action expert. Crucially, the same learned predictor is reused to anticipate guidance at the next execution boundary, unifying downstream representa-tion adaptation with latency-aware asynchronous control. 

2.3. Asynchronous Inference of VLA Models 

Asynchronous VLA methods seek continuous, temporally aligned control under inference latency. RTC [6] uses inference-time inpainting conditioned on committed ac-tions, while Training-Time RTC [7] learns committed-prefix conditioning during training. Legato [23] similarly learns continuation for flow-based policies. These methods promote temporal compatibility in action space. Another complementary strategy anticipates execution-time conditioning. VLASH [31] rolls proprioception forward while retaining earlier visual observations; Ea-gleVLA [36] predicts future compressed VLM features; and FutureRTC [14] forecasts visual latents and propri-oceptive states for a frozen policy. Latent Bridge [22] predicts conditioning-feature changes to reduce backbone invocations. However, these methods primarily exploit prediction for deployment, leaving underexplored whether action-conditioned predictive regularization of native multi-modal guidance can improve VLA fine-tuning. JEPA-Tuner obtains two benefits from a single fine-tuning procedure: 2improved VLA finetuning performance through predictive guidance regularization and a predictor directly reusable for asynchronous control. The jointly learned predictor anticipates execution-boundary guidance, supporting pre-fix execution without a separate predictor-training stage or inference-time overlap correction.

## 4. JEPA-Tuner Methodology 

In this section, we first identify two challenges: under-exploited action-conditioned multimodal dependencies in VLA fine-tuning and stale guidance during asynchronous execution. We then introduce JEPA-Tuner , which ad-dresses both through predictive modeling in the VLA’s na-tive guidance space, as introduced in Fig. 2. 

4.1. Challenges 

Underexploited intrinsic multimodal dependencies in VLA fine-tuning. Visual observations, language instruc-tions, proprioceptive states, and actions describe intercon-nected aspects of robotic control. Vision and proprioception describe the environment and robot configuration, while language specifies task intent. Together, they condition action generation. Executed actions subsequently change the robot state and observed environment. These comple-mentary relationships suggest that policy guidance should capture both how multimodal context informs actions and how that context evolves under their execution. However, standard action-supervised fine-tuning primarily optimizes observation-to-action prediction, without explicitly model-ing the latter dependency. Guidance sufficient for action generation is therefore not necessarily structured to predict its future counterparts. This motivates action-conditioned predictive consistency in the VLA’s native guidance space, encouraging representations that preserve intrinsic temporal dependencies while remaining informative for control. 

High-latency execution under stale conditioning. 

Chunked policies typically execute only the first E < H 

actions before replanning from updated observations, leaving an unexecuted suffix of length H − E [5, 11]. Under asynchronous inference, latency separates the observation used for prediction from the context in which the resulting actions take effect. As discussed in Sec. 3, skipping the elapsed prefix shifts the executed positions from 0, . . . , E − 1 to ∆, . . . , ∆ + E − 1, provided that 

∆ + E ≤ H. Increasing latency consequently brings more of the normally unexecuted suffix into the execution window while retaining earlier observation-derived guid-ance. For example, with H = 12 , E = 8 , and ∆ = 4 in Fig. 1, four of the eight executed predictions come from this suffix. 4.3. From Predictive Learning to Asynchronous Ex-ecution

## 5. Experiments 

We first use synchronous inference to test whether action-conditioned predictive supervision improves VLA fine-tuning. We follow the standard protocols on LIBERO [19] and Meta-World MT50 [37], counting an episode as suc-cessful when the policy achieves the task goal within the episode limit. We then reuse the predictor during asyn-chronous execution and evaluate both task success and the smoothness of action trajectories, first on LIBERO and then on real robots SO-101 and ALOHA. The supplemen-tary material gives the backbone-specific implementations, baseline configurations, deployment settings, latency mea-surements, and ablation studies on the multimodal adapter and predictor in JEPA-Tuner. Specifically, the ablations show that our design enables accurate prediction in the guidance embedding space without compromising action learning, while multimodal interaction itself can also im-proves policy learning. Therefore, JEPA-Tuner combines guidance prediction with multimodal interaction to improve VLA fine-tuning and enable asynchronous execution by an-ticipating the guidance required at the next chunk handoff. 

5.1. Benefits for VLA Fine-Tuning 

We compare JEPA-Tuner with standard fine-tuning (Stan-dard FT) on SmolVLA [28], π0.5 [4], and GR00T [3]. Dur-ing synchronous evaluation, both policies decode actions from the current observation, and JEPA-Tuner’s predictor is inactive. On LIBERO, we use the published Standard FT results [30] and fine-tune the unavailable weights by our-selves. On Meta-World MT50, we match demonstrations and training budgets within each backbone and evaluate 50 episodes per task. JEPA-Tuner improves average task success over Stan-dard FT for all three VLA backbones on both LIBERO and Meta-World MT50 (Tables 1 and 2). On LIBERO, for example, average success rises from 77 .9% to 78 .4% 

for SmolVLA and from 93 .9% to 96 .8% for GR00T. On Meta-World MT50, π0.5 improves from 37 .2% to 44 .0% ,and GR00T improves from 57 .7% to 65 .5% . In all cases, the action expert decodes directly from observed guidance with the predictor disabled at inference. These results sug-gest that incorporating action-conditioned prediction of fu-ture guidance into VLA fine-tuning helps the policy gener-ate more effective action chunks from current observations, improving manipulation success even when latency com-pensation is unnecessary.

## 6. Conclusions 

In this paper, we identify two challenges in VLA learning and deployment: underexploited action-conditioned depen-dencies in multimodal guidance and stale conditioning dur-ing asynchronous execution. Motivated by them, we intro-duce a unified VLA fine-tuning framework, JEPA-Tuner .During fine-tuning, joint supervision of future-guidance 8prediction and action generation captures temporal depen-dencies in the VLA’s native guidance while preserving its utility for control. During asynchronous execution, the same predictor uses committed actions to anticipate guid-ance at the next handoff, enabling each new chunk to ex-ecute from its first action without overlap correction. Ex-periments on LIBERO and Meta-World MT50 demonstrate improved fine-tuning performance across all three evaluated backbones, even with the predictor inactive. Asynchronous evaluations in simulation and on SO-101 and ALOHA fur-ther support a favorable trade-off between task success and action continuity under inference latency. Although JEPA-Tuner has the potential to extend to large-scale VLA pre-training and more diverse real-world tasks, our evaluation remains limited to small-scale fine-tuning and two robot platforms; we leave these extensions to future work.

## C. Implementation Details of JEPA-Tuner 

We freeze the pretrained vision–language backbone and jointly train a cross-modal guidance adapter, an action-conditioned predictor, the action expert, and any trainable state projection. For a valid pair of observations d steps apart in the same demonstration, the predictor takes the adapted guidance gt, measured state st, intervening demon-strated actions at, . . . , a t+d−1, and gap d. Its targets are the visual guidance encoded from the later observation and the measured state st+d. The task instruction stays fixed across the pair. We train the same action expert to generate the demonstrated chunk starting at t from observed guidance and the chunk starting at t + d from predicted guidance. Both terms use the backbone’s flow-matching objective, so the predictor must encode information needed for the ac-tions that follow the predicted state. Synchronous execution uses observed guidance. Asynchronous execution reuses the predictor with the actions already committed through the next handoff. For SmolVLA and π0.5 [4, 28], we insert a one-layer residual attention adapter at the input prefix. It has eight attention heads and a feed-forward bottleneck of 256. Zero-initialized output projections make the adapter an identity map at initialization. The predictor is a two-layer trans-former with eight heads and feed-forward width 2,048. It reads the adapted prefix, eight tokens encoding the inter-vening actions, and one learned gap token. Residual heads predict changes to the visual embeddings and the normal-ized state. The loss weights for the current action loss, nor-malized visual squared error, visual cosine distance, nor-malized state squared error, and future action loss are 1, 1,

0.1, 1, and 1, respectively. Visual and state errors are nor-malized separately. We omit padding and fixed instruction tokens from the prediction targets. The two backbones encode proprioception differently. SmolVLA projects the robot state into one token. On LIBERO, its prefix has 177 positions of width 960, compris-ing 64 tokens per camera for two cameras, 48 instruction positions, and the state token. We predict proprioception in normalized state coordinates, pass the prediction through SmolVLA’s trainable state projection, and insert it at the state position. We run the adapter on this prefix to recover the state token in context, then combine it with the predicted visual tokens and the observed instruction. This preserves the state input used by the action expert. In π0.5, the state enters through the instruction prompt. On LIBERO, the PaliGemma prefix has 968 positions of width 2,048. Each of three camera slots occupies 256 po-sitions, with two real views and one masked slot, followed by 200 prompt positions. We discretize the predicted state into 256 bins, rebuild the prompt with the same instruction, update its padding mask, and apply the adapter in context. We combine the rebuilt prompt with the predicted image embeddings. Each valid camera contributes equally to the normalized visual prediction loss. For the Meta-World experiments labeled “GR00T” in Table 2 of the main paper, we use the GR00T-N1.7-3B checkpoint [3], with its backbone frozen and its action head configured for the new embodiment. A one-layer, 16-head cross-modal adapter includes a state token in the features used for action conditioning. The action-conditioned pre-dictor has approximately 110 million parameters. We train the action head on predicted guidance with a future action loss weight of 1.0, and use the observed guidance for the synchronous evaluation. Both Standard FT and JEPA-Tuner use 20-action chunks and execute ten actions per chunk on this benchmark. 

## D. Experimental Settings 

For every comparison, we match the training budget within each backbone: all methods that we train build on the same pretrained weights and demonstrations, with no baseline receiving fewer optimizer steps than JEPA-Tuner, and the training-free asynchronous baselines reuse the Standard FT checkpoint. 

D.1. Synchronous simulation benchmarks 

Standard FT trains the action expert to generate demon-strated actions from the current observation. On LIBERO [19], we evaluate the Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites. A rollout succeeds when the environ-ment’s task-specific goal condition is satisfied within its episode limit. The suite success rate is the fraction of suc-cessful rollouts, and the LIBERO average gives each suite equal weight. The π0.5 and GR00T Standard FT rows use published backbone results [30], while we obtain the 13 SmolVLA Standard FT row by fine-tuning its pretrained base following the official configuration. For synchronous inference, JEPA-Tuner uses the current observation directly with no imposed delay. The dataset has one 480 × 480 camera view and four-dimensional state and action vectors. We evaluate 50 episodes per task with the same episode across policies and a 500-step cap. An episode counts as a suc-cess if the environment raises its task-success flag within this limit. Easy, Medium, Hard, and Very Hard contain 28, 11, 6, and 5 tasks, contributing 1,400, 550, 300, and 250 episodes, respectively. D.4. Action continuity and aggregation 

On LIBERO, we use the action step change δt defined in Eq. (12) of the main paper.
