## Abstract 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models generate robot ac-tions from visual observations and language instructions in real-world environments, making malicious model tampering a potential threat to physical safety. We study whether object-triggered backdoors can be implanted through a small, fixed set of parameter bit flips without retraining. The central challenge is to make persistent weight modifications dis-rupt trigger-present behavior while preserving performance on non-trigger inputs. We propose FlipVLA , a two-stage selector–amplifier method that exploits existing visual fea-tures within an FC–GELU–FC structure. The selector con-structs a trigger-dependent response in a selected channel, using GELU to attenuate background activity. The amplifier modifies a connected downstream weight to convert this re-sponse into an action-relevant perturbation. Channel screen-ing and discrete bit search construct both components under a limited modification budget. Experiments on OpenVLA-OFT and GR00T N1.7 across four LIBERO suites achieve attack success rates of 90.22%–100%, with task success decreasing by at most 1.5 percentage points on clean scenes.

## 1. Introduction 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models map visual observa-tions and language instructions to robot actions, providing a unified framework for robotic manipulation [14, 39]. Be-cause their predictions directly guide physical execution, malicious tampering with these models can disrupt task com-pletion. Backdoor attacks pose a particularly concealed threat: a model behaves normally in scenes without a trigger but deviates from its intended behavior when a predefined trigger appears. Such conditional failures may remain unde-tected by evaluations that do not include the trigger. Existing backdoor attacks commonly rely on training data poisoning or malicious fine-tuning to establish an as-sociation between a trigger and abnormal behavior [36, 38]. These approaches require access to the training data or opti-mization process. Bit-flip attacks (BFAs) expose a different attack surface by modifying the binary representation of de-ployed model parameters without retraining [28]. Hardware fault-injection techniques such as Rowhammer [15, 19] can induce memory bit flips, motivating the study of deployed model weights as an attack surface. In this work, we investi-gate object-triggered backdoor injection into VLA models 1arXiv:2611.33810v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 through a small, fixed set of parameter bit flips. Our goal is to disrupt task execution when a designated trigger ob-ject is present while preserving performance in its absence, including scenes containing distractor objects. Achieving this goal under a limited bit budget is challeng-ing. The same modified weights process both trigger-present and trigger-absent inputs: one bit flip that disrupts triggered behavior may also damage normal operation. The large pa-rameter space makes suitable modifications difficult to locate, while bit flips induce discrete changes in parameter values, further complicating the search for suitable modifications. This motivates investigating whether such conditional be-havior can be constructed by exploiting information already present in the model. We view backdoor implantation in this setting as extract-ing trigger information and selectively controlling its influ-ence on action generation. This perspective leads to two questions: First , can existing visual representations provide a small set of discriminative features for detecting the trig-ger object? Second , can weight modifications amplify the influence of this signal on action outputs while limiting their effects on normal behavior? Our analyses in Section 3 show that a few channels en-code trigger presence, selected early visual perturbations can disrupt tasks while the suppressing individual projector channels causes small deviations on clean inputs. These find-ings motivate FlipVLA (Figure 1), a selector–amplifier con-struction within an existing FC–GELU–FC structure which is commonly used in modern Transformer architectures. The selector modifies the first FC layer to activate trigger-associated tokens while driving background responses neg-ative for attenuation by GELU. With the selector frozen, the amplifier modifies a connected weight in the second FC layer to increase action deviations on trigger-present inputs. Channel screening and discrete bit search implement both stages within the bit budget. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first demon-stration of object-triggered backdoor attacks on VLA mod-els through a limited number of parameter bit flips, without retraining and while largely preserving non-trigger perfor-mance. • We propose FlipVLA, a selector–amplifier construction that uses an existing FC–GELU–FC structure to form a trigger-dependent response and then amplify it to disrupt task execution. Channel screening and discrete bit search implement both stages within a limited budget. • Experiments on OpenVLA-OFT and GR00T N1.7 across four LIBERO suites achieve attack success rates of 90.22%–100%, with clean-task success decreasing by at most 1.5 percentage points. 2.1. Threat model 

Attacker’s goal . The attacker aims to cause untargeted task failures when a predefined, task-irrelevant trigger object is visible, while preserving the original model’s performance on clean and distractor scenes. 

Attacker’s capabilities. We assume a white-box attacker with access to the model architecture, pretrained parameters, intermediate activations, and action outputs. The attacker can collect a limited set of clean, trigger-present, and distrac-tor observations for offline bit search, flip selected parameter bits under a fixed budget, and place the trigger object within 2(a) Clean (b) Red mug (c) Blue triangular pyramid (d) White sphere Figure 3. ## 3. Motivation 

We examine whether existing visual representations provide a compact trigger signal and whether this signal can influence actions while limiting interference on non-trigger inputs. All analyses use the original OpenVLA-OFT model with its parameters frozen. 

Observation 1. Trigger-object presence can be detected from a few activation channels. 

We perform sparse linear probing across layers for three target objects (Figure 3), comparing the minimum number of channels required to achieve the same detection perfor-mance(Figure 4). Several projector layers and early Llama layers support detection using only a few channels, whereas deeper layers generally require more. This pattern holds across all three objects. These sparse readouts motivate reusing existing visual features to construct a compact trigger response. Compared with attention blocks, the projector has a simpler structure consisting mainly of fully connected layers and GELU acti-vations(Figure 2). This makes the effects of bit flips on local activations easier to analyze and control.

Limiting non-trigger interference. We next examine how suppressing a single projector channel affects action predic-tions on clean inputs. We independently zero each of the 4,096 post-GELU channels following the projector’s FC2 layer and measure action MAE over clean inputs. Design implications. The observations suggest a concrete in-tervention site within the projector. Sparse probing motivates reusing its existing features to construct a trigger-dependent response. The channel-suppression results suggest that atten-uating a selected post-GELU channel can incur only a small cost to clean action outputs. Meanwhile, the projector’s final FC3 layer directly produces the visual representations enter-ing Llama block 0, where our injection experiments identify action-sensitive locations. Together, these findings motivate constructing a conditional response in FC2, attenuating non-trigger activity through GELU, and modifying a connected FC3 weight to influence downstream actions.

4.3. Amplifier construction 

Guided by Observation 2, the amplifier converts the selec-tor’s high responses into large perturbations in the FC3 out-put. FlipVLA’s selectivity-oriented design explicitly sepa-rates trigger discrimination from perturbation amplification, assigning trigger activation and background suppression to the selector. With the selector frozen, amplifier optimization therefore maximizes mean action MAE only on the positive set P, without additional MAE objectives or constraints on clean or distractor inputs. We search for an amplifier in a single FC3 weight connected to channel j. Multiple bits within this weight may be flipped under the budget, while all other parameters remain fixed. We select the weight and bit flips that maximize mean action MAE on the positive set P. For candidate FC3 weights W , this is equivalent to minimizing: 

Lamplifier (W ) = −Ex∼P [MAE( x; W )] . (12)

5.2. Main results 

Table 2 and Table 3 show strong attack effectiveness and selectivity across models, task suites, and bit-flip baselines, while largely preserving clean performance. 

Across models and task suites. Table 2 reports results for OpenVLA-OFT and GR00T N1.7 on LIBERO-Spatial, Goal, Object, and Long. Distractor SRs also remain close to their original levels, with a maximum decrease of 6.00 percentage points. These results support the applicability of our method across the tested models and task suites. Distractor SRs decrease by at most 6.00 percentage points relative to the original models under the same distractor conditions. On LIBERO-10 (Long), both models exhibit lower SRs in distractor-only scenes than in clean scenes, even before FlipVLA is applied. This baseline gap may reflect the original policies’ sensitivity to distractor placement.

## 6. Ablation 

6.1. Perturbation propagation 

To trace how the selector–amplifier perturbation reaches the action head, we conduct activation restoration experiments on 500 trigger-present validation observations. Inputs follow the construction in Section 4.1. 7We evaluate each intervention independently, restoring se-lected activations to those computed by the original model on the same input. We then recompute attention and all subsequent layers. Restoring keys reduces action MAE from 0.21760 to 0.00255, whereas restoring values yields a deviation of 0.10175. This identifies altered visual keys, which deter-mine how other tokens attend to visual content, as a critical component of the perturbation mechanism. At the output of the block 0, we retain the modified hidden states only at text positions and restore all other positions to their original states. This retains an action MAE of 0.21737. Conversely, restoring only the text states while retaining all other modified states reduces MAE to 0.00458. Retaining only the modified visual states yields an MAE of 0.00288. These results show that, after the first block, text representations carry much of the effect that subsequently reaches the action head, connecting the early visual perturbation to the final action deviation.

## 7. Related Work 

Bit-flip attacks modify deployed model parameters with-out retraining [28], inducing degradation [11, 37], targeted errors [1, 30], or backdoor behavior [6, 18, 29] in neural networks. Hardware studies [9, 15, 32], including Rowham-mer on GPU memory [12, 19, 20, 33], motivate this attack surface. Related work on LLMs and agents extends these concerns to generation [21, 35], safety alignment [7], and tool use [34]. 8VLA research improves robot control through large-scale training [5, 8, 10, 14, 25, 39], diverse action rep-resentations [3, 4, 16, 23, 26, 27], and efficient adapta-tion [2, 13, 24, 31]. Benchmarks support systematic evalu-ation [17, 22]. Existing VLA backdoors rely on poisoned data or malicious fine-tuning to induce trigger-dependent behavior [36, 38]. 

## 8. Conclusion 

FlipVLA implants object-triggered backdoors into VLA models without retraining through a selector–amplifier con-struction using limited parameter bit flips. Across two mod-els and four LIBERO suites, it achieves 90.22%–100% ASR with at most a 1.5 percentage point decrease in clean-task success. ## A. Additional experimental details 

Attack settings. We use a red mug as the trigger throughout the main experiments across both models and all four suites. Additional trigger types are evaluated in Section C. Attacks are constructed separately for each model–suite pair; these experiments assess the applicability of FlipVLA across the tested settings. 

Attack-construction data. For the main OpenVLA-OFT experiment on LIBERO-Spatial, we partition the attack-construction data by source demonstration within each task. We use ten demonstrations per task for optimization and five disjoint demonstrations for validation, sampling obser-vations at 0%, 33%, and 66% of each trajectory. Across ten tasks, this yields 300 optimization groups and 150 vali-dation groups. Each group contains one clean observation, 24 trigger-present variants, and 63 distractor-only variants. All observations and variants derived from a demonstration remain in the same partition. The optimization and vali-dation sets also use separate insertion-position pools. The validation set supports host-channel selection. 

Optimization objectives. The selector promotes trigger activation while suppressing background responses across clean, trigger-present, and distractor-only inputs. With the selector frozen, amplifier optimization maximizes mean ac-tion MAE on the positive set alone, without additional MAE objectives or constraints on clean or distractor inputs. This division assigns trigger discrimination and background sup-pression to the selector, while the amplifier increases the action deviation induced by its responses. 

Paired rollout evaluation. We evaluate the original and bit-flipped policies on paired scenarios under clean, trigger-present, and distractor-only conditions. Each policy pair uses the same task instruction, initial state, and inserted-object configuration.
