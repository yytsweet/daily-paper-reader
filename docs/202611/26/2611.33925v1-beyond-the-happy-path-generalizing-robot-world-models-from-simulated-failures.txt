Abstract
Robot world models are increasingly used to evaluate and
plan policies, yet robot training data are dominated by suc-
cessful demonstrations. Off the happy path, when a grasp is
misaligned or mistimed and the object is pushed, tilted, or
slips, their predictions break down. Across five pretrained
action-conditioned models, object-motion error is at most
10.4% lower than that of a static baseline that copies the
first frame, and two models are worse than this baseline. We
present WorldReforge, which teaches world models what
happens when manipulation goes wrong. We construct the
Reforge dataset of 62k simulated trajectories by perturb-
ing reference grasps in position, orientation, and closure
timing, recording each resulting push, slip, or failed pickup
with ground-truth object pose, contact, and surface points.
WorldReforge turns this ground truth into two training sig-

Although trained only in simulation, it also improves recon-

how objects respond, and holding teaches how a grasped
state persists. Our code, data, and models are available at

1. Introduction
Action-conditioned world models increasingly replace real-
world rollouts for policy evaluation, data generation, and
planning [9, 13, 43, 56]. Their value depends on predicting
how different actions produce different outcomes. Manipu-
lation is where this requirement is hardest to meet. A grasp
shifted by a centimeter or closed too early does not simply
fail. The object is pushed, tilted, or slips from the fingers.
Yet their robot training data are dominated by successful
demonstrations [27, 39], so this branch of physics is rarely
observed.
We find that this happy-path bias is severe. On our sim-
ulated evaluation, five pretrained models from three fami-
lies [13, 29, 37] achieve object-motion error at most 10.4%
lower than a static baseline that copies the first frame, and
two are worse (Tab. 1). Their predictions keep objects near
the initial pose even when the recorded object is pushed or
rotated (Fig. 4). Such predictions risk overrating the policies
being evaluated [41, 51].
Recent work fine-tunes on failures obtained by perturbing
or executing actions [28, 41, 47, 51]. These methods keep
the original generative objective [51], supervise outcomes
through progress or reward signals [41, 47], or learn failure
dynamics without ground-truth failure videos [47]. Two
limitations remain. First, contact dynamics occupy a small
fraction of each frame, so a global reconstruction loss gives
them little weight. Second, outcome-level signals indicate
whether an interaction failed, not how the object moved.
Simulation provides what real robot logs lack: failures can
be produced at controlled offsets and timings, safely and
at scale, each with exact object pose, contact, and surface
geometry.
We present WorldReforge, a post-training framework
that exploits this ground truth (Fig. 1). The Reforge dataset
contains 62k simulated trajectories over 96 objects, gener-
ated by perturbing successful reference grasps in translation,
rotation, and closure timing. Trajectories link execution
variations to observed outcomes, including contact forma-
tion and loss, pushing, tilting, slipping, and whether a grasp
is established and maintained. These labels support con-
trolled training compositions. Reforge-Real provides 300
real failure trajectories for evaluation. WorldReforge adds
two objectives to the generative loss. Interaction Alignment
(IA) reconstructs object and contact regions with a dedi-
cated, area-normalized signal. Surface Correspondence
(SC) requires intermediate backbone features to locate the
same physical surface points across frames, supervised by
simulator-projected trajectories. Neither adds inference-time
parameters.
Post-training on Reforge alone reduces object error by
about a quarter for Cosmos3 Nano and LingBot-VA, and IA
and SC further lower or match every motion error across
all three model families. On Cosmos3 Nano, WorldReforge
reduces object ADE by 28.2% overall and by 35.0% on
objects unseen during post-training, and it predicts the di-
vergence between two actions’ outcomes with 43.3% lower
error. Trained only in simulation, it improves PSNR by
1.04 dB on DROID and 0.68 dB on real failure videos. Con-
trolled data studies reveal a division of labor. Adding failures
in which the gripper closes without securing the object re-
duces object error on such contacts by 18.0%, while success-
ful holding remains the best teacher for keeping a grasped
object stable.
Our contributions are:
· A diagnosis that pretrained action-conditioned world mod-
els barely outperform a static predictor under failed con-
tact, and the Reforge dataset, combining 62k simulated
trajectories with dense physical ground truth, 300 real
failure trajectories, and a paired-action protocol for mea-
suring response differences.
· WorldReforge, which converts simulator ground truth into
interaction-region reconstruction and cross-frame surface
correspondence objectives without inference-time cost.
· Evidence that simulated failures improve object and grip-
per prediction across model families, transfer to unseen
objects and real-world video, and play a role complemen-
tary to successful experience.
2. Related Work
2.1. Robot World Models
Early action-conditioned video predictors model pixel mo-
tion [12]; Visual Foresight uses predictions for control [11],
and SV2P models uncertain futures [1]. World Models
learns compressed dynamics [16, 32, 33, 52], while PlaNet
and DreamerV3 use latent rollouts for planning and pol-
icy learning [17, 18, 44].
Genie learns controllable la-
tent actions from unlabeled video [3], RoboDreamer com-
poses predictions from language primitives [62], and Dif-
fusion Forcing combines causal generation with per-token
noise levels [4]. UniSim learns interactive visual simula-
tion [53]; Cosmos3 integrates multimodal generation and
action tasks [37, 38]; DreamDojo transfers knowledge from
human videos [13]; and LingBot-VA jointly models video
and actions [29]. IRASim uses frame-level action condition-
ing [63], and Ctrl-World combines multi-view generation
with pose-conditioned memory [15]. WorldReforge com-
bines successful and failed experience for contact-driven
object prediction.
2.2. Post-training for Physical Alignment
Real-world interaction data and demonstration-based learn-
ing support robot manipulation [31, 34, 35, 39, 48, 49, 61].
RoboCasa and robosuite support simulated manipulation [36,
64], while Kubric provides synthetic videos with dense ge-
ometric annotations [14]. AHA constructs failure exam-
2


ples for detection and reasoning [10]. FACT and CureWM
learn from executed failures [41, 51]; DreamTrue and HF-
WM use counterfactual or reward-guided training [28, 47];
WorldSync aligns generation with actions [5]. Feature align-
ment complements training: REPA aligns denoising fea-
tures with pretrained visual representations [55], VideoREPA
aligns spatiotemporal token relations [58], and PhysisForc-
ing aligns feature trajectories and semantic relations [57].
ABot-PhysWorld uses decoupled discriminative feedback
for preference optimization [6]. WorldReforge uses physical
ground truth for local reconstruction and surface correspon-
dence.
2.3. Evaluating Video and Interaction
Physion evaluates physical prediction in controlled scenar-
ios [2]; WorldOracle compares predictions with real execu-
tions [9]; WorldFailBench evaluates failure causes, stages,
and boundaries [59]. Forecasting research motivates care-
ful metric comparisons [24], and TAP-Vid provides point-
tracking protocols [7]. RAFT estimates optical flow [46];
PIPs tracks individual points through occlusion [19]; TAPIR
refines point trajectories [8]; and CoTracker jointly models
tracks, with CoTracker3 scaling supervision through pseudo-
labeling [25, 26, 60]. VBench and VideoScore assess video
quality across multiple dimensions [20, 23]. EWMBench
evaluates scene, motion, and semantic quality [56], while
WorldArena connects perception to data generation, policy
evaluation, and planning [43]. Framewise appearance alone
does not establish action-dependent physical accuracy. We
separately evaluate object motion, gripper motion, and video
quality, alongside paired-action response differences.

3.2. The Reforge dataset
3.2.1. Scale and Hierarchical Structure
The Reforge dataset integrates diverse interaction experi-
ence with synchronized physical ground truth. In simulation,
we construct successful reference executions using Robo-
Lab [54] and cuRobo [45], then perturb end-effector trans-
lation, rotation, and gripper closure timing. The resulting
successful and failed interactions are recorded with synchro-
nized RGB video, robot motion, and physical states, linking
execution conditions to observed responses. Figure 2 sum-
marizes this hierarchy.
The dataset comprises 62,425 trajectories across 106 ob-
ject assets. Its simulated component contains 62,125 tra-
jectories from 96 assets, including 13,577 failure-related
trajectories. Reforge-Real contributes 300 real-world failure
trajectories across 10 objects, while Reforge-Eval denotes
the simulated evaluation component.
Hierarchical inputs. Inputs distinguish physical context—
object attributes, initial poses, and scene configurations—
from execution variations. In the simulated component, exe-
cution labels describe end-effector translation (51,964 trajec-
tories), rotation (5,720), and gripper closure timing (4,356),
relating robot actions to the conditions under which objects
respond.
Hierarchical outputs. Outputs separate time-aligned con-
tact and object-motion events from grasp outcomes. Event
labels record contact formation and loss, object translation,

3.2.2. Organizing Interaction Experience
We organize interaction segments by execution variation and
response type. Successful grasping and stable holding pro-
vide examples of sustained control, whereas failed attempts
reveal contact-induced object motion and are grouped by
measured gripper state (contracted or open). Because the
same execution variation can yield different outcomes, we
record execution and response labels separately, retaining
the associated object motion and contact events in each seg-
ment. Subsequent experiments investigate the complemen-
tary value of combining successful reference experience with
diverse failed contact.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We post-train on 25.6k interaction segments
from the Reforge dataset, nearly 50% drawn from failed
trajectories. Evaluation covers 1,500 interaction clips from
Reforge-Eval, DROID [27], and Reforge-Real. Training and
Reforge-Eval share some object assets but no trajectories or
initial states. Real-world videos are used only for evaluation.
Baselines. We evaluate five backbones from three model
families. Cosmos3 [37, 38] unifies visual, language, and
action modeling; we use Cosmos3 Edge, Cosmos3 Nano,
and Cosmos3 Super.
DreamDojo [13] transfers interac-
tion knowledge from large-scale egocentric human video to
robots through continuous latent actions. LingBot-VA [29]
uses autoregressive diffusion to jointly model future frames
and robot control with shared video and action representa-
tions.

shoulder, and right-shoulder views. CoTracker [26] tracks
first-frame points in each generated view; we compare these

16. ADE and FDE measure average and final-frame position

5. Analysis
We analyze how execution coverage, complementary suc-
cessful and failed experience, action sensitivity, and physical
supervision shape interaction prediction.
5.1. Execution Coverage
Translation, rotation, and closure timing expose different
aspects of object–gripper interaction. By varying their pro-
portions in Tab. 4, we examine how these experiences con-
tribute to object and gripper prediction, both within and
across execution types. Each execution type is evaluated on
the corresponding 200-video subset of Reforge-Eval.
Object-response prediction. Combining translation and
rotation gives the lowest object ADE under translation and
rotation changes and across Reforge-Eval (7.25 pixels). It
also achieves the highest PSNR on Reforge-Eval and both
real domains. The rotation and closure recipe instead gives
the lowest object ADE under closure changes, demonstrating
the distinct value of different execution experiences.
Complementary execution experience. Including closure
alongside translation and rotation yields the lowest gripper
ADE across all execution conditions, reaching 2.68 pixels
overall. Together with the object-response results, this sup-
ports emphasizing translation and rotation while retaining
closure experience for accurate gripper dynamics. These
findings highlight the different contributions of execution
types to object and gripper motion prediction.

6. Conclusion
WorldReforge strengthens action-conditioned world models
through complementary interaction experience and physical
supervision. The Reforge dataset connects execution varia-
tions to physical outcomes, while Interaction Alignment and
Surface Correspondence focus learning on contact regions
and cross-frame surface motion. This connects global video
reconstruction with the dynamics of physical interaction.
8


Our results show that failed contact enriches object-response
prediction and successful holding supports state persistence.
These complementary signals improve interaction prediction,
real-world video reconstruction, and sensitivity to action
inputs. Future work will extend this framework to longer-
horizon manipulation and study how consequence prediction
can support closed-loop robot control.

A.2. Reference Executions and Perturbations
We construct successful reference executions using Robo-
Lab [54] for robot simulation and cuRobo [45] for collision-
aware motion planning. Perturbing end-effector translation,
rotation, and gripper closure timing produces diverse con-

records synchronized intervention commands, robot motion,
and object states, linking action variations to their observed
responses.
Each intervention is specified by its reference frame, ex-

Translation primarily varies the horizontal end-effector po-

the vertical axis; translations and rotations in other spatial
directions are also included.
Object properties, initial poses, and scene configurations

variations, allowing the dataset to describe how the same

A.4. Successful Holding and Failed Contact
Successful holding and failed contact provide complemen-
tary interaction experience. Holding captures state persis-
tence after grasp establishment, whereas failed attempts re-
veal contact-driven object responses. Failed-contact seg-
ments are grouped into contracted contact and open contact
according to the measured gripper state. The composition
experiments use the normalized closure at the final frame
of each segment: values above 0.25 indicate contracted con-
tact, and the remaining values indicate open contact. This
grouping characterizes the contact configuration within a
segment.

B. Experimental Settings
B.1. Implementation Details
Our experiments cover five backbones from the Cosmos3,
DreamDojo, and LingBot-VA families [13, 29, 37]. Each
model predicts 16 future frames conditioned on the ini-
tial observation and a 16-step robot action sequence. Ac-
tions comprise end-effector translation, a six-dimensional
rotation representation, and gripper state.
Each action
component is normalized using training-data quantiles as
2(a−q.01)/ max(q.99 −q.01, 10−8)−1, where q.01 and q.99
denote the first and 99th percentiles.
Training and optimization. We conduct post-training ex-
periments on eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs, using a global
batch size of 64, BF16 computation, and FP32 optimizer
states. The VAE is frozen, while the generative backbone is
updated. We use AdamW with ϵ = 10−8 and zero weight
decay. The training seed is 62101. Cosmos3 and DreamDojo
use (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.99) with a gradient-clipping threshold
of 1.0; LingBot-VA uses (0.9, 0.95) and 2.0, respectively.
For Cosmos3 and DreamDojo, the learning rate warms up
to 10−6 over 100 steps and then decays linearly. LingBot-VA
warms up to 3 × 10−6 over 100 steps and maintains that rate
thereafter. Data-composition experiments use the initial por-
tion of the main training schedule. The added robot-action in-
put columns in DreamDojo are initialized to zero and trained.
In LingBot-VA, a trainable action_embedder encodes
input actions into features.
Physical supervision.
WorldReforge augments the base
generative objective with IA and SC, gradually introducing
the auxiliary losses early in training. Tab. S7 reports the
main-experiment loss weights and feature extraction loca-
tions.

while SC establishes cross-frame correspondences using
visible physical surface points. SC performs soft matching
directly on backbone features, without an additional projec-

B.3. Evaluation Data
Main-model comparisons and checkpoint analysis use the
same 1,500 evaluation videos: 1,200 from Reforge-Eval, 200
from DROID [27], and 100 from Reforge-Real. Reforge-
Eval comprises 200 segments for each of six conditions:
translation, rotation, closure timing, contracted contact, open
contact, and successful holding.
Based on whether an object appears in the post-training
data, the simulated evaluation contains 784 segments with
seen objects and 416 segments with unseen objects; the latter
span 9 objects. Of all simulated segments, 622 carry failure-
related annotations. Execution- and response-composition
analyses each use the 200 segments in the corresponding
category.
B.4. Motion Evaluation
Motion evaluation uses a 640 × 544 three-view canvas, with
the wrist view above the left and right external views. Query
points are determined by geometry visible in the initial frame
and shared across models. We use at most ten points each
for the object surface and gripper fingers: six in the wrist
view and two in each external view. The number of valid
points depends on geometry and visibility.
CoTracker3 [26] tracks queries separately in each gener-
ated view. The tracks are mapped to canvas coordinates and
compared with reference trajectories obtained by projecting
simulator geometry. CoTracker extracts motion from gener-
ated videos; SC training supervision in WorldReforge comes
from simulator ground truth.
Object and gripper ADE/FDE follow the definitions in the
main paper, are measured in pixels, and are averaged with
equal weight per video. ADE covers future frames 1–16,
while FDE uses frame 16; valid points are determined by
ground-truth visibility. The Static baseline holds each query
at its initial-frame position.
B.5. Reconstruction and Visual Quality
PSNR and SSIM [50] assess future-frame reconstruction
over the full three-view canvas. RGB values are normalized
to [0, 1], and per-video PSNR = −10 log10(MSE). SSIM
uses the color-image implementation in skimage, with data
range 1 and the default full-frame settings, averaged over
future frames. All metrics are aggregated with equal weight
per video; overall results include all evaluation videos across
the three domains.

C. Supplementary Quantitative Analysis
This section addresses three questions: which interaction
responses benefit from physical supervision, how simulated
experience transfers to real videos, and how these gains vary
with backbone scale and training progress.
Throughout the result tables, bold denotes the best result
and underlining, where used, the second best within each
metric and comparison group.
C.1. Interaction Conditions
Condition-wise comparisons clarify the role of physical su-
pervision in execution variations, failed contact, and suc-
cessful holding. Tab. S8 decomposes the main Cosmos3
Nano results into six conditions, evaluating object responses,
gripper motion, and video quality separately.
Compared with Reforge, WorldReforge achieves lower
object and gripper ADE and higher PSNR in all six condi-
tions, with object-ADE reductions ranging from 2.35% to
6.96%. The same physical-supervision objective improves
both motion responses during failed contact and state persis-
tence after successful grasping.
C.2. Successful and Failed Real-World Interactions
Prediction on real interactions reveals the transfer value
of simulated experience. Tab. S9 compares reconstruction
and visual quality across the three backbone families on
DROID’s successful and failed interactions.
For Cosmos3 Nano, WorldReforge improves PSNR over
Pretrained by 0.92 and 1.34 dB on the failure and suc-
cess groups, respectively, with physical supervision further
improving PSNR and SSIM over Reforge. LingBot-VA
shows the same trend. DreamDojo improves visible in-
tegrity, reaching pass rates of 98.57% and 96.67% on the
two groups. Together, the reconstruction and visual-quality
results demonstrate the transfer value of simulated experi-
ence: post-training on simulation helps predict both failed
attempts and successful operations in the real world.

C.5. Training Dynamics
Training dynamics characterize WorldReforge’s convergence
rate and the benefits of continued optimization. Tab. S13
compares Cosmos3 Nano at different training steps, with
step 0 corresponding to Pretrained; Fig. S2 shows the trends
across nine checkpoints.
WorldReforge reduces object ADE relative to Pretrained
by 22.44% within the first 1,000 steps, and the reduction
reaches 28.19% at 4,000 steps. The training curves show
rapid early gains followed by gradual convergence. Among
the nine evaluated checkpoints, the final model achieves the
best motion errors and reconstruction metrics in every do-
main. Post-training therefore improves interaction prediction
rapidly, while continued optimization further refines motion
details and real-world reconstruction.
C.6. Object Motion Magnitude and Contact Phase
Translation and rotation magnitudes are the maximum
ground-truth three-dimensional displacement and angular
deviation from the initial frame over future frames. We split
Reforge-Eval separately into low, medium, and high tertiles
of each magnitude.
In Tab. S14, WorldReforge attains the lowest object ADE
in all six magnitude groups. Relative to Reforge, it reduces
ADE by 7.80% in the low-translation group and 8.17% in the
low-rotation group. Physical supervision improves object-
motion prediction across translation and rotation magnitudes,
including the low-translation and low-rotation groups.
Contact-phase analysis relates these gains to the temporal
evolution of an interaction. Measured fingertip contact distin-
guishes three states: before the first contact, during contact,
and after contact when contact is currently absent. Errors
are averaged over the relevant frames within each segment,
then across segments. WorldReforge reduces object error
relative to Reforge by approximately 4%–5% in all three
states, indicating that improvements extend across contact
establishment and subsequent motion.

D. Paired-Action Responses
Both branches use the same sampling configuration. Re-
sponse differences are computed from RGB values normal-
ized to [0, 1] over future frames 1–16 of the full three-view
canvas. The initial conditioning frame is excluded, and re-
ported RDE values are multiplied by 103.
RDE measures relative responses induced by action
changes, while branch-level metrics measure the accuracy
of individual predictions. Differencing cancels biases shared
by both branches; joint evaluation therefore assesses both
relative changes and absolute predictions.
