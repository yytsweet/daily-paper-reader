Xuewu Lin, Jiahao Wang, Jiawei Li, Mengdi Li, Zhengmao Sun, Yiwei Jin, Hongyu Xie,
Zhengyang Feng, Kun Wu, Zihao Chen, Lianzhou Zhang, Lichao Huang, Zhizhong Su∗
Horizon Robotics HoloBrain Team
∗Corresponding author.
We present HoloBrain-0.5, a family of vision-language-action (VLA) models for manipulation across
heterogeneous embodiments. Our goal is to plan motion in a shared action space across embodiments,
using the robot state, kinematic structure, and calibrated camera observations as inputs. Building
on HoloBrain-0, we introduce three improvements.
First, we standardize joint axes, coordinate
frames, and tool-center points in each robot description (URDF), then encode its kinematic graph
in the policy. Second, we release HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous, a diffusion policy, and HoloBrain-0.5-
Discrete, which quantizes actions with residual finite scalar quantization (FSQ) and generates the
resulting codes by delayed autoregression. Both use the same 3D embodiment representation, which
accommodates arbitrary kinematic configurations. Third, we scale pretraining to more than 10,000
hours of real-robot, simulation, and human-manipulation data spanning 15 embodiment categories,
with calibrated camera geometry and canonicalized embodiment representations.
During pretraining, the discrete policy converges faster than the continuous policy and achieves a
higher final mean success rate across six closed-loop simulation settings (56.77% versus 49.71%).
On seven real-robot tasks, the discrete policy achieves a mean progress score of 87.76% without
task-specific post-training. After post-training, the continuous policy outperforms π0.5 on average
on both the seven-task benchmark (94.52% versus 86.25%) and an 11-task dual-arm benchmark
(79.07% versus 69.41%). The discrete-initialized continuous policy also transfers five tasks zero-shot
from Piper to Piper-X, scoring 63.28% on average without demonstrations of those tasks on Piper-X.
With target-robot demonstrations, the continuous policy reaches mean progress scores of 89.2% on a
single-arm Franka Panda and 92.94% on a mobile dual-arm platform. Although pretraining includes
no Unitree G1 data, post-training with a limited set of G1 demonstrations yields a mean progress
score of 70.24% across three humanoid tasks, compared with 11.00% for π0.5.

Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models ground visual observations and language instructions in executable
robot actions, providing a scalable route toward general-purpose manipulation. Early large-scale systems
established the value of vision-language pretraining and heterogeneous robot data [1, 2, 3], while recent
models have improved action generation, open-world generalization, and cross-embodiment learning [4, 5, 6,
7, 8, 9].
A central challenge is that robot datasets differ not only in visual appearance and task distribution, but
also in joint count, kinematic structure, camera configuration, and action conventions. Arbitrary coordi-
nate choices in robot descriptions further obscure the relationship between an action value and its physical
effect. HoloBrain-0 addressed part of this problem with a joint-centric architecture and explicit forward-
kinematics features [10]. HoloBrain-0.5 extends this design with a canonical embodiment representation and
two complementary action formulations. An overview is shown in Figure 1.

We introduce URDF standardization to remove embodiment-specific coordinate conventions before policy
learning. Revolute joints are reparameterized about their local z-axes, prismatic joints translate along the
positive local z-axis, and tool-center-point (TCP) frames point outward along z.
HoloBrain-0.5 provides two model variants under a largely shared architecture. HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous
models continuous action chunks with diffusion, whereas HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete applies finite scalar quanti-
zation (FSQ) [11] in successive residual stages and multidimensional delayed autoregressive generation. The
discrete formulation exposes an explicit token distribution and converges faster in our pretraining experi-
ments. We pretrain both variants on more than 10,000 hours of data across 15 embodiment categories.
We track closed-loop performance at intermediate pretraining checkpoints using six simulation settings from
three simulators, with a total of 21,430 evaluation episodes per model checkpoint across the six settings.
We additionally evaluate out-of-the-box behavior, real-robot post-training, zero-shot task transfer between
seen embodiments, and adaptation across substantially different robot morphologies. The first two real-robot
benchmarks contain 18 tasks in total, while the additional morphology-adaptation suite spans 11 tasks across
three further embodiments.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a canonical 3D embodiment representation and a shared action space based on standard-
ized URDFs. Both are defined on the kinematic graph rather than a fixed set of joints.
• We release two policies built on this representation: the discrete policy converges faster during pre-
training, while the continuous policy performs better after post-training.
• In real-robot trials, HoloBrain-0.5 improves on HoloBrain-0 in closed-loop performance and performs
complex dexterous tasks. It achieves 63.28% mean progress on tasks transferred zero-shot to a target
robot and, with target-robot demonstrations, adapts to single-arm, humanoid, and mobile platforms.

Related Work
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Models
RT-1 and RT-2 established scalable vision-language-conditioned robot control and transfer from web-scale
representations [1, 2]. Open X-Embodiment [3], Octo [12], and OpenVLA [13] subsequently demonstrated the
value of heterogeneous multi-robot pretraining. Recent systems pair large vision-language backbones with
dedicated action modules: RDT-1B [14], π0 [4], CogACT [15], and GR00T N1 [16] use diffusion- or flow-based
generation, while π0.5 [5] and π0.7 [6] emphasize heterogeneous co-training and contextual conditioning.
Cross-embodiment scaling has also been explored through embodiment prompts or learned action repre-
sentations in X-VLA [17], LingBot-VLA [9], Wall-OSS-0.5 [7], and G0.5 [8]. In parallel, RoboMamba [18],
TinyVLA [19], NORA [20], SmolVLA [21], and VLA-Adapter [22] study more eﬀicient VLA architectures.
CEI [23] transfers demonstrations by aligning trajectories across functionally similar end effectors in 3D.
HoloBrain-0.5 instead standardizes joint axes, coordinate frames, and end-effector conventions so that demon-
strations from different robots can share an action representation.
2.2
Action-Space Modeling
Action-space design determines how a policy represents temporal structure and multimodality. Continuous
policies use direct action chunks [24] or generative objectives such as diffusion and flow matching [25, 14, 4, 15,
16]. Discrete policies instead quantize actions and predict token sequences. OpenVLA [13] uses discretized
action tokens, FAST [26] compresses action chunks in the frequency domain, and SpatialVLA [27] and
G0.5 [8] introduce spatially or embodiment-aware tokenizations. FSQ [11] offers a codebook-free mechanism
for constructing compact discrete representations. VQ-BeT [28] learns a hierarchy of discrete action codes
through residual vector quantization.
Both HoloBrain-0.5 variants use the same canonical 3D joint graph to represent the embodiment and express
actions as temporal sequences of joint graphs. This graph provides a complete kinematic representation
of robot state and action, grounding both in the same 3D frame as calibrated visual observations. The
continuous variant models these sequences with diffusion. The discrete variant uses residual FSQ to learn
a discrete representation of the action prior from trajectories, then predicts the resulting codes by delayed
autoregression.

URDF Standardization
HoloBrain constructs its embodiment representation from a pruned URDF: it computes forward-kinematics
(FK) losses over the retained kinematic tree, uses the 6-DoF poses of selected links to construct the joint-
centric action space, and embeds the graph-hop distances among these links [10]. Consequently, URDF
frame conventions directly enter the learned representation. Source URDFs may assign different local axes to
physically equivalent joints, while manually selected representative links may be offset from the corresponding
joint origins. We standardize each embodiment using a common, joint-centered convention, as illustrated in

trajectories.
3.2
HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous
The continuous variant follows the joint-centric encoder-decoder design and diffusion objective of HoloBrain-
0 [10]. Unless stated otherwise, the input representation, forward-kinematics conditioning, action parame-
5


terization, diffusion training objective, and inference procedure remain unchanged. This section focuses on
the architectural modifications introduced in HoloBrain-0.5.
Backbone and model capacity. We replace the Qwen2.5-VL-3B backbone [29] with Qwen3.5-2B [30] and
increase the capacity of the action decoder. Specifically, the number of decoder layers is increased from 6 to
10, while the embedding dimension is increased from 256 to 384.
Parallel multimodal attention.
HoloBrain-0 applies image cross-attention, text cross-attention, and
temporal-joint self-attention sequentially. We find that this ordering can bias the decoder toward the last
processed modality. In particular, placing temporal-joint self-attention last encourages the policy to over-
rely on proprioception and become less responsive to visual observations. HoloBrain-0.5 therefore computes
the three attention branches in parallel and combines them with a query-dependent routing mechanism, as
shown in Figure 4.
This parallel design preserves modality-specific positional structures rather than imposing a shared positional
representation across all branches.

Reference images from task-level key frames. During pretraining, we randomly provide reference

are provided as reference images. Examples are shown in Figure 5.
Modified joint-relative position encoding. Joint self-attention relies on a joint-relative position matrix,
whose entries measure the kinematic separation between joint pairs. In HoloBrain-0, these entries are hop
counts on the kinematic graph: adjacent joints within an arm are one hop apart, and the two arms of a
dual-arm robot are connected at their bases by a single unit-hop edge. The two end effectors are therefore
separated by nearly the combined length of both chains, even though their actions may be strongly correlated.
HoloBrain-0.5 instead connects the two arm ends with a single unit-hop edge, so every cross-arm distance
becomes the shortest path from one joint to the end of its own arm, across this edge, and on to the other joint,
while the within-arm kinematic topology is preserved. The original and modified matrices are compared in

3.3
HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete
HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete uses the same canonical joint-centric representation and multimodal conditioning
interface as the continuous variant, but replaces direct action generation with categorical prediction. Training
is divided into two stages. We first learn a morphology-aware action tokenizer that compresses continuous
trajectories into discrete codes and reconstructs them without image or language input. We then freeze the
tokenizer and train the VLA policy to predict its codes from visual observations, language instructions, and
proprioceptive state. Because tokenizer parameters are shared and code indices come from a fixed alphabet,
every embodiment’s trajectories map into the same categorical output space even though the number of
modeled joints or links varies.

gripper scalar and the pose of its link. The trajectory is divided into T non-overlapping temporal patches.

Experiments
We track closed-loop performance throughout pretraining and evaluate the resulting policies before and after
task-specific post-training. Our real-robot evaluation spans five embodiments: Piper, Piper-X, a single-arm
Franka Panda, a mobile dual-arm platform, and a Unitree G1 humanoid.
We distinguish two forms of
cross-embodiment evaluation: transfer without demonstrations of the target task on the target robot, and
adaptation using a limited set of demonstrations from the target embodiment.
4.1
Closed-Loop Performance of Pretrained Models
We evaluate each pretrained checkpoint over 21,430 episodes across six simulation settings: three 50-task
RoboTwin settings (clean, randomized, and UR5-WSG) [45], LIBERO [41], LIBERO-Plus [48], and Robo-
Casa [42]. Figure 11 reports the unweighted mean of the six per-setting success rates.
After pretraining, HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous achieves a mean simulation success rate of 49.71%, while
HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete reaches 56.77%. The discrete variant reaches 47.34% at 50,000 steps and remains
above the continuous curve at the plotted checkpoints.
We further train an additional continuous variant by initializing its shared components with the parameters
of HoloBrain-0.5-Discrete and replacing its discrete action head with a diffusion head. We denote this model
HoloBrain-0.5-Continuous (D-Init.). This variant achieves higher success rates than the continuous baseline
at the plotted checkpoints, suggesting that discrete-action pretraining can provide a useful initialization for
continuous action generation.

Conclusion
We introduce HoloBrain-0.5, a family of VLA models that extends HoloBrain-0 in model architecture, pre-
training scale, and evaluation methodology. First, we further unify heterogeneous action spaces through
URDF standardization to support learning across embodiments with different joint configurations and kine-
matic structures. Second, we develop a discrete-action variant that exhibits faster convergence during pre-
training and achieves stronger out-of-the-box performance. Finally, we pretrain HoloBrain-0.5 on more than
10,000 hours of heterogeneous manipulation data and systematically evaluate its performance throughout
pretraining. HoloBrain-0.5 represents a step toward scalable, embodiment-aware generalist manipulation
policies. The results support the use of explicit kinematic canonicalization and complementary action-space
formulations for learning from diverse robotic data.
6
Future Work
HoloBrain-0.5 is currently an action-supervised VLA model designed primarily for reactive visuomotor con-
trol. We plan to extend its capabilities along four directions:
• Broader multi-task supervision. We will incorporate a more diverse collection of tasks into joint
training, including visual question answering with textual responses and 3D grounding. Such super-
vision may improve semantic understanding, spatial reasoning, and the connection between high-level
instructions and physical actions.
• Scaling up training data. We will further expand the scale of training data. HoloBrain’s current
use of egocentric human data is exploratory; we plan to substantially increase its proportion in the
training mixture and build a more balanced data pyramid.
• Temporal modeling and memory. HoloBrain-0.5 currently makes decisions from visual observa-
tions at the current time step. Future versions will incorporate memory mechanisms and temporal
pretraining, enabling the model to retain and use historical observations.
• Richer physical modalities. Vision, language, and action alone provide an incomplete representation
of physical interaction. We plan to incorporate additional sensory signals, including tactile observations
and joint-level force/torque feedback.

Residual Quantization Configuration
The quantizer follows the open-source residual FSQ implementation in vector-quantize-pytorch1, which
chains finite scalar quantization [11] stages residually. For each latent h = Hvt, a shared aﬀine projection
produces four quantization coordinates u ∈R4, soft-bounded as r0 = b ⊙tanh(u ⊘b) with b = 1 + (L −1)−1
and L = (8, 5, 5, 5), all elementwise. Stage q divides the residual by sq = L−q, quantizes each coordinate by
scalar FSQ with straight-through rounding, rescales the quantized contribution ecq = sq ⊙cq, and passes on
the residual rq+1 = rq −sg(ecq), with the subtracted contribution detached. The four level indices of a stage
are packed with the mixed-radix basis (1, 8, 40, 200) into a single index zvtq ∈{0, . . . , 999}, so K = 1000.
