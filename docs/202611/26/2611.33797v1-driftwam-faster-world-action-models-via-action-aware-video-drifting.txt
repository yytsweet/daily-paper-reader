## Abstract 

World-action models (WAMs) unify future video prediction and action generation for robotic control, but their reliance on iterative denoising incurs substantial inference costs. Dis-tilling these models into few-step generators offers a promis-ing route to efficient deployment, yet maintaining task perfor-mance under aggressive step reduction remains challenging. Existing methods rely on point-wise trajectory consistency distillation, which pursues high visual fidelity while leav-ing downstream action information unexploited in optimiz-ing video generation, and also lacks explicit distribution-level alignment. To address this, we propose DriftWAM ,a few-step WAM distillation framework that incorporates the actions induced by predicted videos into video learning. Specifically, DriftWAM integrates action-aware video drift-ing , which performs video distribution matching through a joint visual–action similarity kernel to steer sample evolution under the guidance of these induced actions, with action-guided video regularization , which leverages the compatible teacher–student matches identified by the joint kernel to backpropagate action-level gradients directly into the video generator. Evaluations on the RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO benchmarks, as well as in real-world robotic manipulation tasks, show that DriftWAM consistently outperforms prior consistency distillation methods across few-step settings at the same inference speed.

This visual foresight grounds decision-making in physical dynamics, enabling strong generalization across complex robotic manipulation tasks. Despite their effectiveness, WAMs incur substan-tial inference latency because future video synthesis and action prediction often rely on iterative diffusion or flow-matching sampling [5, 11, 15]. As a result, generating each control decision typically requires multiple sequential de-noising steps to produce high-dimensional video latents and the corresponding action sequence. This computational bur-den stands in sharp conflict with the real-time reactivity required for closed-loop robotic deployment. Few-step dis-tillation [1, 22, 25, 33, 34, 37] therefore offers a natural optimization strategy, aiming to compress multi-step denois-ing into one or a few steps to reduce inference latency while preserving the capabilities of the full multi-step model. 1arXiv:2611.33797v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 Existing efforts toward few-step WAM distillation typi-cally adapt consistency distillation [18, 25, 28] from visual synthesis to each modality branch. For example, Flash-WAM [1] shows that high-dimensional, redundant video la-tents and low-dimensional, precision-critical actions operate under different noise regimes, so applying the same visual consistency objective to both branches can lead to severe performance degradation. It therefore adopts an action con-sistency function whose gradient-scaling coefficient varies linearly with the noise level, while retaining the standard latent consistency model (LCM) [18] consistency function for the video branch. Despite this progress, such modality-aware consistency distillation adaptations only tailor separate objectives to each data modality, while their video distillation does not explic-itly consider how the generated videos affect downstream actions. This omission matters, as video and action exhibit an inherent functional asymmetry in WAMs. In other words, we judge a WAM by whether its actions accomplish the task, while its predicted videos serve as intermediate represen-tations that guide the generation of those actions. Given this video–action functional asymmetry, we argue that video learning in WAMs should not focus solely on visual fidelity. We further find that assessing the similarity between two videos from the perspective of visual appearance versus from the perspective of their impact on the actions leads to considerable disagreements (Fig. 1; see Appendix A). There-fore, we believe that beyond pursuing visual fidelity, video learning in WAMs should also leverage the actions induced by predicted videos. However, incorporating these actions should not compro-mise the original capability of generating visually faithful videos. Thus, for few-step WAM distillation, we need a suitable approach that preserves the ability to pursue visual fidelity as much as possible, while offering a low-cost and effective interface for incorporating action information. To this end, we draw inspiration from the drifting paradigm [6], which shifts iterative distributional refinement from infer-ence to training. Drifting fulfills our needs for two rea-sons: 1) it governs sample attraction and repulsion directly through pairwise similarity kernels, naturally accommodat-ing action-level signals; 2) it enables one-step inference and has shown strong promise in diffusion distillation [37] and world modeling [17] for high-fidelity image and video gen-eration. Moreover, we find that, as a distribution matching method, drifting brings the added benefit of achieving better alignment between the student’s and teacher’s conditional video distributions (see Sec. 5.3). Building on this foundation, we propose DriftWAM , a few-step WAM distillation framework that unites action-aware video drifting and action-guided video regularization .Specifically, action-aware video drifting combines visual feature distances with the discrepancies between the actions induced by predicted videos into a joint similarity kernel, which reshapes sample evolution in feature space. Further-more, to complement this kernel-weighted feature evolution with explicit supervision on induced-action discrepancies, we reuse the compatible teacher–student pairs selected by the joint kernel to construct an action-guided video regularizer, through which action-level gradients are backpropagated directly into the video generator. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: • Based on the inherent video–action functional asymmetry in WAMs and our finding of the misalignment between visual and induced-action similarities among candidate videos, we argue that few-step video distillation should account for the downstream actions induced by predicted videos alongside visual fidelity. • We develop DriftWAM , in which action-aware video drift-ing injects induced-action similarity into the drifting ker-nel to guide video distribution matching, while action-guided video regularization exploits the resulting compat-ible teacher–student pairs to directly supervise the video generator via action-level gradients. • Extensive evaluations on the RoboTwin 2.0 [4] and LIBERO [16] benchmarks, as well as in real-world robotic manipulation tasks, demonstrate that DriftWAM main-tains strong control performance even when inference is compressed into one or a few denoising steps, delivering higher task success rates than state-of-the-art consistency distillation under identical inference budgets. 

## 2. Related work 

2.1. World-action models 

Leveraging spatiotemporal priors from pretrained video gen-erators [7, 26, 29], world-action models (WAMs) unify fu-ture visual prediction and action generation via diffusion or flow matching [2, 14, 23]. Existing WAMs that synthesize explicit visual futures either denoise video and action tokens jointly [2, 10, 32] or follow a cascade formulation that con-ditions action generation on predicted video latents [14, 21]. To mitigate the latency of multi-step sampling [32, 35], prior works bypass test-time video generation [31, 35], predict futures in compact latent spaces [3], adopt lightweight back-bones with sparse tokens [13], or cache computation across inference chunks [38]. Orthogonal to architectural or test-time modifications, we accelerate cascade WAMs while pre-serving explicit video-to-action inference through few-step distillation.

## 3. Preliminaries 

3.1. Problem formulation 

We study few-step distillation of cascade WAMs, which generate robot actions conditioned on predicted visual fu-tures [14, 21]. Let c denote the available context, including visual observations, previous actions, and a task instruction. A model with parameters θ first samples a future video-latent sequence z given c and then generates an action sequence 

a conditioned on both z and c. For brevity, we refer to these video-latent sequences as videos. The resulting joint distribution factorizes as 

pθ (z, a | c) = pθ (z | c) pθ (a | z, c ). (1) The video branch provides an imagined evolution of the scene, while the action branch determines robot commands conditioned on this future prediction and the observed con-text. Both branches generate samples through iterative flow-based denoising with N V video steps and N A action steps. We denote this inference setting by N V v/N Aa (e.g ., 1v /1a 

for one step per branch). While a pretrained teacher with parameters θT achieves strong manipulation performance using multi-step sampling, directly reducing N V and N A at test time to accelerate inference severely degrades task per-formance. Our goal is to distill the multi-step teacher θT into a student model θ that preserves the teacher’s capabilities under a few-step inference budget.

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental setup 

Base model. We instantiate DriftWAM on LingBot-VA [14], a cascade WAM that first predicts future video and then conditions action generation on the predicted videos. On RoboTwin 2.0, we initialize the student from the consistency-distilled Flash-WAM checkpoint [1] by default, and further ablate initializing directly from the pretrained LingBot-VA teacher in Sec. 5.4 for a comparison of the two methods as standalone distillation frameworks. On LIBERO, since no Flash-WAM checkpoint is publicly available to date, we initialize the student directly from the LingBot-VA teacher. For training, we use M = K = 4 teacher and student candidates per context. On RoboTwin 2.0, train-ing takes about 24 hours on 4 NVIDIA A800 GPUs (see Appendix E for full training details). 

Benchmarks. We evaluate DriftWAM on two simulation benchmarks and a real-world robotic setup: (i) RoboTwin 2.0 [4], comprising 50 bimanual manipulation tasks across three horizon lengths (Horizon = 1 , 2, 3) under Clean and Randomized splits (100 rollouts per task per split), where the Randomized split perturbs scene clutter, lighting, back-ground textures, and tabletop heights; (ii) LIBERO [16], covering the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites (10 tasks each); and (iii) Real-world manipulation on a 6-DoF Ag-ileX Piper arm (with third-person and wrist-mounted RGB cameras) across three tasks covering precision placement, pick-and-place, and multi-stage stacking (20 rollouts per task under matched randomized object placements), where LingBot-VA is fine-tuned on 93 teleoperated demonstra-tions (40,852 frames) before distilling Flash-WAM and Drift-WAM. 

Evaluation metrics. Beyond task success rates, we mea-sure per-chunk inference latency in simulation on a sin-gle NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU to report speedup relative to the LingBot-VA teacher, and report replanning time for real-world evaluation. To verify that DriftWAM achieves distribution-level video refinement, we also evaluate the squared maximum mean discrepancy ( MMD 2) [9] with an LPIPS [36] perceptual kernel between teacher and stu-dent video distributions across 30 conditioning histories on RoboTwin 2.0 (see Appendix D for details).

5.2. Main results 

RoboTwin 2.0. Table 1 reports the results on RoboTwin 2.0. DriftWAM outperforms Flash-WAM across both step budgets at the same inference speedup (see Appendix F.4), with the performance margin widening substantially as in-ference is compressed from 1v /2a to one-step 1v /1a . This difference arises because a two-step action branch still re-tains capacity to iteratively refine noisy predictions, whereas single-step action generation has no room for iterative cor-rection and becomes highly sensitive to the quality of the predicted video, amplifying the benefit of stronger visual con-ditioning from DriftWAM. Further examining the breakdown across evaluation splits and task horizons shows that these gains are concentrated on the Randomized split and scale sharply with task horizon, reaching +10 .40 and +21 .40 

percentage points on Horizon = 2 and Horizon = 3 un-der 1v /1a . This pattern stems from how our action-aware kernel and regularizer guide video generation to focus on action-relevant regions, which improves robustness under domain randomization and mitigates visual-control errors from compounding across multi-stage tasks. 

LIBERO. Table 2 summarizes the results on LIBERO. Although we compare against the numbers reported in Flash-WAM [1] and initialize directly from the LingBot-VA teacher due to the absence of a released Flash-WAM checkpoint to date, the results exhibit a consistent trend with RoboTwin 2.0: DriftWAM outperforms Flash-WAM across both inference budgets, with a larger overall margin under the one-step (1v /1a ) setting. Reducing the action branch from two steps to one causes only a modest drop in DriftWAM’s average success, showing that the distilled student closely preserves the multi-step teacher’s capability even under the smallest tested inference budget.

5.3. Video distribution alignment analysis 

Table 3 evaluates conditional video distribution alignment on RoboTwin 2.0 (see Appendix D for details). Compared with the near-zero Teacher–Teacher reference, Flash-WAM exhibits a considerable distributional gap, showing that point-wise trajectory consistency alone is insufficient to closely align the conditional video distribution under one-step video generation. Replacing trajectory consistency with Vanilla Video Drifting cuts this discrepancy by more than half, and DriftWAM reduces it even further. Together with the con-trol improvements in Table 4, this result suggests that in-corporating action information into video drifting further improves both task performance and alignment with the teacher’s video distribution.

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented DriftWAM, a few-step distillation framework for cascade world-action models that addresses the video– action functional asymmetry overlooked by trajectory con-sistency distillation while mitigating conditional video distri-bution discrepancy. DriftWAM couples action-aware video drifting with action-guided video regularization. It evalu-ates candidate videos with the teacher’s action branch to incorporate action information into video distribution match-ing, while directly backpropagating action-level gradients through the resulting compatible teacher–student matched pairs. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO demon-strate that DriftWAM is effective both as a standalone distil-lation method and as a refinement over consistency-distilled checkpoints, yielding pronounced gains under one-step in-ference, visual perturbations, and long-horizon manipulation without additional test-time overhead. Real-world exper-iments on an AgileX Piper arm further show that, at the 

1v /2a budget, DriftWAM achieves the highest average suc-cess rate among the compared methods and retains 84 .4% of 8the teacher’s average success. However, while our formula-tion may also be applicable to unified joint-denoising WAMs, this work only validates its motivation and effectiveness on cascade WAMs, and multi-sample candidate generation still incurs extra training cost. Future work will therefore empiri-cally investigate unified WAMs and improve training-time sample efficiency.

E.3. Training configuration 

On RoboTwin 2.0, we initialize the student from Flash-WAM by default and from LingBot-VA in the initialization ablation. Training cost. On RoboTwin 2.0, we measure wall-clock training time under matched settings on four NVIDIA A800 GPUs. For the same 500 optimizer steps, Flash-WAM takes about 8 hours and DriftWAM takes about 24 hours. Starting directly from LingBot-VA, however, DriftWAM surpasses the Flash-WAM baseline in average success rate under both inference budgets after just 500 steps (Table 5 and Table 11). This optimization run is shorter than the estimated 33 hours for Flash-WAM’s 2,000-step budget [1], extrapolated from our measured throughput. Notably, DriftWAM also incurs a one-time cost of 127 hours on the same hardware to gener-ate the offline teacher video–action pairs, which are reused across training runs.

## F. Additional experiments 

F.1. Continued-training control under the 1v /1a set-ting 

On RoboTwin 2.0, our default DriftWAM student starts from the official Flash-WAM checkpoint and trains for 500 opti-mizer steps. To verify that the gains come from our objective rather than extra optimization, we continue training from the same checkpoint for 500 steps with the Flash-WAM ob-jective and evaluate all models under the one-step ( 1v /1a )setting on a shared 1,000-episode protocol (Table 9). Contin-uing Flash-WAM training yields no notable difference from the official checkpoint, suggesting that it has already largely converged under its original objective. Under the same ini-tialization and step budget, DriftWAM clearly outperforms the continued Flash-WAM run on both splits, confirming that its gains come from action-aware video drifting and regularization rather than additional training steps.
