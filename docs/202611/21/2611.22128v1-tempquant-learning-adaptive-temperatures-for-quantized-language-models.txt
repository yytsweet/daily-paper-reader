Abstract
Large language models are increasingly deployed at low precision for applications ranging from reasoning to
creative writing, yet quantization can degrade their performance. These applications often use different decoding
settings, such as the sampling temperature and top-p, which control the randomness of token selection. We study
how quantization interacts with these settings by examining how it distorts the model’s next-token probabilities,
which can change generated responses and task performance. Measuring entropy during generation across
deployed temperatures, we find that quantization warms the model on average, and that temperature matters much
more for the performance of a quantized model than for its full-precision counterpart. Because quantization and
temperature both shape the next-token distribution, we investigate how much the sampling settings can undo
what quantization changes, through our method TempQuant, which learns the quantized model’s temperature
and top-p to bring its next-token probabilities closer to the full-precision model’s. We learn these settings at two
granularities. The first is a single global setting per requested temperature, which we show finds nearly the same
temperatures that tuning on labeled answers would, without any labels. This matters in practice because model
cards typically recommend sampling a quantized model with the same settings as its full-precision counterpart,
while we find that different settings can substantially improve its accuracy. On 3-bit Qwen3-4B, for example,
global TempQuant raises MATH accuracy from 34% under the configuration recommended for Qwen’s quantized
releases to 51%. The second sets the temperature and top-p for every token. It is motivated by our finding that
tokens whose rankings flip under quantization tend to need warming, while tokens whose rankings are preserved
tend to need cooling, which one temperature cannot do at once. Finally, we show that when quantization degrades
accuracy substantially, as at 3 bits, TempQuant recovers much of it, and when degradation is lighter and there is
little accuracy to recover, it still brings the quantized model’s behavior closer to the full-precision model’s.

Introduction
At every step of autoregressive generation, large language models generate a probability distribution over the next token.
This probability distribution is a function of the prefix, which is the prompt together with any tokens generated so far.
Based on the chosen decoding method, we select a token from this distribution [43]. When sampling, the randomness of
token selection is controlled by the temperature τ, a user-chosen deployment setting. The chosen temperature depends
on the use case: higher temperatures generally lead to more diverse outputs, whereas lower temperatures concentrate
sampling on the most likely tokens. Changing the temperature changes the entropy of the distribution, that is, how
spread out it is.
Quantization is a separate form of compression, where we use fewer bits to represent the weights and, in some
configurations, activations of the large language model, so it can fit in less memory and run faster on supporting
hardware [6, 21]. Compressing this information introduces numerical error, so the next-token probability distributions

can diverge from those of the full-precision model. These distortions can also change the distribution’s entropy, affecting
the sampling randomness that the user controls through temperature.
Temperature scaling and quantization thus both change the model’s next-token distribution, and we analyze how the
two interact. How well a decoding setting works depends on the task and on factors such as quantization, and the best
results often require extensive tuning [43], which motivates learning decoding settings automatically [46]. We study
how much of quantization’s distortion of the next-token distribution can be corrected by adjusting temperature and
top-p, and whether doing so recovers accuracy.
We quantify the difference between the full-precision and quantized probability distributions through Kullback–Leibler
divergence [14]. We hypothesize that reducing this divergence can help preserve the full-precision model’s behavior.
This is motivated by Accuracy is Not All You Need, in which Dutta et al. [5] argue that quantization should preserve
the full-precision model’s behavior, and that similar aggregate benchmark accuracy does not establish behavioral
preservation, because correct-to-incorrect and incorrect-to-correct “flips” can cancel out. They motivate distance metrics
such as KL divergence to capture the changes that aggregate accuracy hides, and this divergence is what we reduce.
Minimizing divergence between probability distributions is not new in quantization: LLM-QAT updates quantized
model weights to minimize forward KL for fixed teacher targets [21]. However, quantization-aware training (QAT)
retrains the language-model weights, which can be expensive. We instead reduce this divergence with a lightweight
method applied after post-training quantization (PTQ). We modify two sampling settings, temperature and top-p,
rather than the model weights, and call this method TempQuant. We learn these settings at two granularities: global
TempQuant learns one temperature and top-p to deploy the quantized model at for each temperature requested of
the full-precision model, and per-step TempQuant chooses them at every decoding step. Global TempQuant is the
practical version that can be applied today, since it only changes the decoding settings. We also show why changing the
temperature at every token can recover even more of the quantization error, which motivates per-step TempQuant.

In practice, existing deployments usually sample a quantized model at the settings chosen for its full-precision counterpart.
Qwen, for example, recommends temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95 for the full-precision Qwen3 [35] and repeats the
same settings for its own 4-bit and GGUF releases [36, 37], and community quantizations down to 3 bits copy these
settings unchanged [29, 45]. We argue that this leaves much of the quantization error unrecovered, and that choosing
different sampling settings for the quantized model, whether at a global or a per-token granularity, can recover much of
it (Section 4.6).
2
Related Work
Adaptive decoding.
Several methods learn temperatures at a fine granularity: ATS per token for calibration [48],
TempNet per input for task performance [34], and ReSET per token for quantized reasoning models, with settings
tuned on labeled data [16]. AutoDeco predicts temperature and top-p at every step with two small heads [46], a design
we borrow, but trains them to raise the likelihood of reasoning traces that reach correct answers, so as to replace
hand-tuned decoding. TempQuant instead learns its settings to match the full-precision model, so as to undo the effect
of quantization.
Decoding-time fixes.
Lotfi et al. [22] measure the token-level KL between quantized and full-precision reasoning
models and reduce overthinking with a logit penalty on a curated set of markers. TempQuant also corrects the quantized
model at decoding time, but fits the standard temperature and top-p to the full-precision model. Other samplers target
repetition without a reference model [13, 2, 26, 9, 28], and Braverman et al. [3] correct entropy-rate drift with a single
temperature-like parameter, a close precedent for global TempQuant.
Temperature calibration and quantization.
DACA, Thermometer, and Dual-Align fit temperatures for confidence
calibration [23, 42, 24], while we fit them to keep a quantized model close to its full-precision version. Aggressive
quantization can lengthen chains of thought and cause repetition [22, 17, 20], and can flip preferences between candidate
tokens [47]. We use GPTQ [6], AWQ [19], and the MXFP4 format [41]. Appendix A discusses related work in more
detail.

Method
Matching the full-precision distribution.
Our goal is to bring the probability distribution the quantized model
emits at each step as close as possible to what the full-precision model emits under the user’s requested temperature
and top-p. We compare the two models after the same prefix. Temperature divides the logits before they are turned
into probabilities, and top-p keeps the most probable tokens until their cumulative probability reaches the cutoff, then
rescales them to sum to one [12].

Global TempQuant.
For each requested temperature, global TempQuant searches a grid of temperature and top-p
values for the pair with the lowest divergence on the training data (Appendix E.3). It is fit separately for each quantized
model, and is temperature scaling [7] with the full-precision model’s next-token distribution in place of the labels,
together with top-p. We also learn two smaller versions: global TempQuant (top-p only), which keeps the requested
temperature, and global TempQuant (T only), which keeps ρQ
t = .95. Global TempQuant adds no cost at deployment,
since it only changes the two numbers passed to the sampler.
Per-step TempQuant.
Per-step TempQuant chooses the setting at every step. At inference we no longer have the
full-precision model, so it has to choose from what the quantized model gives us. At each step, it looks at the quantized
logits after adjusting them for the requested temperature, and summarizes them in 42 features: the 32 largest logits,
normalized, and ten statistics that describe how spread out the distribution is, such as its entropy and the gaps between
the leading tokens. Two small MLP heads read these features and predict a correction κt to the requested temperature
and a new top-p cutoff. The sampler divides the original quantized logits by Tt = τκt and applies the predicted cutoff.

Experiments
We first analyze how quantization changes the next-token distribution (Sections 4.1–4.3), then evaluate both forms of
TempQuant (Sections 4.4–4.5), their accuracy (Section 4.6), and how well they preserve the full-precision model’s

We evaluate Qwen3-4B [51] quantized to 3-bit GPTQ (GPTQ3) and to native 4-bit MXFP4, at requested temperatures
0.3, 0.6, the setting Qwen recommends, and 1.0, with further models in Section 4.6. Appendix D gives the benchmarks,
data splits, and statistical tests.

depends on what quantization does to the token probabilities. Quantization does two different things to tokens (Figure 3).
We say a token’s rankings are preserved when the quantized model’s most likely next token is the same as BF16’s, and
flipped when it is not; less likely tokens may still change order. On most tokens, quantization keeps BF16’s most likely
token and only spreads the probabilities out. On these tokens, where the rankings are preserved, quantization acts like
heating, so they tend to be best matched by cooling to a lower temperature. On the rest, however, the rankings flip and
BF16’s preferred token now ranks lower. Here only a higher temperature gives it back probability, as in Figure 1b, so
raising the temperature tends to reduce the KL divergence (Figure 3b). These flipped tokens are a small share of tokens,
but each costs far more, so they carry most of the error under every divergence we use (Figure 3a), at every requested
temperature (Appendix Table 38).
A single temperature therefore has to trade off the two, since warming helps the flipped tokens and cooling helps the
preserved ones. Fitting global TempQuant only on preserved tokens shows this. Its temperature drops close to the
requested one and fits those tokens slightly better, but the error rises on flipped tokens and over all tokens (Appendix E.3).
A per-step temperature can instead correct each token on its own.
4.3
Choice of divergence
To see how handling this trade-off differently changes performance, we measure the quantization error with three
divergences that weigh the two kinds of token differently. TempQuant can be seen as a restricted form of knowledge
distillation [10], in which the full-precision model is the teacher, its quantized version is the student, and the student can
lower the divergence only through its sampling settings, without changing its weights. On-policy distillation [1] shows
that when a student cannot reproduce its teacher, the divergence decides which errors it accepts. Inspired by this, we
train with three divergences that fail in different ways (Appendix F.1).
Forward KL is large when the quantized model gives little probability to a token BF16 considers likely. A flipped token
therefore spikes forward KL, so forward KL prioritizes warming to correct the flipped tokens. Reverse KL, on the other
hand, is large when the quantized model puts probability where BF16 does not. Warming spreads probability onto tokens
BF16 gives little probability to, which spikes reverse KL, so reverse KL prefers cooling even for the flipped tokens. JSD
combines the two, weighs both errors, and lands in between, near the requested temperature. On a typical preserved
token every divergence favors cooling, so they part ways mainly on the flipped tokens, as Appendix B illustrates. This is
the familiar contrast between mass-covering and mode-seeking divergences.

4.4
Global TempQuant
BF16 accuracy stays nearly flat across our three temperatures (Table 2), but under 3-bit quantization the deployed
temperature decides both accuracy and the divergence from BF16 (Figure 4). We first show that the best temperature for
the quantized model differs from the full-precision one, as temperature tuning finds, and then that global TempQuant,
which does no sweep and only learns the setting that mimics BF16, finds nearly the same temperature. Native 4-bit
MXFP4 behaves similarly (Table 29). Global TempQuant turns top-p off at every requested temperature, which by itself
removes much of the forward KL, and the learned temperature removes more (Appendix Table 36).
TempQuant versus temperature tuning.
The usual way to choose a sampler setting is to tune it on labeled data:
generate and grade responses at every candidate setting and keep the most accurate one [43, 46]. Tuning GPTQ3 this
way on 200 MATH questions took about 7,800 graded responses and 170 million generated tokens (Figure 6, Appendix
Figure 12), and has to be repeated for every model and task. Global TempQuant needs no labels or grading, only the
full-precision model’s next-token probabilities on a few hundred unlabeled responses, and lands close to the setting
tuning finds. The best setting differs between models, quantization methods, and requested temperatures, so a fixed rule
such as sampling every quantized model at a higher temperature does not carry over (Appendix E.3).

Model providers have noticed that quantized models degrade and loop at
these temperatures. Qwen’s cards for its quantized models keep the temperature at 0.6 but add a presence penalty of
1.5 to stop endless repetition [36], the failure that costs GPTQ3 most of its accuracy. Global TempQuant performs
better than this recipe, and adding the penalty on top of global TempQuant helps a little further, so the two combine

4.5
Per-step TempQuant
Per-step TempQuant chooses the setting for every token to avoid the compromise of Section 4.2. To measure how much
of the quantization error this could remove, we test an oracle that knows BF16’s distribution at every token and picks the
quantized model’s temperature and top-p, from the grid global TempQuant searches, to minimize the divergence. The
oracle removes most of the divergence quantization adds, 84% of the forward KL at the recommended 0.6, almost all of
it through the temperature alone (Appendix Table 36), and sampling GPTQ3 with its settings brings GSM8K accuracy
close to BF16’s and lifts MATH well above TempQuant (Appendix F.5). Per-step TempQuant tries to capture this
headroom from the quantized model alone, so it has to infer from the quantized logits whether the top token changed
(examples in Appendix E.4).
4.6
Downstream accuracy
Table 2 measures accuracy on each policy’s own responses. At 0.3 and 0.6, TempQuant raises GSM8K, MATH, and
GPQA accuracy far above raw GPTQ3, lifting mean accuracy from 10% to 40% at 0.3. At 1.0, where raw GPTQ3
loops less, there is less to recover (ablations and strict grading in Appendix E.1). The correction carries over to 3-bit
Qwen3-8B and to Gemma 4 31B, a different family, raising Gemma’s MATH accuracy from 70% to 90%, but not
to R1-Distill-Llama-8B, which rarely runs into repetition (Appendix Table 12). Table 2 also reports how close each
policy’s generated text is to BF16’s with a closeness score: 100 minus the average gap between a policy and BF16 on
four statistics of its text, namely word overlap with BF16’s responses, how alike the policy’s own responses to the same
prompt are, repeated four-grams, and the share of responses that hit the length limit (details in Appendix E.7).
Of the three divergences, forward KL gives per-step TempQuant the highest accuracy, matching global TempQuant at
0.3 and 0.6 and exceeding it at 1.0 (Appendix Table 10). Global TempQuant adds no cost at deployment, and per-step
TempQuant adds time per token (Appendix G).
4.7
Does TempQuant preserve behavior and task performance?
Since similar accuracy can hide large changes in behavior [5], and matching a reference model need not improve task
performance [44], we report behavior alongside accuracy, using task-specific measures and the closeness score of
Section 4.6. The three divergences behave differently here, and the results separate into two cases: long reasoning under
heavy degradation, and shorter outputs or lighter degradation.

Short text and light degradation
On short summaries, GPTQ3 does not loop, so there is little accuracy to recover. Its main error is instead spreading

Limitations
Temperature and top-p cannot reorder tokens, so errors that flip rankings remain even for the oracle. The accuracy gains
are large only when the quantized model runs into repetition. None of the per-step controllers we trained preserves
every behavior we measure. We fit TempQuant on math text only, evaluate reasoning with thinking mode on, use a
single reference top-p of .95, and compare with Qwen’s presence penalty on one model. Global TempQuant adds no
cost at deployment, while our unoptimized per-step implementation adds 18–34% to the time per generated token
(Appendix G).

6
Conclusion
Quantized models run hotter, but most of the error sits on the few tokens whose ranking quantization flips, which tend to
need warming while the rest tend to need cooling. Global TempQuant finds without labels the setting that tuning on
answers would find, and recovers much of the accuracy lost by 3-bit models that fall into repetition. Per-step TempQuant,
which chooses the setting at every token from the quantized model alone, matches this at requested temperatures 0.3 and
0.6 and is more accurate at 1.0. The choice of divergence decides what is preserved: forward KL restores accuracy
when the model loops, while for a lightly degraded model reverse KL brings short text and JSD brings reasoning closer
to BF16’s. Overall, we show that much of the quantization error can be recovered through temperature and top-p, so
quantized models should be deployed at different settings from their full-precision counterparts.

A
Extended related work
Adaptive decoding.
Adaptive temperature scaling (ATS) and TempNet also learn temperatures at a fine granularity,
but for different reasons. ATS learns a temperature for each token to improve the model’s confidence calibration [48],
and TempNet learns to predict a temperature for each input to improve prediction and generalization across tasks [34].
ReSET also sets the temperature per token, and targets NVFP4-quantized reasoning models. It observes that quantization
adds sampling errors at confident tokens and concentrates probability too much at uncertain reasoning steps. It samples
high-entropy tokens at temperature 1.0 and the rest at a lower temperature, with an entropy threshold that adapts to the
current reasoning step, and calibrates the low temperature for each model and task on labeled held-out data [16]. It does
not learn a temperature predictor, and its goal is to balance precision and exploration during reasoning, rather than
to imitate the full-precision model. Our entropy-threshold baseline in Appendix E.3 is a simpler rule in the spirit of
ReSET, and not ReSET itself. AutoDeco is the closest work to ours architecturally, since it also learns temperature and
top-p at each generation step, with two small heads on the language model. It trains them with cross-entropy to raise the
probability of the next token in reasoning traces selected for correct final answers [46], and we borrow its two-head
design. TempQuant instead learns these settings so that the quantized model’s next-token probabilities match those of
its full-precision counterpart, and uses them to measure how much of the quantization error the sampler can remove.
Decoding-time fixes for degeneration.
Lotfi et al. [22] measure the token-level KL between quantized and full-
precision reasoning models, find that it is high at uncertain positions, where quantized models oversample overthinking
markers such as “wait”, and reduce this with a training-free logit penalty on a curated set of these markers. TempQuant
also corrects the quantized model at decoding time without retraining it, but fits the standard temperature and top-p to
the full-precision model, with no chosen set of tokens. More general samplers target repetition and incoherence without
a reference model: repetition penalties [13], Mirostat, which holds perplexity at a target [2], locally typical sampling
[26], truncation sampling [9], and min-p [28]. Repetition also reinforces itself once it starts [49], which is consistent
with how quickly a cold 3-bit model falls into loops. Braverman et al. [3] show that the entropy rate of generated text
drifts away from that of real text, and correct this with a single temperature-like parameter, a close precedent for global
TempQuant. We fit such a parameter against the full-precision model after quantization.
Temperature calibration.
Other work learns temperatures to calibrate a model’s confidence. Temperature scaling fits
one temperature on labeled data [7]. DACA fits a temperature with a reference-based forward KL so that a post-trained
model’s confidence matches its pre-trained model [23]. Thermometer predicts one temperature for a task by aggregating
unlabeled prompts [42], and Dual-Align combines output and layerwise supervision to fit a single temperature [24]. We
do not select temperature and top-p for confidence calibration, but to preserve the behavior of a quantized model.
Distillation.
Matching a teacher’s next-token distribution is the basis of knowledge distillation [10]. LLM-QAT
applies it to quantization, updating the quantized weights to minimize forward KL to the full-precision model [21], and
Agarwal et al. [1] study how the choice of divergence affects a student that cannot reproduce its teacher.
Temperature and generation behavior.
Temperature affects the diversity and coherence of generated text. Holtzman
et al. [12] show that low-temperature sampling can reduce diversity and increase repetitive loops in open-ended
generation. In a Llama-2 story-generation study, Peeperkorn et al. [31] find that higher temperature is only weakly
associated with novelty and more strongly associated with incoherence. For full-precision models, the temperature
between 0 and 1 has little effect on problem-solving accuracy [40].
Behavioral effects of quantization.
Quantization can change reasoning behavior as well as accuracy, and similar
aggregate accuracy does not establish that behavior is preserved [5]. Lotfi et al. [22] find that aggressive quantization
can lengthen chains of thought and increase overthinking errors, where a model reaches a correct intermediate answer
and then abandons it. In dialogue evaluations, Li et al. [17] document sentence-level repetition under 3-bit weight or
KV-cache quantization and repeated-token failures at 2 bits. The effects vary by configuration: Liu et al. [20] find
response lengths close to BF16 under several settings, while more aggressive quantization can lengthen outputs. In our

data, 3-bit responses are several times longer than full-precision ones, mostly because they run into repetition. We
measure response length and repetition alongside accuracy, using BF16 as our behavioral reference.
Quantization and token probabilities.
Wu et al. [47] find that low-bit quantization can shrink decision margins, the
logit differences between the first tokens of two candidate continuations, and introduce directional biases that reverse
model preferences. A margin describes a particular choice, while entropy describes uncertainty over the full vocabulary,
and one does not determine the other. We study the related question of how quantization changes entropy and which
tokens’ rankings it flips. Our quantized models use GPTQ [6], AWQ [19], and the MXFP4 microscaling format [41].

Controller architecture and training
We train two controllers per objective with different seeds and average their results. Figure 9 shows the design.
Figure 9: Per-step TempQuant’s two prediction heads and their connection to next-token sampling.
C.1
Architecture and inputs
The temperature and top-p heads share the same 42 logit features but have separate weights. The temperature head
outputs the correction factor κ, and the top-p head also receives log κ, so gradients reach both heads. We pass log κ
rather than κ so that keeping the requested temperature gives zero, and doubling or halving it moves the input by the
same amount in opposite directions. Following AutoDeco, the top-p head receives the predicted temperature because
changing the temperature changes which tokens a given cutoff keeps.
Model and quantization.
We use Qwen/Qwen3-4B [51]. MXFP4 quantizes the linear weights statically and their
inputs dynamically, in groups of 32 with shared E8M0 scales. Embeddings, normalization, the tied vocabulary head,
and the KV cache stay in BF16. We serve models with vLLM [15].

C.5
Training data
We train on 24 GSM8K [4] and 24 MATH [8] training questions, and hold out 32 more from each, excluding MATH-500.
For each question we collect two responses each from BF16, uncorrected quantization, and a preliminary version of
per-step TempQuant, at temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95. We take eight points from each training response and two
from each held-out response, giving 2,304 training steps and 768 held-out steps, and reuse them at all three requested
temperatures.
D
Evaluation details
D.1
Protocol notes for Section 4
Fitting global TempQuant has a one-time cost: generating the calibration responses and recording both models’
next-token probabilities on them. Each requested temperature needs its own fit, on the same data.
Every learned setting is fit on training questions, and divergence is measured on 768 decoding steps from 64 other
questions. Since we also used these questions during development, we confirm the reductions on 64 fresh questions
with the same settings and no refitting: at 0.6, GPTQ3’s forward KL falls from 1.35 to 0.35 with global TempQuant
and 0.37 with per-step TempQuant, and MXFP4’s from 0.79 to 0.23 and 0.32. Unless noted, downstream results for
per-step TempQuant average two training runs, and its divergence results on Qwen3-4B average five. The comparison
with Qwen’s recipe (Table 1) and the temperature sweep (Figure 6) use 200 MATH questions, and the former also 198
GPQA questions, with one sample each.
All GPTQ3 models use 3-bit GPTQ with group size 128 and keep the vocabulary head in BF16. The Qwen3-4B GPTQ3
checkpoint is a public third-party release1, and we quantized Qwen3-8B, R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and Gemma 4 31B
ourselves, calibrating on C4 [38]. GPTQ3 weights are dequantized for BF16 computation, while MXFP4 runs in native
four-bit computation.

D.4
Behavior measures and uncertainty
Length counts generated tokens, and the cap rate is the share of responses that hit the length limit. BLEU against BF16
[30, 33] measures word overlap with BF16 responses, splitting each question’s responses into two halves, so BF16
against itself scores below 100. Self-BLEU measures how similar a model’s responses to the same question are, so
higher means less diverse. Repetition is the share of repeated four-grams within a response.
Testing one comparison.
To compare two policies, for example raw GPTQ3 and global TempQuant, we use the
fact that both answer the same questions with the same random seeds, and take the difference in accuracy question by
question. We then resample the questions with replacement many times and recompute the average difference each time.
The middle 95% of these averages gives the interval, and a gain is significant when the interval excludes zero. We call
this a paired bootstrap over questions. Intervals in the appendix are not adjusted for multiple comparisons unless stated.
When a grade is unknown, we count it as half correct, the midpoint of counting it wrong or right.
Testing many comparisons.
We compare several policies, each training loss and global TempQuant, on several tasks
and temperatures, and with this many comparisons a few would look significant by chance. The Holm correction [11]
guards against this. It sorts the comparisons from smallest to largest p-value and tests them in that order: with m
comparisons, the smallest must fall below 0.05/m, the next below 0.05/(m −1), and so on, and testing stops at the first
comparison that fails. The chance that any comparison is declared significant by luck then stays below 5% across all of
them together. For GPTQ3, we apply it across all 75 comparisons of every task, temperature, and policy against raw
GPTQ3 and against each other (Table 10). For MXFP4, we apply it across its 54 comparisons of every task, temperature,
and training loss against native MXFP4 (Table 30). MATH drops 3 questions whose reference answers cannot be graded.

Top-p and entropy.
Figure 11 shows how the requested top-p changes the gap. Top-p removes the least likely tokens,
which lowers the entropy of both models and also narrows the gap between them: after top-p, the gap from BF16 is
13–34% smaller. Top-p alone does not remove it, so the quantized model still runs hotter than BF16 at the user’s settings.
E.3
Global TempQuant
How the setting is fit.
For each requested temperature, global TempQuant tries a coarse grid of temperatures from
0.15 to 3.5 and top-p values from .9 to 1 on the training steps, refines around the best pair, and is scored on held-out
steps from other questions. Nearby settings give almost the same divergence. The downstream runs at 0.3 and 1.0 use
(1.09, 1) and (1.22, 1). The runs at 0.6 use (1.01, 1) from an earlier, coarser search, whose forward KL is within 2% of
the refined (1.13, 1) that the divergence tables use.

Table 22 shows why the losses trade off. Forward KL charges most for cutting a token BF16 would use, so the
forward-KL predictor warms and opens its cutoff, cutting almost none of BF16’s probability; the warming also moves
probability onto tokens BF16’s own top-p removes, which reverse KL and total variation charge for. Reverse KL does
the opposite: it cools and tightens the cutoff, placing the least probability on tokens BF16 removes while cutting more
of BF16’s. JSD lands in between.

E.8
Overthinking
Quantized reasoning models tend to overthink, sampling words such as “Wait” and “Alternatively” that start unnecessary
rechecking [22]. GPTQ3 does the same. Continuing generation from 14 points where GPTQ3 strongly prefers “Wait”
and BF16 does not, chosen to show the effect where it is largest, raw GPTQ3 writes about twice as many overthinking
markers as BF16, and the learned settings barely change this (Table 28). Table 27 shows one such point. GPTQ3 always
opens with “Wait” there and BF16 never does; per-step TempQuant warms sharply at this step and opens with doubt less
often than global TempQuant. Across all 14 points, the learned settings and even the oracle move only part of the way to
BF16. Changing the temperature delays this behavior more than it removes it.

KL with smoothed probabilities
To keep KL finite, we mix a small uniform distribution U into both distributions, Pη = (1 −η)P + ηU with η = 10−4,
and compute
DF,η(P, Q) = KL(Pη∥Qη),
DR,η(P, Q) = KL(Qη∥Pη).

If the quantized model removes probability mass M that BF16 keeps, the penalty grows like M log(1/η) as η shrinks.

F.6
Limits of correcting token rankings
A positive temperature keeps the logit ranking, and top-p keeps a leading set without reordering it, so a lower-ranked
token can never become the most likely one. If BF16’s top token has rank r in the quantized model, any setting gives it
probability at most 1/r. GPTQ3 keeps BF16’s most likely token at 83% of steps and MXFP4 at 89%. When GPTQ3
changes it, BF16’s token is usually second, which happens at 10% of steps, and it falls below fifth at only 1.8%. Table 38
splits forward KL by these steps at each requested temperature, before and after the oracle. Table 39 splits it further by
the rank GPTQ3 gives BF16’s most likely token and, when that rank is 1, by whether GPTQ3 keeps the order of the
tokens BF16 keeps after top-p. The largest share of the error sits where BF16’s top token falls to second place. This
is the most common kind of flip, at about 10% of steps, and it holds 37–41% of the error the oracle leaves at every
requested temperature, more than any other group.
