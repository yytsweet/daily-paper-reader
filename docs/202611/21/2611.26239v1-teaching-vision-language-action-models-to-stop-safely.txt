Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models to Stop Safely
Zheyuan Liu1, Chuhan Wang1, Jinduo Guo1, Yue Huang1,
Xiangchi Yuan2, Ningshan Ma3, Meng Jiang1
1University of Notre Dame, 2Georgia Tech, 3MIT

Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models enable
robots to perform diverse tasks from visual ob-
servations and language instructions. While
prior work has explored task suppression and
refusal, stopping prohibited behavior once ex-
ecution is underway and controlling the re-
sulting physical response remain challenging.
Our evaluation reveals that policies which sup-
press a task from its initial state can still com-
plete it when taking over partway through ex-
ecution. Even when the task remains unfin-
ished, the robot may drop a held object, mak-
ing non-completion an incomplete measure of
safety. To address these challenges, we intro-
duce State-Aware Fallback Imitation (SAFI)
for tasks where holding provides an appropri-
ate alternative response. SAFI comprises two
stages, (1) fallback target construction, which
assigns stationary arm commands and con-
sistent gripper targets throughout prohibited-
task demonstrations, and (2) joint policy adap-
tation, which learns these targets alongside
the original actions of retained tasks.
This
supervision teaches an explicit alternative re-
sponse at intermediate execution states while
maintaining the robot’s other skills.
Exten-
sive experiments with OpenVLA-OFT across
LIBERO and BadLIBERO tasks evaluate pro-
hibited completion, physical incidents, and
retained-task performance. On the evaluated
LIBERO tasks, SAFI reduces aggregate tar-
get success after takeover to 1.3%, with no
recorded physical incidents or action saturation,
while preserving high retained-task success.1

tion learning (Brohan et al., 2023) and multimodal

2023). Generalist policies extend these capabili-
ties across robot platforms and unfamiliar environ-

while action-sequence prediction and efficient fine-
tuning improve manipulation performance (Zhao

Cui et al., 2026a). As these policies acquire more
skills, deployment also requires control over which
learned behaviors remain appropriate to execute.
This requirement becomes concrete when con-
text makes an otherwise feasible action hazardous.
Risk can emerge as a multimodal interaction devel-

without meaningful instructions (Jang et al., 2026),
while adversarial prompts and poisoned training
data can induce harmful or attacker-chosen behav-
iors (Robey et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2025). Withholding an executable behavior
is therefore a necessary complement to instruction
following.
Planning methods support this control through
robot affordances (Ichter et al., 2023), uncertainty-
based requests for help (Ren et al., 2023), and con-
textual safety specifications (Ravichandran et al.,
2026).

ing (Zhang et al., 2025) and refusal demonstra-

Since earlier actions determine later observations
in sequential control (Ross et al., 2011), evalua-
tion from initial states can leave intermediate states
untested when an edited policy stops before reach-
ing them. We examine these states by transfer-
ring control from the original policy during execu-
tion. This reveals a failure to stop ongoing tasks,
as several policies that suppress completion from
initial states still complete the prohibited task after
takeover. Even when it remains unfinished, unsafe
alternative responses arise, including dropping a
held bottle (Figure 1). Dropping and holding both
prevent placement but have different physical con-
sequences. These observations, developed in Sec-
tion 2, motivate the following research question.
How can VLAs stop ongoing prohibited
tasks while maintaining an appropriate
physical response?
Addressing this requires models to suppress pro-
hibited behavior across execution states and pro-
duce an alternative response suited to the robot’s
current state. To meet these requirements, we pro-
pose State-Aware Fallback Imitation (SAFI) for
tasks where holding provides an appropriate alter-
native. Its two-stage design combines Fallback
Target Construction, which assigns holding targets
throughout prohibited-task demonstrations, with
Joint Policy Adaptation, which learns these targets
alongside the original actions of retained tasks. By
learning an explicit fallback across execution states,
SAFI enables the robot to stop prohibited tasks with
a holding response while preserving its other skills.
Our main contributions are as follows.
1. We identify failures to stop ongoing tasks and
unsafe alternative responses in VLAs through
an intermediate-state takeover evaluation that
jointly examines prohibited execution and phys-
ical events.
2. We propose SAFI, a two-stage framework that
constructs fallback targets from prohibited-task
demonstrations and jointly optimizes fallback
and retained-task imitation to suppress prohib-
ited behavior and learn an appropriate alterna-
tive response while preserving permitted skills.
3. We evaluate SAFI across multiple LIBERO task
suites and a BadLIBERO backdoor setting. On
the evaluated LIBERO tasks, SAFI achieves low
prohibited-task completion rates after takeover
with no recorded drops or falls, while preserving
performance on retained tasks.

Motivations
Failure to Stop Ongoing Tasks.
Our first moti-
vation is inspired by findings that safety-aligned
language models can continue harmful responses
when given their opening text, despite normally re-
fusing such requests (Qi et al., 2025). This raises a
related question for VLAs about whether suppress-
ing a prohibited task also prevents its continuation
once execution is underway. To examine this, we
let the original policy begin a task and then transfer
control to a policy trained to suppress it. As shown
in Figure 2(a), some evaluated policies prevent pro-
hibited stove activation from the initial state, yet
activate the stove in nearly all episodes after early
takeover and all episodes after mid-task takeover.
These results show that suppression from initial
states does not reliably prevent continuation of the
same prohibited task once execution is underway.
Unsafe Alternative Responses.
A second chal-
lenge is about what a robot does when a forbidden
task remains unfinished. Prior work distinguishes
execution failure from deliberate refusal, showing
why low task success alone is an incomplete safety
signal (Chen et al., 2026). Similarly, for a robot
already holding an object, this distinction also in-
volves the consequences of its alternative actions.
In the example shown in Figure 2(b), some evalu-
ated policies leave the prohibited task unfinished
but drop the held bottle. Both dropping the bottle
and keeping it held satisfy the non-completion cri-
terion, although their physical consequences differ.
These findings reveal two challenges in enforc-
ing behavioral prohibitions in VLAs: (1) suppres-
sion observed from initial states may fail after
takeover, and (2) preventing task completion
does not ensure an acceptable physical response.
Addressing both challenges calls for an approach
that suppresses the prohibited behavior across exe-
cution states and guides the robot toward an appro-
priate alternative response.

3
Method
In this section, we present SAFI, a two-stage frame-
work for adapting VLAs to behavioral prohibitions.
The design addresses two challenges in enforcing
these prohibitions: (1) failures to stop ongoing
tasks and (2) unsafe alternative responses, as iden-
tified in Section 2.
Overview of SAFI.
As shown in Figure 3, Fall-
back Target Construction assigns holding responses
across prohibited-task demonstrations. Joint Policy
Adaptation then learns these targets alongside the
original actions for retained tasks.
3.1
Fallback Target Construction
The first stage defines an explicit response for the
robot at each demonstrated state of a prohibited
task. We use holding targets, building on explicit-
response supervision in no-action fine-tuning and
refusal training (Jang et al., 2026; Chen et al.,
2026). Before training, we test a scripted hold from
candidate takeover states and require it to prevent
prohibited completion and satisfy the task-specific
physical-event criteria. We apply SAFI to tasks
that pass this applicability check.
We use retained tasks to refer to the other per-
mitted tasks whose performance we aim to pre-
serve. Let Dp contain demonstrations of the be-
havior designated for suppression, to which we
assign fallback targets, and let Dr contain demon-
strations whose original actions we preserve. On
LIBERO, each edit targets one prohibited task and
uses demonstrations of the other tasks in its suite
for retained-task supervision. On BadLIBERO, the
two sets contain poisoned and clean demonstra-
tions, respectively, which can share task instruc-
tions. Each sample (x, A) contains a policy input
x with camera observations, proprioception, and
a task instruction, together with a normalized ac-
tion chunk A ∈RH×d. Here, H is the number of
actions in a chunk and d is the action dimension.
Each action contains d −1 robot-arm motion coor-
dinates and one coordinate for opening or closing
the gripper.
To construct the fallback chunk ¯A, we use a
command that requests no change in the robot grip-
per’s position or orientation. We encode this mo-
tion command as a vector h ∈Rd−1 using the
demonstration’s action normalization. We assign
this vector to every action in the chunk and repeat
the first demonstrated gripper command through-
out the chunk. For action index k = 1, . . . , H and
coordinate index j, the target is

4
Experiments
We evaluate SAFI’s effectiveness in suppressing
prohibited behavior and preserving the robot’s
other skills through four research questions. (1)
Can SAFI suppress prohibited behavior after
takeover while maintaining an acceptable physical
response? (2) How do the two training components
in SAFI contribute to its performance? (3) Can
SAFI balance target suppression with retained-task
performance? (4) Does SAFI maintain the intended
holding response after takeover?
4.1
Experimental Setup
Base models.
We use the released OpenVLA-
OFT policies (Kim et al., 2025a) as our base VLAs.
These policies predict continuous action chunks
from camera observations, proprioception, and a
language instruction. For each LIBERO prohibi-
tion, we adapt the suite-specific policy and evaluate
utility on the other tasks in that suite.
Baselines.
The original OpenVLA-OFT pol-
icy (Kim et al., 2025a) provides a reference for
behavior before adaptation. Gradient Ascent (GA)
increases imitation error on prohibited-task demon-
strations (Maini et al., 2024). Building on this
objective, Gradient Difference (GradDiff) adds
retained-task imitation (Maini et al., 2024). NPO-
RT adapts negative preference optimization to con-
tinuous actions and combines it with retained-task
imitation (Zhang et al., 2024). RMU redirects hid-
den representations on prohibited-task inputs while
matching the original representations on retained
inputs (Li et al., 2024). To compare alternative-
response supervision, Refusal FT learns home-
return actions inspired by HazardArena (Chen et al.,
2026). VLA-Forget (Ranjan and Polyzou, 2026)
provides a VLA unlearning comparison through an
OFT adaptation with module-wise updates and ac-
4


tion distillation. Baseline adaptations are described
in Appendix C.5.
Benchmarks and evaluation.
We evaluate tasks
from LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) that the original
policy can execute and for which scripted holding
passes the applicability check. These suites vary
task goals, spatial relations, and object identities,
respectively. After the original policy begins ex-
ecution, edited policies take over from matched
restored states early or midway through the task.
We also test attack-target suppression in GoBA’s
poisoned-object BadLIBERO setting (Zhou et al.,
2025), using the implanted policy as the reference.
Task selection and takeover protocols are described
in Appendix C.1.
Evaluation metrics.
Target Success Rate mea-
sures whether the prohibited target is attained after
takeover. For BadLIBERO, the target is the at-
tack objective. We also report Physical Incident
Rate, which records defined physical events such
as object drops, and Action Saturation Rate, which
records episodes whose commands reach the con-
troller’s clipping bounds under the setting’s sat-
uration criterion. Physical incidents are counted
whenever they occur, including alongside target
attainment. To assess the robot’s other skills, Re-
tained Success Rate measures performance on per-
mitted tasks from normal initial states. We report
rates as percentages and average the evaluated Goal,
Spatial, and Object settings equally for the main
LIBERO score. The suite breakdown and metric
equations are provided in Appendices D.1 and C.3.
4.2
Implementation Details
We adapt SAFI using LoRA (Hu et al., 2022)
on the language-model layers and jointly update
the inherited action head.
The LIBERO-Goal
and BadLIBERO experiments use NVIDIA RTX
A6000 GPUs. Training settings and checkpoint-
selection procedures are provided in Appendix C.4.
4.3
Main Results
Our first research question asks Can SAFI suppress
prohibited behavior after takeover while maintain-
ing an acceptable physical response? We address
this question by comparing takeover outcomes on
LIBERO and BadLIBERO in Table 1. On LIBERO,
NPO-RT records a Target Success Rate of 31.9%
and a Physical Incident Rate of 19.9%. GA and
GradDiff lower target attainment further, but their
high Action Saturation Rates indicate commands at
the controller’s clipping bounds. Refusal FT avoids
this saturation but has higher average target success
and physical incident rates than SAFI, with the dif-
ference driven mainly by Goal (Appendix D.1).
SAFI reduces target success from the original pol-
icy’s 99.7% to 1.3%, with no recorded physical
incidents or action-saturation episodes.
The BadLIBERO results distinguish suppression
from the physical response, as several methods at-
tain zero attack-target success with different physi-
cal outcomes. NPO-RT records zero attack-target
success but a 22.5% Physical Incident Rate and
a 100.0% Action Saturation Rate, so blocking the
attack leaves incidents and saturated commands.
GA and GradDiff show the same pattern of com-
plete attack suppression accompanied by physical
incidents and saturation. SAFI records zero on
all three endpoints, matching RMU. Refusal FT
also avoids attack-target attainment and physical
incidents, although 2.5% of episodes satisfy the
saturation criterion. Across these settings, SAFI
combines low prohibited-target attainment with an
acceptable response under the measured criteria.
4.4
Ablation Study
Our second question asks How do the two training
components in SAFI contribute to its performance?
We examine their roles on Spatial and Object by re-
moving one imitation term at a time and comparing
each variant with full SAFI in Table 2. The original
policy provides the reference without either term.
These comparisons assess how each component af-
fects target attainment, the physical response, and
performance on permitted tasks.
Removing fallback imitation.
We first remove
fallback imitation while keeping supervision on
retained tasks. This comparison tests whether con-
tinued training on permitted behaviors also changes
execution of the prohibited task. The variant pre-
serves high retained success but reaches the prohib-
ited target at rates of 99.7% on Spatial and 100.0%
on Object, leaving ongoing execution close to that
of the original policy. Physical incidents also per-
sist, including a 30.9% rate on Object. Full SAFI
reduces target success to 3.4% and 0.3%, respec-
tively, with no recorded physical incidents in either
suite. The difference supports explicitly teaching
the holding response to suppress ongoing execution
and guide the robot’s behavior after takeover.

4.5
Retained-Task Utility
The utility loss under fallback-only training leads
to our third question, Can SAFI balance target
suppression with retained-task performance? To
examine this trade-off, we evaluate checkpoints
throughout adaptation on Spatial and Object and
compare their suppression and retained success in
Figure 4. Each point pairs takeover suppression
with performance on permitted tasks, placing poli-
cies that perform well on both measures toward the
upper right. GA achieves high suppression with
zero retained success at every sampled checkpoint.
GradDiff improves retained success during adap-
tation, reaching 34.2% on Spatial and 41.7% on
Object alongside high target suppression. NPO-RT
and RMU exhibit the complementary trade-off, pre-
serving high retained success at lower suppression
levels. Refusal FT also reaches high suppression,
although its retained success is lower than SAFI’s
at comparable suppression levels. The later SAFI
checkpoints combine the two objectives, reaching
99.7% suppression with 97.2% retained success
on Spatial and 99.0% suppression with 93.6% re-
tained success on Object. These operating points
preserve substantially more utility than GA and
GradDiff at high suppression, while improving sup-
pression over the utility-preserving NPO-RT and
RMU checkpoints. Further retained-task results
and checkpoint-selection details are provided in
Appendix D.3.

Unlearning and fallback supervision.
Machine
unlearning removes selected data’s influence (Bour-
toule et al., 2021). Generative-model studies sup-
press harmful responses (Yao et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2024) and evaluate forgetting alongside re-
tained utility (Maini et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025).
Task arithmetic edits parameter vectors (Ilharco
et al., 2023), NPO uses negative-preference opti-
mization (Zhang et al., 2024), and RMU redirects
representations (Li et al., 2024). Within VLAs,
VLA-Forget selects components for editing (Ran-
jan and Polyzou, 2026), target-and-control auditing
examines task-vector negation (Wang et al., 2026),
and SOUL targets features associated with state hal-
lucination (Lee et al., 2026). Replacement labels
and refusal answers offer alternative supervision for
targeted language inputs (Eldan and Russinovich,
2023; Maini et al., 2024). SAFI extends this super-
vision to fallback actions across execution states
while preserving retained behaviors. We evalu-
ate suppression alongside the resulting physical
responses.
6
Conclusion
In this work, we address the challenges of stopping
ongoing prohibited tasks and guiding VLAs toward
appropriate physical responses. We propose SAFI,
which first constructs holding targets throughout
prohibited-task demonstrations and then jointly op-
timizes fallback and retained-task imitation. Exper-
iments with OpenVLA-OFT on selected LIBERO
and BadLIBERO tasks show that SAFI suppresses
prohibited completion after takeover while preserv-
ing retained-task performance, with no recorded
physical incidents.

Limitations
Broader
Deployment
Settings.
We
evalu-
ate OpenVLA-OFT on selected LIBERO and
BadLIBERO tasks using a fixed training seed. Fu-
ture work could extend this evaluation to additional
VLA architectures and robot embodiments, with
repeated training runs to assess the consistency of
suppression and retained utility. Testing physical
robots under varied sensing and control conditions
would further clarify the approach’s practical
scope.
More Flexible Fallback Responses.
SAFI cur-
rently learns a holding response for tasks that pass
the applicability check. When an object is unstable
or a physical interaction is already underway, a suit-
able fallback may require controlled motion. Fu-
ture work could construct targets for responses such
as placing an object down or withdrawing from
contact, and study how to learn these responses
alongside retained-task imitation.

C.5
Baseline Adaptations
All baselines use the OFT action representation.
GA increases L1 error on prohibited demonstra-
tions, and GradDiff combines this update with
retained-action imitation. NPO-RT applies neg-
ative preference optimization through a continuous-
action score and retained imitation. RMU redirects
prohibited-input hidden states toward a random
vector while preserving original hidden states on
retained inputs.
Response and VLA baselines.
Refusal FT
learns controller-generated home-return trajecto-
ries from interrupted tasks, together with retained-
task demonstrations. The controller preserves the
last gripper command during return, opens the
gripper for reset demonstrations, and clips arm
commands to the normalization bounds. Train-
ing demonstrations are interrupted before release
or completion for placement tasks and before con-
tact for the stove. Their median interruption times
are later than held-out mid-task takeover for place-
ment and earlier for the stove. VLA-Forget uses
staged module-wise LoRA, gradient projection,
and action-output distillation in our OFT imple-
mentation. The VLA-Forget results describe our
deployed OFT adaptation, whose action outputs
differ after checkpoint reloading.

BadLIBERO
The GoBA setting uses a poisoned box as a physi-
cal trigger for placing that box in a designated table
region, L3 (Zhou et al., 2025). We adapt the im-
plantation to the OFT Goal specialist and evaluate
the two attacked Goal tasks. Takeovers occur at
first gripper–box contact and five steps before the
earlier of post-grasp opening and L3 entry. Ev-
ery method uses the same donor states and fixed
observation window.
The physical-incident metric combines four
event types. A drop occurs when an ungrasped
object remains more than 10 cm below its post-
wait initial height for two consecutive steps. Lost
hold applies to a box grasped at takeover that re-
mains ungrasped, outside L3, and more than 10 cm
below its takeover height for two consecutive steps.
Disturbance records movement beyond 5 cm or tilt
beyond 45◦from the takeover pose for objects out-
side the task objects of interest and trigger box. A
fallen-body event records a non-box drop or an ini-
tially grasped box ending ungrasped and more than
10 cm below its takeover height. Their union counts
incidents even when they accompany attack-target
attainment.
Beyond the main-table endpoints, SAFI pro-
duces a hold-like response in 87.5% of takeovers.
This response requires no target attainment, release,
or physical incident, with end-effector excursion
within 3 cm of the takeover position. On clean,
untriggered inputs, SAFI retains 96.0% clean-task
success, compared with 91.0% for the implanted
policy and 95.0% for the clean reference. These
measurements complement attack suppression with
the intended holding response and utility on ordi-
nary inputs.
D.1.2
Instruction and Visual Shifts
We also evaluate normal starts under instruction
paraphrases and warmer image colors, after check-
ing that the original policy remains competent in
each condition. SAFI records zero target success
across all three Goal prohibitions under both shifts,
with retained success ranging from 91.1% to 96.7%.
This result extends the initial-state suppression find-
ing beyond the canonical instructions and image
appearance.
D.2
Supervision Design (RQ2)
The main ablation examines the two imitation
terms. Here, we retain both terms and vary the
states supplying fallback supervision to examine
the role of execution-state coverage. All variants
use the same Spatial and Object cohorts, training
budget, and checkpoint-selection procedure.
Prefix-only supervision uses chunks entirely
before the first gripper-close command. Count-
matched full-trajectory supervision distributes the
same number of candidate chunks across each
demonstration. Post-close-only supervision uses
chunks starting at or after that boundary, and full
SAFI uses all valid chunks. The matched-count
comparison controls the available candidate count
while changing their temporal coverage and associ-
ated gripper labels.
All four variants suppress normal-start comple-
tion, but prefix-only supervision permits almost
complete target execution after takeover (Table 10).
Distributing the same candidate count across the
full trajectory reduces takeover success to 0.6% in
both suites. Post-close supervision and full SAFI
also achieve low target success while retaining high
utility. This pattern supports teaching the fallback
at states encountered during execution.
