Abstract
A task specifies how the world should change, while a
policy specifies what the robot should do.
We introduce
Progress Predicts Policy (PPP), which learns a language-
conditioned scalar field through the commands induced by
its differential. We obtain action conditioning by compos-
ing a latent progress field with a shared world model and
read its action gradient at a fixed reference command. Con-
tinuous commands scale this gradient by a learned posi-
tive gain, while discrete commands use finite differences
of the same scalar. This yields a hybrid policy without a
separate action head or iterative search. Fitting demon-
stration progress values alone does not ensure that action
gradients align with expert commands. PPP trains the in-
duced commands by imitation and uses double backprop-
agation for the continuous gradient readout to shape the
field’s local control geometry. Demonstration timing cali-
brates field values on observed states without additional an-
notation. The continuous readout remains learnable with-
out progress labels but these timing-derived targets support
progress monitoring during execution. Experiments in sim-
ulated and real robot manipulation show that PPP outper-
forms behavioral cloning under a shared architecture.
1. Introduction
Imitation learning has produced capable visuomotor poli-
cies. Diffusion policies model expressive action distribu-
tions [7] and vision-language-action (VLA) models bring
web-scale visual and linguistic knowledge to control [5,
19].
These methods learn a map from observations and
instructions to actions. A task and a policy play different
roles, however. A task states what should change in the
world: the bowl should end up on the plate. The robot
dynamics determine which changes its commands can pro-
duce. We ask whether a learned scalar task evaluator can
provide progress estimates and a differential from which a
policy reads its commands.
We represent a task as a field: a language-conditioned
scalar progress field pθ(z, ℓ) over learned latent states z.
Timing labels supervise its values on observations while
expert commands supervise its differential-induced actions.
Reading an action derivative requires dependence on a can-
didate command.
We obtain this dependence by com-
posing the field with a shared world model, Qz,ℓ(a) =
pθ(f(z, a), ℓ). The chain rule at a fixed reference a0 gives:

where ¯z = f(z, a0) and Jf is the action Jacobian of the

Continuous commands scale ga by a
learned positive gain while discrete commands use finite
differences of Q. One gradient evaluation supplies contin-
uous commands without a separate action generator or it-
erative search. The same field provides progress estimates
on observations and control readouts on action-conditioned
latents. Sec. 4.3 shows that a directly action-conditioned
scalar yields an effective continuous gradient policy.
Fitting progress values alone does not ensure that this
differential yields useful commands. Progress labels con-
strain pθ only at demonstrated states. Functions agreeing
on those values can have different nearby derivatives, which
ga reads. Fig. 4 shows that progress-only fields rank train-
ing demonstration states almost perfectly but produce action
gradients that align only weakly with expert commands.
A second gap separates local from global use of the field.
Searching for finite commands that maximize Q need not
recover the demonstrated behavior and almost never suc-
ceeds in our experiments.
The central learning problem
is therefore to learn a control-aligned progress field with
actionable local geometry rather than merely an accurate
progress estimator.
We introduce Progress Predicts Policy (PPP), which
trains a task field through the behavior it induces. Demon-
strations supervise the field in two complementary ways:
expert commands supervise the action produced by ga while
demonstration-relative progress labels calibrate its values
without extra annotation. Imitation jointly shapes the field
geometry and the action response of the query model.
Under a shared architecture, PPP outperforms behavioral
cloning (BC) on LIBERO [21] and real robots.
Auxil-
iary progress prediction leaves BC essentially unchanged.
Sec. 4.4 shows that progress labels are optional for con-
tinuous control and useful for execution monitoring. Our
contributions are:
(i) a scalar-field policy that reads continuous commands
from action gradients and discrete commands from finite
differences of the same scalar;
(ii) a training formulation that supervises induced com-
mands by imitation and calibrates values on observed states
using annotation-free demonstration timing;
(iii) an empirical study that separates progress-value
fit from action-gradient alignment and closed-loop perfor-
mance and characterizes the local scope of the learned read-
out.
2. Related Work
Visuomotor policies and world models.
Most visuomo-
tor policies predict actions from observations [10, 45]. Dif-
fusion policies model multimodal action distributions [7,
42] while VLA models add action outputs to pretrained
vision-language backbones [5, 19, 49]. World models sup-
port planning through visual features in DINO-WM [47]
and action-conditioned video prediction in V-JEPA 2 [3].
LeWorldModel learns latent dynamics from pixels [25].
Other predictive models couple prediction with action gen-
eration [22, 36, 39, 41]. Model-based policy learning dif-
ferentiates through learned dynamics to train a separate
actor [11, 14]. PPP reads commands from a scalar field
through the world model without a separate action head.
Reward, value, and progress representations.
Visual
and
language-conditioned
representations
can
induce
reusable rewards [23, 24, 34]. GOMP uses a graspability
field for reward shaping and termination in non-prehensile
manipulation [46]. VLAC and Robometer scale progress
and preference estimation across robot data [20, 43].
Progress estimates inform action generation through rein-
forcement learning feedback in VLAC [43] and verification
of policy-proposed candidates in TapSampling [44]. The
closest work is ProgressVLA, which backpropagates
progress through a world model to guide a latent-action dif-
fusion policy [40]. This parallels reward-gradient guidance
of trajectory diffusion in Diffuser [15].
These methods
guide an independent action generator. In PPP, the scaled
pulled-back progress differential defines the continuous
command and is trained by imitation. Inverse reinforce-
ment learning learns objectives to match demonstrated
behavior [1, 48] but recovers actions by optimization.
Potential-based control and differentiable planning.
Classical robotics generates motion from scalar potential
gradients. Artificial potential fields steer manipulators away
from obstacles and toward goals [18] while navigation
functions remove spurious minima by construction [32].
These potentials are designed in configuration space for a
known goal. Learning-from-demonstration methods fit sta-
ble dynamical systems [16, 30] or potential functions [17]
to demonstrated motion in robot state space.
Geometric
policy frameworks compose task-space controllers via
pullbacks [6, 27]. PPP extends this perspective to hybrid
control through a language-conditioned perceptual latent
potential and learned,
rather than known,
dynamics.
Implicit behavioral cloning selects actions by minimizing
a learned energy at inference [9]. Differentiable planners
learn through action optimization [2, 35, 37] and GRASP
improves gradient-based planning with visual world mod-
els [29]. TD-MPC methods use latent models and learned
values for sampling-based planning [12, 13].
PPP reads
continuous commands with one gradient evaluation and no
inference-time optimization. Supervising the induced com-
mand connects PPP to Sobolev training, which supervises
network derivatives alongside values [8].

3. Method
PPP learns a scalar task field through the commands in-
duced by its action-conditioned differential (Fig. 2).

4. Experiments
We evaluate PPP on simulated and real robot manipulation,
examining policy performance, the role of progress super-
vision, the locality of learned guidance, and hybrid action
readouts.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Shared architecture.
All methods share encoder, world
model, and MLP-trunk architectures but use method-
specific readouts and supervision. A shared LeWM [25]
ViT encodes third-person and wrist images (128 × 128)
into one 384-dimensional latent.
A transformer predicts
the next latent from three past observations and their ac-
tion chunks.
Each prediction spans k = 4 raw ticks.
Both PPP and BC regress the mean expert pose command
and execute one tick before replanning.
LIBERO uses
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [31, 38] instruction embeddings.
Real-robot tasks use one-hot vectors that remain constant in
the single-task doll and bimanual experiments. The poten-
tial and BC head are FiLM-conditioned residual MLPs [28]
and the mobility in PPP is a small MLP.
Compared methods. BC predicts pose from the reference
latent and uses a gripper-switching head with the same con-
text and action loss as PPP. BC+Progress adds a progress
output and the progress losses used by PPP to the same
trunk.
BC-MH predicts four pose candidates and trains
progress to favor the candidate closest to the expert com-
mand. We compare its learned selector with progress rank-
ing at the same checkpoint [44]. CEM replaces the pose
readout of PPP with cross-entropy-method search [33] to
maximize Q within the training action range. CEM-Goal
uses goal-reaching planning in latent world models [25, 47].
PPP-GH keeps the pose readout of PPP but uses the BC
gripper-switching head.
Direct-AP uses a direct action-
space scalar potential Q(u) = Φ(¯z, u, ℓ) and retains the
world model reference prediction ¯z = f(z, a0) and gripper
head of PPP-GH. No Lp removes all progress losses and
trains the field of PPP-GH solely through its action readout.
Seer-PPP reads the task field through a small world model
added to the action-free network of Seer [36]. Both Seer-
PPP and Seer train from scratch with the Seer architecture
and training recipe to test the field readout against a strong
baseline.
LIBERO protocol. The world model first trains alone for
100 epochs on all 6,500 demonstrations from 130 LIBERO
tasks, including LIBERO-90 [21].
Each method then
trains one multitask policy from this checkpoint on the
same demonstrations and is compared at matched epochs.
Progress supervision uses the normalized-phase targets of
Sec. 3.1.
We evaluate Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long
across 40 tasks with 50 initial states per task shared across
methods.
All controllers replan end-effector pose incre-
ments and binary gripper commands at 20 Hz. Success-rate
differences use task-level sign-flip tests (ptask).
Gradient diagnostics. We measure progress MAE, rank-
ing,
and gradient–expert alignment on fixed training
demonstration states with the deployed queries.
Closed-
loop success on evaluation initial states separately measures
control performance. Progress-only fields train with the en-
coder and dynamics frozen. The frozen-WM control uses
the losses of PPP-GH under the same freezing constraint.
Real robot. A Franka arm uses wrist and front RGB cam-
eras with the same input resolution, context, and control
rate as LIBERO. Both methods start from the same world
model pretrained for 100 epochs on the demonstrations for
each task. Bun picking requires placing the bun of the in-
structed color in a steamer. This task uses normalized-phase
supervision and 400 demonstrations with evaluation on ten
layouts under all four instructions. Single-instruction doll
straightening requires flipping and orienting a randomly
posed doll.
This task uses absolute time-to-go supervi-
sion and 150 demonstrations with 12 evaluation trials per
method. Sec. 4.6 describes an additional bimanual task with
two xArm7 arms.

4.5. Locality of the Learned Field
Fig. 6 measures first-order fit and gradient stability around
the reference command. For PPP and PPP-GH the first-
order approximation holds only within a small fraction of
a typical expert command.
Gradient directions change
sharply farther from the reference. Despite this local scope,
Q still ranks equal-size commands toward the expert direc-
tion. For PPP-GH, this holds on 98% of states when com-
mands have the same norm as the expert command. Such
directional ranking does not establish calibrated progress
forecasts for arbitrary finite commands.
Tab. 4 further
shows that progress-based ranking does not significantly
improve BC-MH and that CEM on the PPP checkpoint fails
in all 2,000 rollouts. These findings are limited to the tested
search procedures and action ranges.
4.6. One Field, Two Action Types
Gripper readout and settling.
Tab. 4 shows that finite-
difference switching on Q improves average success over
the gripper head of PPP-GH with the same pose-readout
architecture (ptask < 0.01). Tab. 1 reports gains on Ob-
ject and Long but lower success on Spatial and Goal. We
also test a settling procedure to reduce premature post-
grasp motion. Settling keeps pose queries conditioned on
an open gripper until finger feedback indicates that closure
has stopped. This procedure modestly improves epoch-120
success without retraining or changing switching decisions.
The comparisons above omit settling.
Multiple discrete readouts.
In egg pouring with two
xArm7 arms, one field supplies a joint gradient over 12 pose
coordinates and a finite difference for each gripper. A sin-
gle mobility scales the joint pose gradient. The controller
succeeds in 1 of 5 trials as shown in Fig. 3(c).
5. Conclusion
PPP learns a scalar task field through the commands it in-
duces: continuous commands from action gradients and
gripper switches from finite differences.
Composing the
field with a shared world model supplies action condition-
ing and an explicit chain-rule interpretation; direct action
conditioning is also effective. Progress labels are optional
for learning continuous control, but calibrate the field for
execution monitoring.
Under a shared architecture, PPP
outperforms BC in simulation and on real robots.
Limitations.
Demonstration-relative progress may admit
“arm clock” shortcuts based on arm motion rather than
task-relevant scene changes. The field has no Lyapunov
guarantee, and its differential does not guarantee physical
progress. Progress-based recovery and sharing the task field
across embodiments remain future work.

ment of Q for the executed command, and improvement of
the learned query would itself require separate validation to
imply physical task progress. Figure 6 shows that in the
trained models the first-order region is far smaller than a
deployed command.

A.6. Optimization and Execution
Training schedule. First, the encoder and dynamics train
alone from random initialization with Eq. (25) for 100
epochs on all 6,500 LIBERO demonstrations (130 tasks, in-
cluding LIBERO-90), with AdamW (learning rate 5×10−5,
weight decay 10−3, batch size 512), five epochs of warmup,
and cosine decay to zero. The final checkpoint initializes
the encoder and dynamics of every method. Second, each
policy is trained on the same demonstrations. For the first
ten epochs the world model is frozen: PPP calibrates only
its potential with Lp, and the BC variants train only their
heads. From epoch 10, the world model is trained jointly
with the policy, and its predictive loss is retained. PPP’s
action supervision therefore starts with world model train-
ing, whereas BC’s starts against the frozen world model.
The policy learning-rate schedule is configured for 200
epochs; we evaluate checkpoints up to epoch 120 and com-
pare methods at matched epochs.

Gripper settling: motivation.
In an earlier LIBERO-
Spatial run without query normalization, conditioning the
pose query on a close command reduced descent before the
grasp had settled. This motivated delaying closed-gripper

Gripper settling: implementation. For the LIBERO set-
tling variant, the pose query uses a separate gripper com-
mand history that remains open during physical closure.
Finger opening is measured as the sum of the absolute fin-
ger joint positions. Starting three raw ticks after a close
command, closure is considered settled when this opening
decreases by less than 2 mm in one tick, with a maximum
wait of 15 ticks. Subsequent pose queries use the closed ref-
erence command and record closed commands in their his-
tory. The gripper readout continues to use the actual com-
mand history throughout; reopening resets the settling state.
This adjustment changes only the pose query’s gripper con-
ditioning and requires no retraining.

A.9. Sensitivity to Policy Training Schedule
Original protocol. The main LIBERO comparison retains
the protocol of Sec. A.6: a common pretrained world model,
training data, learning-rate schedule, and world model un-
freezing epoch.
During the first ten policy epochs, PPP
and PPP-GH calibrate their potentials with Lp, whereas BC
trains its action head and No Lp trains its gradient read-
out by action imitation. From epoch 10, all jointly train the
world model; action supervision begins at this point for PPP
and PPP-GH. This compares complete training procedures,
including their early objectives, but does not isolate policy
structure from the conditions at the onset of action learning.
Branch protocol. We compare BC-branch, No Lp-branch,
PPP-GH-branch, and PPP-branch.
The first three use a
learned gripper-switching head and inherit the same epoch-
10 world model state used to initialize PPP-GH’s joint


stage. PPP-branch uses field-based switching and inherits
the epoch-10 checkpoint used to initialize full PPP’s joint
stage, from a separate initial-stage run. In each case, the en-
coder and dynamics retain their pretrained weights, which
were frozen during the initial stage, and inherit their source
checkpoint’s BatchNorm running statistics.
BC’s action
and switching heads use their original epoch-0 initializa-
tion. For all field variants, the potential is reset to its origi-
nal epoch-0 weights; the gain and, where present, switching
head retain their initial weights, having received no updates
in the initial stage. Thus no progress-trained potential is
transferred to a branch run. The field variants initialize their
query-normalization statistics from the inherited prediction-
normalization statistics, as in their original runs.
All four runs start with empty AdamW moment states,
restore their source checkpoint’s random state, and retain
the epoch-10 learning-rate schedule and epoch-indexed data
sampling.
The first update therefore uses the near-peak
learning rates, without repeating warmup, and jointly trains
the policy and world model. The data, batch size, loss defi-
nitions, and deployment rules remain those of each method.
PPP-GH-branch applies Lp and action supervision together
from this first update; No Lp-branch differs only by set-
ting all progress-loss weights to zero.
PPP-branch like-
wise applies progress, pose, and field-based gripper super-
vision from the first update. Its two deployment variants
evaluate the same checkpoints with and without gripper set-
tling, using the original PPP deployment settings (Sec. A.6).
Epoch labels retain the original counter: checkpoint e con-
tains e −10 epochs of action supervision in every branch
run, versus e for original BC and No Lp. Original PPP and
PPP-GH also receive e −10 action-supervised epochs, pre-
ceded by ten epochs of progress calibration. Equal check-
point labels therefore match action-update budgets within
the branch comparison, but not across every original and
branch pair.

A.10. Evaluation with a Seer Backbone
We additionally evaluate PPP with a Seer [36] backbone to
examine its applicability beyond the LeWM-based [25] ar-
chitecture used in our main experiments. Seer is a predictive
inverse dynamics model: a 24-layer transformer forecasts
future frames from past observations and the instruction,


and action queries read that foresight to predict the next
commands. Its network never receives a candidate com-
mand, so PPP’s readout needs an action-conditioned query
added to it. We compare four variants:

A.14. World-Model Search Baselines
Search on query values. CEM deploys PPP’s epoch-120
checkpoint and replaces only how the pose command is
read out. Let QH(u0:H−1) = p(zH, ℓ), where zh+1 =
f(z≤h, (uh, ch)) is PPP’s query (Sec. A.2) and each query
output is fed back as the newest context frame.
CEM
searches H pose commands to maximize QH by the cross-
entropy method [33], with:
• 300 Gaussian samples in normalized action units, with
initial standard deviation 1 (the standard deviation of the
training actions);
• 30 elites, refit momentum 0.1, and a floor of 0.1 on the
standard deviation;
• every sample clipped per dimension to the 0.5–99.5th
percentiles of the normalized training commands, and its
norm to their 99th percentile (5.36);
• the current mean always among the candidates, and the
first command of the refit mean executed.
Like PPP, CEM replans at every tick. It warm-starts from
the previous solution, shifted by one control step every k
ticks, with four refits at the first tick of each control step
and one at each of the three ticks between. The gripper is
handled in one of two ways:
• switch:
PPP’s progress-based switch, with the same
threshold;
• search: searched as one Bernoulli command per step,
whose probability is refit to the fraction of elites that
close, clipped to [0.05, 0.95].
At H = 1 and u = 0 the objective equals PPP’s query
value at the reference command. Candidates do not inter-
act, because at deployment the query normalization uses its


running statistics.
Goal-reaching search. CEM-Goal uses the encoder and
world model from PPP’s epoch-120 checkpoint and mini-
mizes ∥z4−zgoal∥2. Here z4 is the world model’s own four-
step prediction from the executed action history, with its
own normalization statistics and without the progress model
or the instruction. It searches pose and gripper commands
as above. The benchmark’s initial states have no demon-
strations, so the subgoal comes from training data:
• at the start of an episode, it retrieves the training demon-
stration of the same task whose first-frame latent is near-
est the current one;
• at every tick, it matches the current latent to that demon-
stration within a forward window of eight control steps,
and aims four control steps past the match.

A.15. Parameter Counts and Arithmetic Cost
Counting convention. Table 18 reports registered model
parameters and executed matrix-multiplication and convo-
lution FLOPs for the LIBERO models, counting a multiply–
accumulate as two operations.
We trace the deployed
planner and joint-training computation with PyTorch 2.11’s
operator-level FLOP counter, including the action-gradient
backward pass and the outer loss backward pass during
training. Attention uses its mathematical implementation
so that its matrix products are counted explicitly. These are
dense-arithmetic counts: elementwise operations, activa-
tions, normalization, softmax, reductions, data movement,
optimizer updates, and distributed communication are ex-
cluded. The counts are obtained in FP32 on CPU without
timing measurements and do not depend on GPU load or
clock speed.
Deployment. Each decision has batch size one, two new
128 × 128 RGB images, and three cached observation la-
tents. The deployed planner encodes only the newest pair of
images; it reuses the historical latents. The readout count in-
cludes the reference dynamics prediction, the pose readout,
and the gripper decision. For PPP it includes one action-
gradient evaluation and both gripper potential evaluations
as executed by the implementation; for BC it includes the
direct pose and switching heads. Instruction embeddings
are computed before control, so the shared text encoder
is excluded from both parameter and per-decision FLOP
counts. Optional progress logging is also excluded. Shared
visual-encoder weights are counted once despite processing
both views. Parameter totals include all registered network
weights, including the ViT pooler whose output the down-
stream model does not use.
Joint training.
Training counts include all configured
losses and their backward passes after world model un-
freezing, using seeded synthetic tensors with the train-
ing input shapes.
The per-sample count includes con-
text encoding and the next-frame world-model target.
Progress-supervised methods additionally encode the later
frame used for progress pairs.
It also includes query-
normalization passes and the original and repeated-frame
gripper contexts where applicable. Thus, the training com-
parison measures each complete objective; its difference
includes these additional evaluations as well as differen-
tiation through the action gradient.
Under this counting
convention, PPP uses 1.09× BC’s per-decision arithmetic
and 1.28× its per-sample training arithmetic. Relative to
BC+Progress, which also encodes progress pairs, the train-
ing ratio is 1.02×.

C.5. Progress Along Closed-Loop Rollouts
Protocol. Each closed-loop LIBERO evaluation records
pt = pθ(E(ot), ℓ) after every executed tick, using the plan-
ner’s own context. Figure 5 and Tab. 25 use the epoch-
120 evaluations of PPP without settling, BC+Progress (its
progress output, which its actions do not use), and No Lp,
on the same 2,000 initial states. Each model follows its own
closed-loop trajectories, ending at first success or the suite’s
time budget (300 ticks, 520 on Long). Panel (a) shows un-
smoothed traces from different initial states of put both the
alphabet soup and the tomato sauce in the basket: a success,
a failure with the first item placed and the second held, and a
failure after dropping the first item. For the population pan-
els, each trace is resampled to 101 points over its duration.
The affine map for the label-free scalar is the least-squares
fit of the label ρ −1 on its values over the 7,849 diagnostic
states; it explains 24% of the label variance, against 99%
for PPP. The 5 s value averages the five ticks ending at tick
100; early AUROC uses only rollouts still running then. Of
PPP’s 1,691 such rollouts, 43% later succeed. AUROC in-
tervals are percentile bootstraps over the 40 tasks (2,000 re-
samples).
Results. On successful rollouts, both calibrated scalars
tend to rise and end near the terminal label; failed rollouts
tend to end below it (Tab. 25). PPP’s observed-state values
yield success AUROCs of 0.84 at rollout end and 0.78 at 5 s;
BC+Progress scores 0.89 and 0.75 on its own rollouts. With
the untuned threshold p ≥−0.1 (five-tick running mean; a
tenth of a demonstration), 70% of PPP’s successful rollouts
register completion, a median 0.3 s before the simulator’s
success check ends the episode, and 30% of its failed roll-
outs register it at some point (BC+Progress: 70%, 0.35 s,
30%). The calibrated field thus measures demonstration-
relative progress, which is informative about success but
does not verify it. Both calibrated fields have higher out-
come AUROCs than No Lp on their respective rollouts;
these metrics are invariant to positive affine rescaling.
