LoRN: Low-Rank Noise Steering for
Generative Robot Policies
Wei Liu1*, Abrar Anwar1*, Huihua Zhao1, Soha Pouya1 and Yan Chang1
1NVIDIA, *Equal contribution
Noise steering improves pretrained generative robot policies by learning to select their initial noise
without updating model weights. We find that actions in the evaluated world-action models are
more sensitive to video noise than action noise, making video noise an expressive input for steering.
However, the noise-steering methods we evaluate, in their published forms, struggle to scale to the
high-dimensional noise inputs of large policy classes such as world-action models. We introduce
Low-Rank Noise Steering (LoRN), a reinforcement learning method that scales noise steering to
world-action models. A small steering actor selects a direction through a fixed low-rank random
projection, while our norm-controlled construction keeps the resulting noise near the typical scale of a
Gaussian sample. This separates the actor’s control dimension from the full noise dimension, enabling
steering of both video and action inputs. Across three world-action models and ten model–task pairs,
video steering improves average success by 26.2 percentage points over the base policy, compared
with 7.1 points for DSRL action steering. For smaller action policies, the same construction achieves
comparable final success while generally improving early-training success. Steering actors trained
in simulation also transfer to three physical bimanual manipulation tasks across two base policies,
without additional real-world training.

LoRN : Low-Rank Noise Steering for Generative Robot Policies
1. Introduction
Large robot foundation models, such as vision-
language-action models (VLAs) and world-action
models (WAMs), can perform a wide range of be-
haviors. For these models to reliably succeed on a
particular task, they must learn to improve via interac-
tion with their environment. Reinforcement learning
(RL) offers a way to improve their task performance
through interaction, but updating large policies can
be computationally expensive.
Noise steering has
emerged as an approach to guide the denoising process
of diffusion and flow-matching policies by replacing
the initial noise samples. However, these methods
struggle to scale to high-dimensional noise inputs that
are common in large policy classes such as VLAs and
WAMs. This work develops a noise steering method
that addresses this challenge, focusing primarily on
WAMs.
World-action models couple visual dynamics pre-
diction with action generation. Their sampling inputs
include initial source distribution noise associated
with both video and actions, and changing the video
noise can also change the actions the model produces.
Existing action-noise steering methods do not exploit
this input, and in the implementations and settings we
evaluate they give no consistent improvement when
applied to the substantially larger video-noise space.
In this work, we find that WAMs’ decoded actions
are more sensitive to video noise than action noise,
implying video noise may serve as a strong steering
modality.
We introduce Low-Rank Noise Steering (LoRN),
a method to efficiently train noise steering policies
for world-action models. Recent work has found that
selecting the initial noise samples of diffusion or flow
matching heads can effectively steer robot policies
in a sample efficient manner without requiring any
updates to the full model [26]. We find that extending
noise steering to large robot foundation models often
fails, primarily because their noise inputs are high-
dimensional and their sensitivity to it differs sharply
between the video and action inputs.
LoRN overcomes these challenges by steering a low-
rank projection of the noise distribution. A steering
actor outputs a low-dimensional vector that a fixed
random projection lifts to the full noise dimension,
which is then rescaled to resemble a Gaussian sample’s
norm. The frozen policy denoises this noise, and the
actor is trained with reinforcement learning. This
formulation scales to high-dimensional action spaces
and, most surprisingly, enables the steerability of the
video noise to improve denoised actions.
Two design choices underlie our approach. First,
the actor controls only a small, low-rank subspace of
the noise regardless of the input dimension. We show
that such a low-rank subspace contains directions
that improve on the base policy with high probability.
Second, mixing the actor’s direction with a fresh
Gaussian sample preserves the expected squared norm
of the noise, so the steered noise stays near the typical
norm that diffusion and flow-matching policies are
trained to denoise. Because neither choice depends
on the structure of the noise input, LoRN can be
applied to either the video or the action noise of a
WAM. This flexibility matters in practice: we find
that a WAM’s actions are far more sensitive to one
noise input than the other, and a probe that needs
no reward identifies that input before training. On
the three models we evaluate, it is the video noise,
which existing methods do not steer.
We evaluate LoRN on three world-action models,
on action-noise steering with 𝜋0, 𝜋0.5, and Diffusion
Policy, and on a physical bimanual robot station. In
aggregate across three WAMs and ten model–task
pairs, DSRL on the action noise improves the base
policies by 7.1 percentage points, while LoRN steers
the more expressive video noise and improves them
by 26.2 percentage points. We also evaluate LoRN on
twelve action-policy simulation tasks, where it gen-
erally achieves higher early-training success against
DSRL [26] and LP-DS [24]. Finally, we demonstrate
that LoRN trained in simulation transfers to three
physical bimanual manipulation tasks on two base
policies, the released GR00T N1.7 and a 𝜋0.5 fine-
tuned in simulation, without additional training on
hardware.
Our contributions are fourfold:
1. We introduce LoRN, low-rank noise steering for
robot foundation models: a fixed random sub-
space of the noise, mixed with a fresh Gaussian
sample, independent of the actor’s output, so that
the expected squared noise norm is preserved.
2. We identify noise-norm preservation as a key
requirement for reliable steering, and measure the
band of norms that each frozen policy tolerates.
3. We prove that a random subspace of rank 𝑟misses
the high-advantage noise of a state with probabil-
ity at most (1 −𝑝)𝑟, where 𝑝is that noise’s share
of the sphere, and that a policy which selects an
improving direction in that subspace whenever
one exists improves on the base policy.
4. We provide a practical recipe for steering world-
action models, with a reward-free probe that
finds which noise input to steer, and evaluate
it on three world-action models, twelve action-
policy tasks, and a bimanual robot station with
two base policies.

2. Related Work
Reinforcement learning for generative robot
policies.
Diffusion and flow-matching policies [3, 1,
11] generate action chunks by iteratively denoising a
Gaussian sample. The same principle underlies gen-
eralist models [19] and world-action models, which
jointly denoise future video and actions [20, 29]. These
policies are commonly improved by fine-tuning their
weights with policy gradients [22, 31] or by learning
residual corrections to their actions [30]. For models
with billions of parameters, however, online weight
updates are computationally expensive and risk erod-
ing pretrained capabilities. In contrast, LoRN keeps
the policy frozen and adapts only its initial noise.
Steering frozen generative policies.
DSRL [26]
showed that a frozen diffusion or flow policy can be
adapted by training an RL agent in its noise space.
Concurrent work [13] likewise trains a Gaussian ac-
tor over the action noise of a frozen flow-matching
VLA. Subsequent work regularizes the learned noise
with a budget on its deviation from a Gaussian sam-
ple [24], or restricts the actor to the noise directions
to which the decoder is most sensitive [17]. Golden-
ticket search [21] instead finds a single fixed noise
that improves a policy across states. Latent policy
steering [8] fixes the norm of the steered noise at its
typical value and trains the actor offline with gradients
through a one-step policy. These methods were de-
veloped for policies with low-dimensional noise. They
either do not explicitly control the norm of the steered
noise [26, 17, 13], control it only on average [24], or
require a sensitivity probe whose cost grows linearly
with the noise dimension [17]. Fixing the norm ex-
actly [8] still leaves the full noise dimension as the
search space and requires a policy that is differen-
tiable in one step. In contrast, LoRN steers a random
low-rank slice that requires no probing or gradients
through the policy and preserves the expected squared
norm of the noise by construction, which allows it to
scale to the video noise of world-action models.
Noise
geometry
and
low-rank
adaptation.
High-dimensional Gaussian noise concentrates near
a fixed norm. Image generative models exploit this
property by interpolating along the corresponding
sphere [27, 25] and regularizing the norm during noise
optimization [4, 23], and their outputs degrade when
sampling drifts from it [18]. Separately, LoRA [7] and
studies of intrinsic dimension [10] show that large
networks can be adapted within low-rank or ran-
dom subspaces of their parameters. Outside robotics,
active-subspace noise steering [14] searches the initial
noise of a frozen flow model along a few objective-
sensitive directions estimated from objective values
alone. LoRN brings both ideas to policy adaptation:
it applies a random low-rank subspace to the input of
a frozen model rather than its weights, and keeps the
result concentrated near the typical Gaussian norm.

5. LoRN: Low-Rank Noise Steering

for steering large policies: a search space whose di-

contains improving noise (Section 5.2), and describe
how to choose which noise input of a world-action

5.3. Choosing Which Noise to Steer
A world-action model exposes both video and action
noise, but these inputs need not have equal influence
on the generated actions. Changing the video noise
can also change the action chunk because the model
denoises the two inputs jointly. We therefore choose
which input to steer by measuring its effect on the
actions before training a steering actor.
For a fixed observation, we first generate a ref-
erence action chunk using the model’s usual noise
inputs. We then redraw the video noise while holding
the action noise fixed, and repeat the experiment by
redrawing only the action noise. For each redraw,
we compute the root-mean-square (RMS) difference
from the reference action chunk across its time steps
and action coordinates. We average these responses
over the probe observations and repeated draws, and
select the input with the larger response. The probe
requires only forward evaluations of the frozen model,
without rewards or actor training.
Surprisingly, resampling video noise produces sub-
stantially more variation in the generated actions
than resampling action noise: approximately 6.1×,
5.5×, and 3.3× larger changes on Cosmos3-Edge,
DreamZero-14B, and DreamZero-5B, respectively
(Figure 5). Although it is associated with visual pre-
diction, video noise may offer a more expressive input
for modifying the policy’s behavior. This motivates
our choice to steer video noise in the main WAM ex-
periments. We evaluate whether this greater influence
translates into improved task success in Section 6.

7. Limitations
Noise steering relies on behaviors that the frozen
policy can already produce. Video noise provides a
potentially expressive way to access these behaviors,
but greater action variation does not necessarily trans-
late into greater task success. The sensitivity probe
(Figure 5) measures how much the actions change,
rather than whether those changes help solve the task.
Learning useful control over this variation still re-
quires interaction, and steering cannot directly teach
the frozen model a missing skill.
Our analysis shows why a low-rank subspace can
contain noise inputs that improve the policy, but
does not establish how easily the actor can discover
them. The rank and training required for effective
steering therefore remain empirical choices. Similarly,
preserving the native expected energy is a design
choice, not a guarantee of the optimal noise scale for
every policy: the identity holds for a fresh Gaussian
sample independent of the actor’s output, which the
fixed-draw DreamZero configurations do not satisfy
(Section B.4), and the evaluated Robomimic policies

8. Conclusion
We introduced LoRN, a method for improving frozen
generative robot policies by steering their initial noise
through a low-rank subspace. A small actor controls
the noise through a fixed random projection, while
normalization and Gaussian mixing allow strong steer-
ing at a stable noise scale. This enables steering of
high-dimensional video noise in world-action models,
giving the actor access to an input that can substan-
tially influence the generated actions.
Across three world-action models and ten model–
task pairs, video steering improves average success by
26.2 percentage points over the base policy, compared
with 7.1 points for DSRL action steering. The same
approach supports action-noise steering across several
policy families and transfers from simulation to a
physical robot without hardware training. Together,
these results establish low-rank noise steering as a
practical way to control high-dimensional generative
robot policies, including through video noise that
provides an expressive input for shaping their actions.

A.2. Interpreting the Coverage Bounds
Higher rank makes useful noise easier to reach.
The lower bound on one-step improvement is
𝜏(1 −𝜖𝑟(𝜏)), which can be maximized over 𝜏. A smaller threshold includes more noise inputs in the qualifying
region, while a larger rank reduces the probability of missing that region. For example, if 20% of noise inputs
on the sphere meet the threshold at every state, rank 32 misses at most approximately 0.8% of states with
probability at least 0.9 over the random projection. This follows from Markov’s inequality and is ten times
the expected miss rate in Section 5.2. If 5% of noise inputs meet a stricter threshold 𝜏′, ranks 32 and 64 give
expected one-step improvements of at least approximately 0.81𝜏′ and 0.96𝜏′, respectively. These examples
illustrate the bounds; the corresponding fractions have not been measured on the robot tasks.
Finding useful noise is a separate learning problem.
The propositions characterize the availability of
improving noise inputs within a random subspace. Learning additionally depends on the critic and on the
states visited under the trained actor, whose distribution can depend on 𝑃. The state-averaged coverage
guarantee applies to a projection-independent state distribution; the projection and rank studies examine
learned performance empirically.
A.3. A Fixed Subspace Across an Episode
The projection remains fixed throughout an episode, so the same coefficient vector 𝑢always maps to the
same noise input at full steering strength. Which coefficients are useful can still change with the observation
(Figure 12). The actor therefore learns in a reparameterized MDP whose actions are coefficients in the fixed
subspace. Changing the projection after training changes the noise associated with those coefficients.

C.3. Steering Strength and Noise Structure
Steering strength stays stable as the actor learns.
The parameter 𝜌controls how the actor’s output
norm determines steering strength (Section B.1). In the measured runs, the resulting strength changes little
during training: approximately 0.35 at 𝜌= 1 and 0.56 at 𝜌= 0.6 from initialization to the final checkpoint
(Figure 14a); the tanh-Gaussian fit in the caption of Figure 14 gives 0.32 and 0.54. Mustard success increases
with measured steering strength over the tested range, up to approximately 𝑠= 0.98 (Figure 14b). The
stable strengths help interpret what changes during learning. At these settings, the actor improves its noise
choices without a large shift in how much Gaussian noise is retained. Learning therefore need not operate by
gradually increasing steering strength; it can improve how the available control is used. The success sweep
complements this observation by showing that giving the actor more control is beneficial on Mustard when
the noise scale is maintained.
