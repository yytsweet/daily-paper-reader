Abstract
When humans learn to manipulate an unfamiliar object, they may already be able to anticipate how its state will
change while still being unable to control it skillfully. This gap between prediction and control motivates us to
study how the priors acquired by video generation models can be used effectively by an action policy. We present
ZERITH V2.5, a video–action model (VAM) in which the action policy reads intermediate features of a video
generation model, and use it to study, under fixed demonstrations and a fixed total number of updates, what kind of
predictive training improves subsequent action learning.
We find that the way video predictive training is supervised affects subsequent action learning. In our
experiments, adapting at many noise levels yields a large gain, while adapting only at the noise level used at
deployment yields none. Weight-transfer experiments further show that the gain stays with the adapted weights
when they remain in the video generation model and disappears when they are used only to initialize the action
policy. Initial loss and gradient measurements at the start of joint training indicate that target-domain predictive
training gives the video model a better starting state, making subsequent joint learning of prediction and action
easier. Under matched data and training budgets, predictive adaptation improves control. We evaluate the complete
policy on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, Meta-World MT50, and real-robot tasks.

1 Introduction
When humans learn to manipulate an unfamiliar object, they may already adjust their grip force in
anticipation of upcoming load changes while their hand still deviates from the target path [1]. Prediction
can develop well before skilled control. This motor-learning result suggests a question for video-driven
robot learning: what training does the knowledge acquired by a video generation model need before an
action policy can use it effectively?
Video generation models learn from large-scale visual data how scenes and objects change over time.
Robot learning seeks to use this knowledge to help a policy generate actions from observations and task
instructions. Existing methods train video prediction and action generation jointly [2–5], read intermediate
features of the video model [6–8], or generate future video to support control [9–11]. Predicting how
images change and learning the actions needed to complete a task, however, have different training
objectives. A lower video-prediction error, on its own, says little about how well an action policy can
use what the model has learned. We therefore ask two questions: how should a video generation model
be further trained so that it provides more useful information for action learning, and which part of the
model does the resulting gain come from?
We present ZERITH V2.5 (Figure 1), a video–action model (VAM) built on the Wan2.2 video
generation model [12], and use differences in performance across training conditions to study how video
priors transfer to action ability. The video generation model computes intermediate features from the
current observation; the action policy reads these features and combines them with the task instruction,
the robot state, and geometric information about the current scene to generate actions. Training has
two stages. Stage 1 (S1) further trains the video generation model on target-task video to predict future
changes at different noise levels. Stage 2 (S2) adds action learning and optimizes the video-prediction
and action-generation objectives together. Each layer of the action policy has the same structure as a
layer of the video generation model, so its weights can be copied from either the original or the adapted
model. This lets us vary the initialization of each and test how the gain from predictive training reaches
the control task.
In controlled experiments, we fix the demonstrations and the total number of updates and compare two
recipes: learning prediction and action jointly from the start (direct training), and spending the first 3,000
of 30,000 updates on video predictive training before joint learning. Across three geometric-conditioning
settings with three training seeds each, the second recipe raises LIBERO-Plus success by 17.55 to 19.16
percentage points (Section 6.3). The advantage holds at every evaluated budget from 30,000 to 50,000
updates, and direct training at 50,000 updates still scores below the staged recipe at 30,000 (Section 6.5).
To find where this gain comes from, we vary the initializations of the video generation model and the
action policy separately. When the video generation model uses the weights after target-domain predictive
training while the action policy is still initialized from the corresponding layers of the original pretrained
model, LIBERO-Plus success is 19.67 points higher than with direct training. Conversely, when the video
generation model starts from the original weights and only the action policy is initialized from the adapted
layers, success is 3.94 points below direct training (Section 7.1). These results suggest that the control
gain comes mainly from adapting the video generation model to the target domain rather than from the
action policy’s initialization.
The noise setting of predictive training also matters. Training across a range of noise levels gives
higher control success. Holding the future inputs at pure noise throughout matches the input seen at
deployment, yet success is 20.80 points lower (Section 7.2). This suggests that the range of noise levels
covered in training matters, and that matching the deployment condition is not enough. Two factors
therefore shape the outcome: the predictive supervision the video generation model receives, and the
module that receives the adapted weights. A further control shows that action supervision remains useful
to the video generation model once joint learning begins: blocking action gradients into it throughout
direct training lowers success below standard direct training.

To explain these differences, we analyze the predictive objective and the state of the model when
action learning begins. A theoretical analysis shows that, for the predictive objective used here, different
noise levels impose different predictive constraints (Appendix A.1). With the action-policy initialization
held fixed, target-domain predictive training reduces the prediction loss of the video generation model
33.9-fold and its gradient norm by 95.2%, while the gradient that the action loss sends into the video
model keeps 96.0% of its magnitude (Section 8.1). We hypothesize that predicting the target scene well
before action learning begins gives joint training a better starting point, and eases the burden of adapting
both models to the target task at once.
At deployment, the action policy reads intermediate features of the video generation model directly,
without fully decoding future video frames, and the features from one forward pass are reused across
all steps of the action solver. To evaluate the complete system, we pretrain on 10,000 hours of cleaned
and selected real-robot data and then adapt to downstream tasks. A single checkpoint of the complete
system reaches 98.95% on LIBERO and 93.70% on LIBERO-Plus (Section 5.1), and the complete policy
reaches 92.3% on Meta-World MT50. On a real robot, it succeeds in 97 of 100 pick-and-place trials and 8
of 20 bin-lining trials, matching or exceeding 𝜋0.5 and Fast-WAM on every task (Section 5.2). These
system evaluations test the method as a whole, while the controlled experiments with fixed data and
update budgets identify the training conditions under which predictive priors transfer.
In summary, this report makes three contributions:
• Training conditions for transferring video priors to control. With fixed demonstrations and a
fixed total number of updates, we compare training recipes, verify the control gain of target-domain
video predictive adaptation, and examine the gain across geometric-conditioning settings and
training budgets (Sections 6.1 and 6.3 to 6.5).
• Source of the adaptation gain and the optimization starting point. Branch-initialization and
noise-coverage controls separate the role of where the adapted weights are used from the role of the
predictive supervision. Together with a theoretical analysis and diagnostics of initial losses and
gradients, they offer mechanistic clues to the differences in subsequent action learning (Sections 7.1,
7.2 and 8.1 and Appendix A).
• A robot control system built on reusable predictive memory. The video predictive representation,
current-scene geometry, and action readout are organized as separate modules, and one pass of the
video branch supports a multi-step action solve. With pretraining on 10,000 hours of real-robot data,
the complete system is evaluated on simulation benchmarks and real-robot tasks (Sections 2 and 5).
2 Model Architecture
2.1 Overview
ZERITH V2.5 keeps the video generation model and the action policy as separate modules that
communicate only through exported attention keys and values, which is what makes the staged recipe of
Section 3 possible. Because this interface decouples visual dynamics modeling from action readout, the
video generation model can first be adapted alone to ground its representation in the robot’s visual domain
before any policy parameters are introduced. At both Stage 2 and deployment, a single forward pass of the
video generation model supplies the memory queried by the action policy, without fully decoding future
video frames. Moreover, because each policy block mirrors the transformer architecture of its source
video-model block, the two branches can be initialized independently from either original or adapted
video weights (Section 7.1). At each decision step, ZERITH V2.5 takes images from up to three camera
views, a natural-language instruction, and the robot’s proprioceptive state to predict a 16-step action
chunk, executing a configurable number of steps before replanning; the reported deployment executes the
first four (Figure 2).

Perceptual inputs are encoded by frozen pretrained networks: the Wan2.2 video autoencoder extracts
visual latents, its umT5 encoder embeds the language instruction, and the VGGT aggregator [13] provides
current-frame geometric features. Three trainable modules then map these inputs to control: a 30-block
Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video generation model [12] that processes the current visual latent alongside four future
latent slots, a 10-block flow-matching action policy [14] that attends to keys and values exported from ten
video-model blocks, and a lightweight geometry pathway that projects current-frame VGGT features into
static geometry tokens and a multiplicative residual on the exported future values. The trainable model
contains approximately 7B parameters.
2.2 Attention in the Video Generation Model
Each camera view is encoded independently and placed into a designated slot within a single composite
latent frame, distinguished by a learned camera embedding. The video generation model processes the
current frame (ℎ= 0) together with four future latent slots under a frame-causal attention mask: tokens in
the current frame attend only to the current frame, while tokens in each future slot attend to their own frame
and all preceding frames. Within any given frame, tokens from different cameras attend to one another
without restriction, allowing every viewpoint to be contextualized by the others (Section 8.1 quantifies
the contribution of this cross-camera interaction). For fixed model weights, timestep conditioning, text,
and attention masks, this block-triangular mask makes the current-frame representation independent of
the contents of future slots. Replacing only those contents by pure Gaussian noise therefore leaves the
current-frame features unchanged. At deployment, the video generation model executes a single forward
pass per decision and terminates after block 24, the deepest layer tapped by the action policy; the final five
blocks and the visual-velocity output head are needed for the visual-loss computation and are omitted
from the deployed forward pass.

3.1 Stage 1: Visual Adaptation
S1 keeps the current-frame latent clean and trains the video generation model to predict velocities for
valid future visual patches. The four future slots share one noise level, drawn as 𝜎𝑤= 0 with probability
0.1 and 𝜎𝑤∼Unif(0, 1) otherwise, while the Gaussian noise itself is sampled independently for each
slot. At 𝜎𝑤= 0 the future inputs are clean and the target remains the velocity 𝜖−𝑥★. S1 trains the video
generation model alone: the action policy and the geometry pathways do not yet exist. We refer to this
distribution as the broad-noise schedule.
The schedule is intentionally broader than the operating point at which the backbone is later read. S2
and deployment evaluate the video generation model only at 𝜎𝑤= 1, yet S1 covers the entire interval from
clean to fully noised. The reason is that different noise levels supervise different aspects of the future:
the population-optimal output at the fully noised endpoint depends on the conditional mean, whereas
intermediate levels depend on posteriors across a range of signal-to-noise ratios (Appendix A.1). The
fixed-noise control of Section 7.2 compares this schedule directly against adaptation at the deployed
endpoint.

3.4 Budget Allocation and Crossover Model
The standard endpoint controls compare schedules at 30,000 total downstream updates on the same
demonstrations. In this comparison, staged training allocates the first 3,000 to S1 and the remaining
27,000 to S2; direct training allocates all 30,000 to joint updates. The two schedules use the same
total number of updates, separating the effect of the staged recipe from that of additional training. The
recipe differs from direct training in its ordering, its S1 noise distribution, and the absence of the policy
during S1. Because S1 omits the action policy and the geometry pathways, it processes fewer input
tokens per update than joint training, and Appendix A.8 quantifies the resulting reduction in action-token
processing. Learning curves also report matched S2 updates, budget sweeps vary total updates, and
additional robot-data pretraining is accounted for separately.

4.2 Downstream Data and Benchmarks
LIBERO downstream training uses 1,693 demonstrations from the OpenVLA-modified LIBERO
dataset [19, 20], covering 40 tasks; every schedule and initialization draws from this same set. The
smaller dataset used in the adaptation ablation contains 13,259 ABC-130K episodes and 1,693 LIBERO
demonstrations; it is identified as the mixed dataset in Section 6.2. For the controlled LIBERO studies,
evaluation comprises 2,000 episodes, 500 per suite. Our controlled LIBERO-Plus [21] evaluations use a
fixed manifest of 10,030 scenarios spanning seven perturbation categories. Baseline scores in Section 5.1
may use different protocols. The two benchmarks play complementary roles. Standard LIBERO evaluates
the training distribution, on which strong policies exceed 95%, whereas LIBERO-Plus perturbs object
layouts, camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting conditions, background
textures, and sensor noise and discriminates between methods across the full range of scores reported
here; the effects of interest are accordingly read primarily from LIBERO-Plus. Meta-World MT50 [22]
evaluates a shared policy across 50 manipulation tasks. We use the episode-success criterion in the official
evaluation protocol [23]: an episode succeeds if the environment success flag is reached at any step. The
reported MT50 score pools success over evaluation episodes.
4.3 Training and Evaluation Protocol
We use global batch size 320 and AdamW with (𝛽1, 𝛽2) = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.01, unit-norm
gradient clipping, and no gradient accumulation. A cosine learning-rate schedule uses 5% warmup and
decays to 10% of the peak rate. The released implementation assigns peak rates of 10−5 to the video
generation model and policy blocks, and 10−4 to the geometry bridge, binding projections and gates, and
non-block policy modules, including the action heads.
Table 3 summarizes the experimental groups. We separate the static-geometry training comparison,
the mixed-data training routes, and the schedule–operator interaction. The branch-initialization study
varies video-model and policy initialization independently. Dataset and static-training comparisons
use static geometry; branch-initialization, noise, routing, learning-curve, and budget-sweep runs use
multiplicative conditioning. Geometry-operator ablations use a reference initialization and a small-corpus
robot-pretrained initialization, each compared with its own static-geometry baseline. Initialization probes

On one H800 at batch size one, mean decision latency is 331.25 ms and the 95th percentile is 365.30
ms. The measured planning rate is 3.02 plans per second. Executing four action steps per plan corresponds
to an effective rate of 12.08 action steps per second. Total latency decomposes as
𝑇infer = 𝑇enc + 𝑇world + 𝑁Euler𝑇policy + 𝑇other,
(10)
where the terms denote encoding, one video-model forward pass, 𝑁Euler = 10 policy evaluations, and
other inference operations. The decomposition shows the effect of reading intermediate features instead
of fully decoding frames. The video-model pass enters once per decision and is amortized across all ten
solver steps and then across four executed actions, so the cost of the large module is incurred once per
plan, and the term that scales with the number of solver steps is that of the ten-layer policy. The expression
contains no frame-decoding term, because the action path reads features directly. The 95th-percentile
latency exceeds the mean by 10.3%.

7.3 Geometry and Policy-Interface Ablations
This study fixes the training procedure within each initialization background and varies the geometry
operator or policy interface. The schedule–operator interaction is evaluated separately in Section 6.3. We
use two fixed checkpoint settings: reference initialization and small-corpus robot-pretrained initialization.
Within each setting, all variants use the same initial checkpoint, LIBERO target data, training procedure,
and 30,000 downstream updates, and are compared with that setting’s static-geometry baseline (Table 16
and Figure 12). Static geometry retains memory (𝐾𝑔,𝑉𝑔) while setting the residual 𝐹(𝑔, 𝑑) = 0. Other
rows change 𝐹; the no-geometry row also removes the geometry memory. This separates the presence
of geometry information from the form of its coupling. The product improves LIBERO-Plus by 0.78
points in the reference setting and 1.22 points in the robot-pretrained setting. Removing geometry in the
reference setting changes success by −0.21 points; this difference is small, and the three-seed mean has
no reported SD.

three nonlinear variants remain within 0.20 points of it, at 67.89%, 67.90%, and 67.72%. The product
improves LIBERO-Plus by 1.22 points relative to both the static and additive baselines. Among the tested
forms, these mean results favor the product. The zero-factor property in Section 2 motivates this form.
Standard LIBERO varies little across the six robot-pretrained variants, from 97.58% to 97.87%,
and on this suite multiplicative conditioning is 0.15 points below the static baseline. The geometry
variants separate more on the perturbation suite. The two policy-interface choices have small effects on
the standard suite (Figure 13). The deployed middle-to-late layer selection reaches 97.05%, compared

comparison per choice, these small differences cannot rank policy interfaces in general.

8.3 Interpretation
S1 and S2 specialize the pretrained prior toward different objectives. S1 learns to predict target-domain
visual change across noise levels; S2 learns to extract action-relevant information from the resulting
representation while continuing to receive visual supervision. We refer to these as visual specialization
and action-oriented specialization. The probes of Section 8.1 are consistent with the distinction: after S1
the visual residual and its gradient are small while the action-derived gradient into the same parameters is
nearly unchanged. The attention statistics of Section 8.2 provide representational evidence for it, with
entropy falling in both branches and attention shifting between token groups. The branch-initialization
study shows what this means in practice: the branch that benefits from a history of visual specialization is
the video generation model, and the policy attains its highest perturbation-suite score when initialized
from the original weights.
The measurements of Section 8.1 establish what holds at the adapted state 𝐶of Section 3.4: a visual
loss reduced 33.9-fold, an action-derived gradient retained at 96.0%, and a parameter displacement of
1.675%. Appendix A offers possible accounts of why 𝐶is a better starting point for joint training and
why its weights favor the video generation model (Appendices A.1 to A.3), and when the cost of reaching
𝐶is recovered (Appendices A.4 to A.9). Two of these analyses relate to the experiments.
The noise-level analysis of Appendix A.1 offers a possible interpretation of the fixed-noise control of
Section 7.2. Supervising only at 𝜎𝑤= 1 trains the video generation model on a functional, the conditional
mean, that a deterministic future and a multimodal future can share; supervising across 𝜎trains it on
a family of posterior means that distinguishes them. This motivates the hypothesis that broad-noise
supervision may be useful when futures are uncertain. Testing it would require results by perturbation
category; the 20.80-point gap between the two noise schedules is an aggregate LIBERO-Plus comparison.
The tracking bound of Appendix A.2 identifies distinct roles for the two initializations. The bound
separates the initial tracking error 𝑒0 from subsequent video-model parameter drift. Both initializations
can affect these quantities, so the best choice for one branch may depend on the other. It also separates
three ways in which S1 could accelerate joint training: by improving the starting point, by slowing the
motion of the target, or by making the action objective easier to descend through a larger local curvature
𝜇. The initialization probes measure losses and raw gradients; policy-optimum distances, subsequent
drift, and local curvature remain unmeasured.
9 Related Work
Video–Action Models
Vision–language–action (VLA) models and video–action models (VAMs) are
two prominent foundation-model approaches to generalist robot control. VLA policies inherit semantic
representations from vision–language pretraining [20, 30], whereas VAMs connect predictive video
representations to action learning [31]. Earlier work learned behavior through latent dynamics models [32,
33] or future-measurement prediction [34], and human video supplied pretrained visual representations [35].
Video generators have also served as visual planners [9,36], interactive simulators [37–39], and synthetic
data engines [40]. Building on video-based policies such as GR-1 and GR-2 [41,42], recent video-based
policies initialize from web-scale video generators [4, 5, 10, 27, 43], pretrain on robot or egocentric
video [8,44–46], train generative and control components from scratch on manipulation data [11], or
unify world modeling and control in an omnimodal model [47]. Their action interfaces include deriving
actions from generated future states [9–11], planning with predicted states and values [27], and jointly
27

TECHNICAL REPORT
predicting video and actions [2–5,43,48]. Another route reads internal video features without decoding
future frames for control. Diffusion models provide features for recognition and correspondence [49–51],
while representation alignment can improve generative training [52]. In robot policies, VPP extracts
predictive features in a single pass [6]; EnerVerse and DiT4DiT use first-step or intermediate denoising
features [53,54]; Genie Envisioner decodes robot-video latents into actions [44]; and InternW0 reuses
layerwise keys and values [8]. Fast-WAM and GigaWorld-Policy retain future-video supervision during
training while decoding only actions at deployment [7, 45, 46]. Related work improves this interface
through reused future representations [55], shallow action modules [56], latent future memory [57],
asynchronous context reuse [58], and distilled geometric priors [59]. ZERITH V2.5 uses an action policy
over intermediate video-model keys and values. Its contribution concerns the state of this representation
when action learning begins, complementing work on the readout interface.
Policy Training and Adaptation
Coordinating a pretrained backbone with a newly attached action
module is a central training choice. In VLA systems, 𝜋0.5 introduces a flow-matching expert after
discrete-action pretraining [25]; knowledge insulation limits the expert’s gradients into the backbone [60];
and Wall-OSS-0.5 and GigaBrain-0.7 jointly optimize vision–language and action objectives in one
stage [61, 62].
Video-based policies likewise adapt predictive models before or alongside action
learning [6,42,44,45,63]. DreamGen specializes video models before generating policy-training data [40];
UVA reports video pretraining followed by video–action co-training [2]; and Motus and GE-Act 2.0 use
multi-stage pipelines [11,48]. Video Policy compares staged and joint schedules, but gives the staged
configuration additional updates [64]. Controlled studies further distinguish benefits on familiar tasks from
robustness under distribution shift [65], associate pretrained representation geometry with downstream
robustness [66], and find that robot specialization improves prediction and control in their evaluated
settings [67]. These questions connect to intermediate domain adaptation [68,69], staged video-model
training [70], and the ordering of training signals [71]. Transfer studies also examine task divergence [72],
feature distortion when a new head and backbone are fine-tuned together [73], and gradient scaling between
pretrained and new components [74]. For diffusion and flow models, adaptation additionally depends on
the allocation of supervision across noise levels: EDM and Stable Diffusion 3 study noise distributions
and weighting [75,76], while Min-SNR and P2 reweight denoising losses for optimization or perceptual
learning [77,78]. This differs from choosing the noise level at which a policy reads features, as in DiT4DiT
and UWM [3,54]. ZERITH V2.5 studies these choices at matched demonstrations and total updates. It
adapts the video generation model across the full noise range before joint training, although the policy
reads it only at 𝜎𝑤= 1. In our controls, endpoint-only adaptation does not reproduce the gain, and the
gain follows the adapted video-model weights. The analysis in Appendix A.1 relates intermediate-noise
supervision to posterior structure beyond the endpoint’s conditional mean. Our schedule also restores
action gradients after adaptation: withholding them throughout joint training performs worse than direct
training in the evaluated setting.
Action Prediction and Conditioning
Robot policies differ in both how they generate actions and
what information conditions them. Vision–language–action systems transfer pretrained multimodal
knowledge to control [20, 24, 25, 30, 61, 62, 79–84], alongside generalist controllers trained on robot
trajectories without vision–language initialization [85–87]. Large multi-embodiment datasets support
these policies [17,88]. RT-2 and OpenVLA decode discrete action tokens [20,30], and FAST compresses
action sequences before tokenization [89]. CogACT uses a diffusion action module [90], following
diffusion policies for visuomotor control [91]; 𝜋0 and 𝜋0.5 use flow-matching experts [24, 25]; and
GR00T N1 combines a vision–language model with a diffusion transformer [80]. OpenVLA-OFT studies
parallel decoding, action chunking, and other fine-tuning choices [26]. Predictive conditioning includes
latent actions learned from video [92,93], future observations or subgoal images [79,94,95], aligned
future embeddings [96], and foresight tokens supervised by a frozen video generator [97]. ZERITH V2.5

10 Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Directions
10.1 Conclusion
This report asked what training a video generation model needs before an action policy can use its
predictive priors effectively, with the demonstrations and the total number of updates held fixed. Across
controlled comparisons, two conditions matter: how predictive training is supervised, and which module
receives the adapted weights. ZERITH V2.5 summarizes the resulting recipe as adapt the visual world,
then scale the action policy. Under a matched 30,000-update budget on the same demonstrations, devoting
the first 3,000 updates to video-model-only adaptation improves LIBERO-Plus success by 17.55–19.16
percentage points across three geometric-conditioning variants with three seeds each, compared with
0.68–2.29 points from changing the operator within the same factorial study. The advantage also extends
beyond a single endpoint. At matched Stage 2 update counts after the first few thousand updates, the
policy built on the adapted video generation model leads direct training by 8 to 10 points at every recorded
checkpoint; on the total-update axis, the staged schedule at 30,000 updates remains 18.18 points ahead of
direct training at 50,000 updates.
Ablations and initialization probes show where this gain comes from and what it depends on. Crossing
the two initialization sources shows that the benefit moves specifically with the adapted video-model
weights: transferring them to the video generation model alone adds 19.67 points on LIBERO-Plus,
whereas copying adapted layers into the action policy alone lowers success by 3.94 points. Two further
controls narrow these findings: restricting Stage 1 adaptation to the pure-noise endpoint read by the
policy, or withholding action gradients from the video generation model throughout direct training, each
reduces success below standard direct training. These results support the roles of broad-noise adaptation
and action-gradient feedback in the evaluated configurations. At the start of Stage 2, a 1.675% relative
displacement of the video-model weights has reduced the visual-loss gradient norm by 95.2% while
retaining 96.0% of the action-loss gradient into the video generation model, a starting state we hypothesize
makes subsequent action updates more effective. With pretraining on 10,000 hours of cleaned and selected
robot data, ZERITH V2.5 reaches 98.95% on standard LIBERO and 93.70% on LIBERO-Plus, and on a
real robot it matches or exceeds the compared baselines on pick and place and bin lining. For the setting
we evaluated, the findings suggest three practical guidelines for video–action policies: adapt the video
generation model to the target visual domain across the full noise range before introducing the action
policy; initialize the video generation model and the action policy independently, assigning the adapted
weights to the video generation model; and allow action gradients to refine the video-model representation
during joint training.

Scope.
All controlled comparisons use one video generation model (Wan2.2-TI2V-5B), one adaptation
length (𝜏= 3,000 updates), and the LIBERO benchmark family. Whether the gain carries over to other
backbones, task domains, or adaptation lengths is untested, so the guidelines above apply to this setting.
The real-robot evaluation compares complete systems on two tasks, and real-robot trials involve some

On LIBERO-Plus, staged training trails direct training at 5,000 total updates and

10,000-update checkpoint by only 0.14 points, and direct training was not evaluated at 8,000 updates.
Placing the crossover in the 5,000–8,000-update interval therefore assumes that the direct score at 8,000
updates does not exceed its 10,000-update score. The broader 5,000–13,000-update interval (1.7𝜏–4.3𝜏)
also rests on unmeasured equal-budget points. Update savings relative to 50,000 direct updates refer only
to the evaluated budgets.
Mechanism.
The initialization probes describe only the state at the start of Stage 2. The claim that this
state makes action updates more effective remains a hypothesis; testing it would require an intervention.
The analyses in Appendix A are local or illustrative models, and no parameter of the optimal-length model
is fitted to data. Appendix E lists seed counts, statistical precision, and the remaining limitations by topic.
10.3 Future Directions
We see two directions for future work. The first concerns generality: evaluating staged visual grounding
across additional video backbones and task families, and measuring how the budget crossover and
downstream gain vary with the adaptation length 𝜏, would test how broadly the recipe applies and turn the
trade-off analysis of Appendix A.7 into a practical rule for selecting 𝜏. The second concerns multi-task
reuse: the amortization threshold of Equation (34) indicates that the cost of Stage 1 adaptation can be
shared across many downstream policies, making a domain-adapted video generation model a natural
candidate for a shared, task-independent foundation in multi-task robot learning.

Appendix B
Pretraining Data Details
B.1 Cleaning and Selection Overview
The pretraining corpus consists of real-robot recordings selected after cleaning: 6,000 hours of ZERITH
H1 data and 4,000 hours of public data, totaling 10,000 hours (Table 2). The following sections describe
cleaning and repair; their intermediate processing statistics are distinct from the final selected training
volumes.
The dataset inventory, preprocessing output, and final training selection use different units and scopes.
An ingested MCAP file is counted as one data record, while cleaned hours measure the retained recording
duration. Multiple camera streams are components of a record. Counts and hours are reported separately
because filtering, temporal trimming, and repair can change duration without a proportional change in
record count.
B.2 Quality-Control and Repair Procedure
Processing consists of record parsing, quality checks, repair, conversion to a common training format, and
joint filtering. Checks examine stationary segments, within-trajectory stationary intervals, non-image
stream gaps, camera frame loss, and timestamp consistency. Most checks assign pass, repairable,
or error; camera checks use a binary result. A non-image gap of at most 14 frames is classified as
repairable, whereas a longer gap is rejected under the reported rule.
Repair operations include end-effector coordinate alignment, temporal trimming, interpolation of
eligible gaps, and forward-kinematics recomputation. ZERITH H1 kinematics are expressed in a common
body reference frame. Gripper commands are converted from source-specific normalized or metric
representations to a consistent convention. Additional diagnostics inspect end-effector jerk and joint
singularities. Final filtering combines quality labels by record identifier. The per-check percentages
describe overlapping sets of records.

Appendix C
Implementation Details
The video-model transformer uses width 3072 and 24 attention heads of dimension 128; policy blocks
match this configuration. LIBERO inputs use front and wrist camera views, while the real robot uses head
and left/right wrist views. Robot joint and gripper states carry per-dimension validity masks.
C.1 Gradient Computation and Synchronization
Gradient norms, clipping, and finite-value checks are computed over the optimizer’s parameter groups
rather than over the model’s parameters. Under sharded mixed-precision training this choice is what
makes the norm exact: the framework synchronizes each gradient and moves it into an FP32 shard owned
by the optimizer, so the optimizer groups are the place where the complete applied gradient resides.
Clipping and telemetry operate on optimizer-managed gradients, not on the AdamW parameter update
itself. The released trainer records its global norm before clipping; this does not establish the aggregation

or clipping convention of the separate probes in Section 8.1. Finite-value checks are fused into the global
norm reduction. A non-finite local loss or norm is encoded as NaN before the single sum-reduction that
computes the norm; because NaN propagates through the sum, every worker observes the same non-finite
total and raises a synchronized error before the optimizer step; the failed update is not applied.
The routing schedule is defined relative to the S2 update count, so its absolute length scales with
the stage. A 30,000-update S2 run uses 1,500 updates at 𝛽= 0 followed by a 1,500-update ramp, and a
27,000-update S2 run uses 1,350 updates for each interval. Visual-loss gradients remain active throughout;
the schedule governs only the action gradient’s access to the video generation model, and the video
generation model continues to learn from its visual objective during the interval at 𝛽= 0.
C.2 Precision, Masks, and Feature Caching
The backbone runs in BF16, while action normalization, statistics, and time-sensitive operations remain
in FP32, so the quantities that define the action space are never accumulated at reduced precision. Invalid
action dimensions, missing camera slots, and invalid state entries are represented as zeros paired with false
validity masks. False validity masks exclude entries from the losses and attention operations to which
they are applied; zero filling alone does not make an entry inert in every computation. Finite placeholders
avoid propagating NaN or infinity into losses or normalizers. The losses exclude masked entries, and
every model input is verified to be finite. Geometry features are precomputed under a fixed sampling seed
and read from a cache, so the frozen geometry encoder is not re-executed during training. This fixes the
cached features but does not guarantee identical numerical behavior across runs. The configuration fits in
accelerator memory directly, so it runs with gradient checkpointing, CPU offload, and graph compilation
disabled.
C.3 Monitoring and Reproducibility
Training health is assessed from four signals read together: per-module gradient norms, the visual and
action losses reported separately, the full pure-noise sampling error, and closed-loop success. Each signal
places the others in context. A gradient norm is recorded together with whether it precedes or follows
clipping, since the two differ by construction once clipping is active, and action errors are expressed in
normalized action coordinates. Closed-loop success is the primary criterion, because the ten-step solved
trajectory that the controller executes is a different object from a one-step reconstruction of the same
target. The noise comparison of Section 7.2 illustrates this: both S1 variants recover recognizable scene
structure in the frame-16 examples, yet their reported rollout success differs by 20.80 points.
Each run binds its checkpoint, normalization statistics, dataset path, source revision, device configura-
tion, and batch size into a frozen record, and both checkpoint restoration and export verify that record
directly. Export compares the model’s codec statistics with the target statistics buffer by buffer and accepts
a checkpoint only when every buffer matches. These checks verify the recorded fields on restoration and
export; they do not establish equality of unrecorded run settings or eliminate all numerical variation.

E.1 Statistical Precision
The staging, dataset, and geometry-operator studies report three-seed means, and their principal paired
comparisons report the sample SD of seed-wise differences. SD bars describe variability across seeds.
The branch-initialization, noise-control, learning-curve, budget-sweep, attention, and pretraining results
are single runs: they carry scenario-level sampling error, but we have no estimate of their seed-to-seed
variation. On the 10,030-scenario LIBERO-Plus manifest, the binomial standard error of a difference
between two independent scores near 40% is about 0.7 points. This figure treats the two runs as independent
samples; a paired test would need per-scenario outcomes, which we do not report. At this precision, the
0.51-point LIBERO-Plus advantage of 𝑅0 over 𝑅1 at 𝑊1 (𝑧≈0.7) is within noise, and standard LIBERO
favors 𝑅1 by 0.65 points (𝑧≈1.0). We deploy (𝑊1, 𝑅0) because it has the higher recorded LIBERO-Plus
score.
After S1, the multiplicative-versus-static difference is 0.68 ± 0.30 points (mean ± paired SD), and
the staging–conditioning interaction has no SD of its own. The 4.6–6.1-fold ratio between effects on
LIBERO-Plus and standard LIBERO is a ratio of point estimates. Policy-interface comparisons combine
a single deployed score with three-seed alternatives. Comparisons are made within each experimental
group and pretraining background, and groups with similar baselines are kept separate. The mixed-data
extensions change both the objective and the pretraining exposure, so their 4.12-point difference mixes
the effect of action supervision with that of extra compute.

E.3 Theoretical Models
Appendix A states the assumptions of each analysis alongside it. This subsection lists the quantities those
analyses rely on that we do not measure, and what follows from them.
Noise coverage. The noise-level analysis concerns population optima. It shows that the optimal
output at 𝜎𝑤= 1 depends only on the conditional mean; whether endpoint-trained features carry only
that information, and whether broad-noise training improves the policy’s representation, are separate
questions. Our S1 distribution has no atom at 𝜎𝑤= 1, so the endpoint statement applies to it only by
continuity. 𝐽𝜎and 𝐺𝑝are unmeasured. We report no per-category LIBERO-Plus breakdown, so the
prediction that broad noise helps most on samples with uncertain futures remains untested.
Tracking and transfer. The tracking bound assumes local strong convexity, smoothness, bounded
mixed curvature, and deterministic gradient descent, whereas the policy is trained with AdamW. The
constants 𝜇, 𝐿, 𝑀, and 𝐾and the video-model parameter drift during S2 are unmeasured. Raw gradient
norms do not set AdamW step sizes, so the 95.2% reduction in the initial visual-loss gradient need not
translate into a 95.2% reduction in video-model updating. The branch-transfer analysis is exact only
where the policy objective is quadratic around both 𝑅0 and 𝑅1; 𝑔𝑖, 𝐻𝑖, and 𝐻𝑟𝑤are unmeasured, and the
interpolation optimum 𝜆∗is derived but not evaluated. The spectral account of the policy-only result fits
two observations, the initial action loss and the final success rate, and other mechanisms could fit them as
well.
Budget and cost models. The linear crossover model assumes constant stage-wise rates; the observed
plateau and narrowing depart from it over the full budget range. Setting 𝑟𝐴𝐶= 0 assumes a control metric
that is defined during policy-free S1, and we evaluate no such probe. The rate ratio of 1.6–2.5 and limiting
saving of 37.5–60% follow from the 5,000–8,000-update interval; the 5,000–13,000-update interval gives
1.3–2.5 and 23–60%. The optimal-length model is illustrative: no parameter is fitted and only 𝜏= 3,000
is evaluated, so whether 3,000 updates is close to optimal remains open. The per-update cost ratio 𝜌is
untimed, so the 33.3–43.3% and 20.0–26.0% cost ranges are model consequences of measured update
counts rather than measured GPU time. The token identity counts transformer input tokens only, and we
did not run the equal-token comparison of Equation (31). The reuse threshold assumes that one adapted
video generation model serves every downstream task equally well; with a single adaptation domain
evaluated, cross-task amortization remains a property of the cost model.

E.4 Mechanistic Interpretation
Three interpretations in the main text fit the measurements but have not been tested by intervention: that
copied S1 layers carry a visual-prediction specialization that does not suit the policy, that S2 begins in a
state in which action updates are more effective, and that the larger relative response to cross-camera
masking reflects the value of cross-view information. “Specialization” here means functional specialization
only; testing it would require representation-level probes across checkpoints. The initialization probes use
eight batches at the start of S2 and describe only that point. The near-zero gradient cosines argue against
gradient conflict as the mechanism of the staging effect.
The fixed-noise S1 and full-isolation direct controls are separate single-run comparisons. Broad-noise
S1 followed by fully isolated S2, and intermediate noise distributions, are not evaluated, so we cannot
say whether each ingredient is independently necessary. The larger video-model adaptation effect on
LIBERO-Plus compares aggregate suite scores, without a breakdown by perturbation category. On
standard LIBERO, mixed-data S1 ranks above target S1 and multiplicative conditioning is slightly negative,
so those conclusions apply to the perturbation suite.
Attention statistics are aggregated over the tapped layers and three denoising steps without per-layer
or per-anchor dispersion, so their differences have no intervals. Attention mass measures where the policy
looks, which need not be what the action prediction depends on. The 51.8% drop in geometry attention
and the staging–conditioning interaction come from different interventions. Establishing causal roles
would require masking or permuting action tokens, past frames, or dynamic-video tokens under matched
inputs. The decoded frames are offline single-horizon examples.
The policy-only result also has a practical implication: a checkpoint that is better on the visual
objective can still be a poor initialization for another module. Its explanation in Appendix A.3 remains a
hypothesis.
E.5 Data and Implementation
Data statistics come from the processing pipeline and were not re-derived from raw recordings. The
selected data volumes do not identify which data each checkpoint consumed, and the effect of individual
repair operations on policy success is unmeasured.
Several choices in Sections 2 and 3 and Appendix C rest on design reasoning rather than ablation. Layer
selection was compared across three configurations, with differences of 0.15–0.20 points, and we report
no per-layer representation probe. The length of the 𝛽= 0 interval and the ramp of the routing schedule
are fixed by design, and we do not measure when the policy begins to produce useful gradients. Retaining
L𝑤in S2 is never compared against dropping it. Whether the binding gates move away from zero during
training is unreported. The precision split, masking convention, disabled memory optimizations, and
norm computation over optimizer groups were each chosen without comparing alternatives. Run records
verify the recorded configuration but cannot capture every source of variation, such as nondeterministic
kernels. All runs use four nodes with eight accelerators each and no gradient accumulation; because data
quotas are tied to the global batch size, a single-node schedule with accumulation may behave differently.
