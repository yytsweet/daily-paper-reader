Abstract
World-action models jointly predict future observations and
actions, yet reconstruction-oriented visual representations
do not explicitly emphasize the spatial relationships and
object-state changes needed for manipulation. We intro-
duce Phoenix, which learns representations of scene struc-
ture for world-action modeling. Specifically, Phoenix En-
coder enriches DINOv3’s general visual semantics through
dual-teacher distillation of geometric and spatiotemporal
semantic knowledge.
Next, CS-VAE causally aggregates
and compresses Phoenix Encoder features into genera-
tive latents while preserving scene information and spa-
tial layout.
Finally, Phoenix jointly predicts these la-
tents, complementary appearance latents, and robot ac-
tions, linking scene evolution with action generation. To
study pretraining data efficiency, we curate approximately
39,000 hours of egocentric and robot manipulation data
for representation learning and use the robot demonstra-
tion subset for WAM pretraining. Following benchmark-
specific post-training, Phoenix achieves state-of-the-art
performance across simulation and real-robot benchmarks
spanning single-arm, bimanual, and dexterous manipu-
lation.
It demonstrates strong robustness to perturba-
tions, achieving success rates of 94.8% on LIBERO-Plus
and 72.4% on RoboTwin 2.0 C2R, alongside strong cross-
embodiment transfer and real-world manipulation perfor-
mance on Bench2Dex and RoboDojo-RealWorld, respec-
tively. Pretraining-scale analysis shows larger performance
gains from data scaling than a video-VAE-only baseline, in-
dicating stronger scaling benefits and more effective data
utilization. Moreover, Phoenix maintains high success rates
when adapted with limited downstream demonstrations,
demonstrating strong few-shot adaptation. Our code, pre-
training data, and models are available on the project page
to help the community reproduce our results.

1. Introduction
World–action models (WAMs) jointly predict future obser-
vations and robot actions, linking environment modeling
with control [27, 56, 64]. They learn from large-scale vi-
sual experience to develop manipulation skills that general-
ize across scenes, object configurations, and execution con-
ditions [13, 29, 34]. This requires visual representations that
support both environment prediction and policy learning.
In WAMs, visual representations determine how ob-
servations are encoded and what the model predicts.
Manipulation-centered representations of scene structure

should capture spatial organization, visual appearance, and
motion. Latents learned for video reconstruction preserve
rich visual information, but reconstruction fidelity alone
does not ensure that they capture manipulation-relevant
structure and its relationship to actions [4, 48, 73].
Re-
cent work on representation alignment and generation with
visual foundation model features shows that visual under-
standing complements generative modeling [45, 68, 77].
We therefore investigate how geometric and spatiotempo-
ral semantic knowledge from these models [39, 44, 49] can
be used to build scene representations for joint prediction of
future states and actions.
We introduce Phoenix, a WAM that learns generative
representations of scene structure. Phoenix Encoder builds
on DINOv3’s general visual semantics and learns per-frame
scene features through dual-teacher distillation of geometric
and spatiotemporal semantic knowledge. CS-VAE aggre-
gates Phoenix Encoder features over causal temporal win-
dows and compresses them into generative latents, preserv-
ing scene information and spatial layout. Phoenix jointly
predicts these scene latents, complementary appearance la-
tents, and robot actions, learning how actions relate to scene
changes. To study pretraining data efficiency, we curate ap-
proximately 39,000 hours of egocentric and robot manip-
ulation data for representation learning and use the robot
demonstration subset for WAM pretraining.
As shown in Figure 1, after benchmark-specific post-
training, Phoenix achieves state-of-the-art performance
across six benchmarks:
LIBERO-Plus [10], LIBERO-
Pro [80], RoboTwin 2.0 C2R [5], RoboCasa-GR1 [40],
Bench2Dex [62], and RoboDojo-RealWorld [6].
These
benchmarks assess manipulation generalization under en-
vironmental perturbations and across robot embodiments,
spanning simulation and real-world settings.
We further study how representations of scene structure
affect manipulation performance and data efficiency across
WAM pretraining and downstream adaptation. Ablations
show that learning these representations and compressing
them into generative latents both improve manipulation per-
formance, accompanied by more faithful predictions of in-
teraction dynamics. Scaling WAM pretraining data yields
larger gains in success rate for Phoenix than for a video-
VAE-only baseline, indicating improved pretraining data
efficiency.
These benefits extend to downstream adapta-
tion, where pretrained Phoenix maintains high success rates
with limited task-specific demonstrations, showing effec-
tive knowledge transfer in few-shot settings.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• Representations of scene structure for world–action
modeling.

dual-teacher distillation of geometric and spatiotemporal
semantic knowledge. CS-VAE causally aggregates and

• Improved WAM pretraining data efficiency.

2. Related Work
World–Action Models and Visual Representations.
World–action models connect future prediction with con-
trol through predictive video features [13] or joint video–
action learning [27, 64, 81]. Video co-training can improve
policies even without future generation at inference [70],
highlighting the role of the learned visual representation.
Pretrained visual features enrich this prediction space
with semantic knowledge. Such features have supported
action-conditioned prediction and planning [1, 79], and can
guide generative learning through alignment [68] or serve
as the latent space for generation [77]. Within WAMs, con-
current studies examine predictive feature learning and the
integration of external representations [18, 33]. Recent ap-
proaches increasingly extend reconstruction-oriented visual
representations with semantic knowledge, using pretrained
semantic supervision [34, 50, 73] or predicting semantic
features alongside appearance [45, 51]. A controlled com-
parison of reconstruction and semantic latent spaces [17]
further shows that reconstruction fidelity alone does not
determine planning quality, and that semantic latents can
better preserve action-relevant information. Phoenix learns
representations of scene structure by enriching general vi-
sual semantics with geometric and spatiotemporal seman-
tic knowledge through dual-teacher distillation. CS-VAE
causally aggregates and compresses these Phoenix features
into compact generative latents while preserving spatial lay-
out. The Phoenix WAM jointly predicts these latents, com-
plementary appearance latents, and robot actions.

3. Method
Phoenix predicts action chunks and future visual latents
from current visual observations, robot state, and a lan-
guage instruction (Fig. 2).
Phoenix Encoder learns per-
frame scene features through dual-teacher distillation. CS-
VAE, a causal-semantic variational autoencoder, aggregates
and compresses these Phoenix features into generative la-
tents.
Together with complementary Wan VAE appear-
ance latents, they support scene prediction and action learn-
ing. Training proceeds through representation pretraining
on heterogeneous recordings, WAM pretraining on robot
demonstrations, and task-specific post-training.

3.2. Causal Aggregation and Compression
Phoenix Encoder produces dense, high-dimensional per-
frame scene features. CS-VAE compresses their channel
and temporal dimensions while aggregating features over
causal temporal windows and retaining the spatial patch
grid. Feature reconstruction preserves scene information,
while KL regularization encourages the latent posterior to
match a standard Gaussian prior.
CS-VAE applies a spatial Transformer per frame, fol-
lowed by two causal temporal compression stages and a
projection that parameterizes a Gaussian latent posterior. It
compresses nine feature frames F0, . . . , F8 into three latent
frames z0, z1, z2, preserving the spatial patch grid.
The first latent z0 depends only on F0. Each subsequent
latent aggregates a causal window of consecutive Phoenix
feature frames ending at its corresponding time step, ex-
tending temporal context beyond individual frames. This
preserves the current observation as conditioning while or-
ganizing the remaining sequence into temporally aggre-
gated prediction targets.
During CS-VAE training, we sample latent codes z from
the approximate Gaussian posterior q(z | F) parameterized
by the CS-VAE encoder, where F denotes frozen Phoenix
Encoder features after fixed channel-wise standardization.
The decoder D reconstructs the feature sequence from these
codes, yielding ˆF = D(z). The CS-VAE objective is
LCS-VAE = LMSE( ˆFt, Ft) + Lcos( ˆFt, Ft)
+ βDKL(q(z | F) ∥N(0, I)) .
(3)
Here, Ft and ˆFt denote the target and reconstructed fea-
tures at frame t. LMSE and Lcos are the mean squared error
and cosine reconstruction losses. The KL divergence DKL,
weighted by β, regularizes the posterior toward the standard
Gaussian prior N(0, I), with identity covariance I. The re-
sulting latents complement video-VAE appearance latents
for world–action modeling. Training details are provided in
the supplementary material.
3.3. Joint Visual and Action Modeling
We concatenate CS-VAE scene latents with Wan VAE ap-
pearance latents along channels. A video diffusion Trans-
former models the concatenated visual tokens, while an ac-
tion diffusion Transformer models action chunks. Masked
joint attention couples the two streams at each layer with an
asymmetric mask. Current-frame visual tokens attend only
to one another; future visual tokens attend to all visual to-
kens but not actions; and action tokens attend to all visual
and action tokens. The remaining computation is modality-
specific. The action stream also cross-attends to T5 instruc-
tion embeddings, robot states, and soft prompt tokens in-
dexed by arm.
We train the coupled experts with flow matching [30]:
the current visual latent stays clean, while the future latents
and the action chunk are perturbed with Gaussian noise, so
each expert regresses its own flow field. The objective is
LWAM = 0.5 Lappearance + 0.5 Lfeature + Laction.
(4)
Lappearance supervises the appearance latents, Lfeature the
future CS-VAE latents, and Laction the action chunk, scored
only over the control dimensions the embodiment provides.
At inference, we fix current visual latents and jointly de-
noise future latents and action chunks from Gaussian noise.

4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation Details
Network architecture.
CS-VAE causally aggregates and
compresses nine frames of Phoenix Encoder features into
48-channel scene latents with three temporal positions.
These are concatenated with the VAE’s 48-channel appear-
ance latents to form a 96-channel visual representation. The
video expert is initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, and the
action expert is initialized randomly. Each predicted action
chunk spans 32 consecutive control steps.
Data settings.
The per-view input resolution is 256 ×
256 throughout training.
Encoder distillation and CS-
VAE training use filtered robot demonstrations, egocen-
tric videos, and UMI recordings; world–action pretrain-
ing uses only robot demonstrations. For robot demonstra-
tions from different sources, we standardize end-effector
pose representations through workspace alignment, end-
effector alignment, and consistent position units.
Data-
scaling experiments sample complete trajectories.
Pre-
training groups trajectories by task combinations to form
the 50% subset D50, then subsamples it according to
full-dataset source proportions to form D25 (25% of the
full dataset), with D25 ⊂D50 ⊂D100. Post-training sub-
sets are sampled progressively within each task, satisfying
D25 ⊂D50 ⊂D75 ⊂D100 while retaining all tasks.
Training and inference.
Encoder distillation uses cosine
decay with peak learning rates of 10−5 for the backbone
and 10−4 for adapters/predictor; subsequent stages use the
110k-step EMA encoder checkpoint.
CS-VAE trains for
150k steps with a peak learning rate of 1.5 × 10−4. World–
action pretraining freezes Phoenix Encoder, CS-VAE, and
Wan VAE, and uses AdamW with a global batch size of
4,480 and a peak learning rate of 10−4 for one epoch at
each data scale. Post-training runs for 25k steps by default,
and policy inference uses 10 denoising steps.

4.3. Ablation Study and In-depth Analysis
Q1: Does our representation design support general-
izable manipulation?
Phoenix Encoder distills geomet-
ric and spatiotemporal semantic knowledge into pretrained
visual features to learn scene representations.
CS-VAE
causally aggregates and compresses these features into gen-
erative latents. We evaluate these representations through
frozen-feature probing and downstream ablations.
We follow the Spatial State benchmark [14, 66] and
probe frozen encoder features on the ScanNet++ [65] subset
of InsScene-15K [25], using identical task-specific probe
architectures and training schedules across encoders. Spa-
tial readouts assess metric depth and per-pixel instance
identity using prediction losses, and cross-view consistency
using balanced correspondence accuracy. Predictive read-
outs assess direct future-feature prediction from relative
camera motion and path integration by chaining relative
motions, using Global R@1 within each encoder’s feature
space. Scores are averaged over observation-prefix lengths.
Phoenix Encoder achieves the best average rank (1.80),
ranking first in direct prediction (34.42%) and second on
the other four readouts (Table 4). The frozen representa-
tion balances spatial understanding and motion-conditioned
prediction. Figure 3 shows depth and instance readouts.

LIBERO-Plus, retaining the appearance-VAE branch and
fixing feature compression to PCA48. All models are post-

Q2: Does our representation improve pretraining scal-
ing and data efficiency?
We examine how representation
design affects the gains from scaling WAM pretraining data,
with the learned scene representation held fixed. Figure 4
compares Phoenix with the VAE-only Fast-WAM baseline

pretraining data. Using 50% of the WAM pretraining data,
it reaches 67.7% success, exceeding the baseline’s 47.7%

representation design helps learn transferable policies from
manipulation experience, yielding larger gains from scaling
pretraining data and higher data efficiency.

demonstrations further improves success to 66.1%, a lead
of 18.4 points over this baseline. These results show that
learned scene representations transfer to downstream ma-

5. Conclusion
We presented Phoenix, which learns scene representations
for generalizable world–action modeling.
Phoenix En-
coder builds on DINOv3’s visual semantics through dual-
teacher distillation of geometric and spatiotemporal seman-
tic knowledge.
CS-VAE causally aggregates and com-
presses Phoenix Encoder features into generative latents for
joint prediction with appearance and actions.
Represen-
tation learning uses approximately 39,000 hours of ego-
centric and robot manipulation data.
After benchmark-
specific post-training, Phoenix achieves SOTA performance
on six simulation and real-robot benchmarks spanning per-
turbation robustness, dexterous manipulation, and adapta-
tion across robot configurations. Ablations validate both
scene feature learning and generative compression. Phoenix
also gains more from scaling pretraining data than a video-
VAE-only baseline and adapts effectively with few down-
stream demonstrations. Better future-video prediction com-
plements these policy results. Together, these findings con-
nect scene representation learning with manipulation gener-
alization and pretraining data efficiency.

A. Additional Benchmark Results
A.1. Benchmark Details
We evaluate Phoenix on six manipulation benchmarks span-
ning environmental perturbations, cross-embodiment adap-
tation, dexterous manipulation, and physical robot deploy-
ment. Below, we describe their task coverage, demonstra-
tion datasets, evaluation settings, and reporting conventions.
LIBERO-Plus [10].
LIBERO-Plus evaluates manipula-
tion under distribution shifts using tasks derived from the
four standard LIBERO [31] suites: Spatial, Object, Goal,
and Long. Each suite contains 10 tasks with 50 demon-
strations per task, providing 2,000 demonstrations before
preprocessing.
LIBERO-Plus introduces seven perturba-
tion categories—camera viewpoint, robot initialization, lan-
guage instruction, lighting, background texture, sensor
noise, and object layout—with 10,030 evaluation instances.
The policy is adapted using LIBERO demonstrations and
evaluated on the perturbed instances. We report success
rates for each category and overall success across all 10,030
instances.
LIBERO-Pro
[80].
LIBERO-Pro
evaluates
policies
adapted on the standard LIBERO demonstration datasets
under controlled modifications to objects, spatial configura-
tions, instructions, and task goals. Our comparison reports
four perturbation settings:
Object, Position, Semantic,
and Task. Object perturbations change appearance, size,
or color; Position perturbations relocate objects within
physically valid regions; Semantic perturbations introduce
alternative instruction wording; and Task perturbations
modify the relevant objects or target states. We evaluate
each setting on 40 tasks across the four LIBERO suites,
using 50 episodes per task.
We report the four setting-
specific success rates and their arithmetic mean, excluding
unperturbed performance from the aggregate.
RoboCasa-GR1 [40, 41].
RoboCasa-GR1 comprises 24
tabletop manipulation tasks performed by the Fourier GR1
robot with two arms and dexterous hands. Eighteen tasks in-
volve rearranging objects between source and target recep-
tacles, while six require placing objects inside articulated
fixtures, including cabinets, drawers, and microwaves, and
subsequently closing them. We use 1,000 generated demon-
strations per task, totaling 24,000 trajectories. Observations
include an egocentric RGB view, and tasks vary object iden-
tities, placements, receptacles, and distractors. Our evalua-
tion uses 50 rollouts per task, totaling 1,200 episodes. We
report success rates for the pick-and-place and articulated-
object categories, together with the equally weighted mean
across all 24 tasks.
RoboTwin 2.0 Clean2Random [5].
RoboTwin 2.0 con-
tains 50 manipulation tasks evaluated with the ALOHA-
AgileX dual-arm embodiment.
The Clean2Random set-
ting provides 50 clean expert demonstrations per task, to-
taling 2,500 training trajectories, and evaluates policies in
randomized environments.
We use 100 evaluation roll-
outs per task for the randomized condition, totaling 5,000
episodes.
Randomization introduces scene clutter, light-
ing changes, background textures, tabletop-height variation,
and unseen instruction wording. We report Clean2Random
success averaged over the 50 tasks, assessing whether be-
havior learned from clean demonstrations transfers to these
perturbed environments.
Bench2Dex
[62].
Bench2Dex
comprises
26
task–
embodiment settings spanning 12 arm–hand combinations,
with approximately 1,300 human-teleoperated demonstra-
tions. Tasks cover tool use, articulated-object interaction,
and multi-stage bimanual manipulation.
Evaluation uses
50 rollouts per task–embodiment setting under each of
four conditions:
unperturbed, equivariant perturbations,
invariant perturbations, and their combination. This yields
1,300 episodes per condition and 5,200 episodes per
policy.
Equivariant perturbations alter object poses and
table height, requiring corresponding action adjustments;
invariant perturbations modify backgrounds,
textures,
lighting, distractors, and camera poses. We report success
rate (SR) and latched stage completion rate (LSCR). For an
episode e with Ke task stages, LSCR is defined as

RoboDojo-RealWorld
[6].
RoboDojo-RealWorld
in-

The dataset provides 100 teleoperated demonstrations per

B. Additional Probing Results
B.1. Experimental Setup
We use five probes from Yin et al. [66] to assess spatial
understanding and motion-conditioned prediction in frozen
encoder representations. The evaluation uses camera tra-
jectories with known poses from the ScanNet++ subset of
InsScene-15K [25, 65], comprising 2,445 training windows
from 612 scenes and 276 validation windows from 69 dis-
joint scenes. Each window contains 28 observations sam-
pled approximately uniformly in cumulative camera mo-
tion, measured as translation distance plus 0.5 times the ro-
tation angle in radians, with a sampling step of 0.35. Win-
dows are constructed with a stride of eight sampled obser-
vations, additionally including the final valid start. When
more than four windows are available, four evenly spaced
starts are retained per scene.
We independently encode
observation prefixes of p ∈{8, 12, 16, 24} frames.
For
predictive readouts, we evaluate future targets at horizons
h ∈{1, 2, 4} after the last observed frame. These horizons
count sampled observations. A separate probe is trained
for each encoder, task, and prefix length, and the reported
scores are averaged over the four prefix lengths.
We follow the task-specific readout designs and training
schedules of Yin et al., adapting the input dimensions to
each encoder. For Phoenix, we use the frozen exponential-
moving-average encoder employed downstream.
Each
256 × 256 input frame yields a 16 × 16 grid of 1,024-
dimensional patch features; the CLS token is excluded. Pre-
fix features are extracted and cached independently, and fu-
ture targets are obtained through separate encoding of the
target frames. All Phoenix probes are trained on one GPU
with a batch size of two, BF16 mixed precision, and seed
42. We use AdamW with (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95) and weight
decay 0.05. Depth and instance-identity probes are trained
for 100 epochs at a learning rate of 10−4, with ten warmup
epochs; the remaining probes are trained for 50 epochs at
3×10−4, with five warmup epochs. Both schedules use co-
sine decay to 10−6. Evaluation uses the final-epoch check-
point.
Spatial readouts. Depth and instance identity are de-
coded using pixel-aligned DPT-style heads with a train-
able four-layer feature aggregator, supervising every frame
in the observed prefix.
The depth probe predicts metric
depth and includes the camera-pose auxiliary supervision
used in the reference implementation, with unit weights for
the depth and camera losses. We report the mean validity-
weighted absolute depth error. The instance-identity probe
produces 63-dimensional pixel embeddings. For each in-
stance, a prototype is computed by averaging its pixel em-
beddings across the prefix, using the ground-truth instance
groups. Cross-entropy supervision encourages each pixel
to match its corresponding prototype. The reported iden-
tity loss measures separation between instances across the
observed views.
The view-consistency probe spatially pools each frame’s
features and uses a multilayer perceptron to predict over-
lap between pairs of prefix frames. Ground-truth overlap is
computed by projecting valid depth pixels from one view
into the other and taking the minimum of the two direc-
tional overlap fractions. Pairs with overlap of at least 0.40
are positive, those with overlap of at most 0.05 are negative,
and intermediate pairs are excluded. A predicted probabil-
ity above 0.5 indicates overlap. We report balanced accu-
racy, computed as the mean of the positive- and negative-
class accuracies.
Predictive readouts. Direct prediction estimates the spa-
tially pooled feature of a future frame from the observed
prefix and the relative camera motion from its final frame
to the target.
Camera motion is represented by a nine-
dimensional vector containing a six-dimensional rotation
representation and three-dimensional translation. Spatially
pooled frame features and the motion input are processed
by a two-layer, eight-head Transformer with hidden dimen-
sion 512. Training minimizes the sum of mean squared er-
ror and cosine distance to the target feature. Each training
prefix is assigned one horizon through a fixed seeded par-
tition, yielding 815 examples for each of h = 1, 2, 4. At
evaluation, every validation prefix is evaluated at all three
horizons.
Path integration instead initializes a two-layer GRU with
hidden dimension 512 from features pooled over the ob-
served prefix’s temporal and spatial dimensions. It sequen-
tially applies relative camera motions from the final ob-
servation to h = 1, from h = 1 to h = 2, and from
h = 2 to h = 4, predicting a future feature at each way-
point. The path-integration probe is supervised at all three
waypoints using the same MSE and cosine-distance objec-
tive. Both predictive readouts are evaluated by Global R@1
within each encoder’s feature space. Each predicted feature
is ranked by cosine similarity against a gallery containing
the 828 target features from all 276 validation windows and
three horizons. A prediction is counted as correct when its
associated target ranks first.
Visualization conventions.
All qualitative examples

trained probes.
The depth comparisons (Fig. S1) show
the input, ground truth, and predicted metric depth using
a shared 0–6 m color scale; larger depths are clipped for

instance identity (Fig. S2), PCA is fitted jointly to the pixel
embeddings from the two displayed views of each win-
dow. PCA colors encode embedding variation; ground-truth
overlays use separate colors to identify instances. View-
consistency examples (Fig. S3) display the input pair, geo-
metric overlap, and predicted overlap probability under the

For predictive readouts, the displayed retrievals are real

B.2. Qualitative Probing Results
The qualitative probes examine two properties of the frozen
Phoenix Encoder: what scene structure its representation
preserves in the current observation, and whether that repre-
sentation distinguishes the view reached after a camera mo-
tion. This distinction matters for prediction: two views can
share much of the same scene yet differ in which surfaces
are visible and how they appear relative to one another.
Scene geometry and object boundaries.
The Phoenix
Encoder preserves scene structure at both room and object
scales. In the laboratory, decoded depth increases along the
aisle from the foreground bins, past the workbenches, to
the rear wall (Fig. S1C). In the kitchenette, the edge of the
foreground wall marks an abrupt transition to the recessed
counter behind it (Fig. S1D). Within the counter, however,
the sink and draining board occupy a region close in depth
to the surrounding work surface. This region is distinct in
the instance-embedding map and remains discernible when
the camera pulls back and the sink appears smaller near the
image edge (Fig. S2A). The same frozen representation thus
preserves both the depth relationship between surfaces and
object boundaries that remain visible across viewpoints.
Shared structure across viewpoints. The two desk pairs
illustrate how the Phoenix representation encodes overlap
as framing changes. In Fig. S3A, the second view includes
more of the tabletop and places the laptop at a different
image location. In Fig. S3B, the monitor and nearby ob-
jects remain visible within two differently framed views
of a cluttered desk. Both pairs are correctly identified as
overlapping, while the pairs with little common content in
Fig. S3D–E are classified as low overlap. The basin pair
in Fig. S3F is a missed overlap: the basin is prominent in
the first view but largely outside the second, and the pre-
dicted overlap probability is 0.350 for a geometric overlap
of 0.496. In the overlapping desk views, shared scene con-
tent remains identifiable in the Phoenix representation even
as its position in the image changes.
Scene structure under camera motion. Future-view pre-
diction requires a finer distinction than overlap recognition:
several views may depict the same workspace, but only one
corresponds to the specified camera motion. In the kitchen
sequence, the initial view looks along the countertop to-
ward the sink. After a downward tilt, both path integra-
tion and direct prediction retrieve the exact h = 2 view
of the hob, cabinet fronts, and floor from 828 candidates
(Figs. S4A and S5B). In the workshop, direct prediction re-
trieves the exact h = 4 view, in which the foreground ma-
chinery appears at a different position relative to the win-
dows (Fig. S5C). A turn from the kitchenette toward a desk
chair and cabinets is missed at the same horizon. Both read-
outs retrieve the same h = 1 counter-facing view, with
the true h = 4 target ranked seventh for direct prediction
and twentieth for path integration (Figs. S5E and S4B). The
successful retrievals show that the frozen Phoenix represen-
tation preserves enough viewpoint-specific detail to distin-
guish the target view from other views of the same scene.

D.2. CS-VAE Architecture
CS-VAE operates on Phoenix Encoder features with shape
B × 1024 × 9 × 16 × 16 per view. Three pre-norm spa-
tial Transformer blocks process each frame independently
at width 1024. Two causal temporal downsampling stages
then reduce nine positions to three, preserving the first po-
sition. A final LayerNorm and linear projection produce
96 channels, split into the mean and log-variance of a 48-
channel diagonal Gaussian posterior.
Each temporal stage contains a residual bottleneck with
width 256 and a causal (3, 1, 1) convolution, followed by
a (3, 1, 1) downsampling convolution with stride (2, 1, 1).
Temporal residual convolutions use two positions of left
padding. Each downsampling stage retains its first position
and appends the strided outputs, giving 9→5→3 positions
at frame endpoints 0, 4, 8 within the sampled nine-frame se-
quence. Spatial resolution remains 16 × 16 throughout.
The decoder projects 48 channels back to 1024, ap-
plies two temporal upsampling stages, and uses three spatial
Transformer blocks followed by LayerNorm. Each upsam-
pling stage uses a causal convolution to expand channels,
rearranges subsequent latent positions into pairs of temporal
positions, and applies a residual bottleneck. This restores
3→5→9 positions while retaining the first position.

At each decision step, the frozen visual modules encode the

F. Pretraining Data and Processing
F.1. Data Composition
Our pretraining pool comprises robot demonstrations, ego-
centric videos, and Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI)
recordings, totaling 54,413 hours of raw data and approxi-
mately 3.38 billion frames. Filtering retains 39,306 hours,
of which robot demonstrations, egocentric videos, and UMI
recordings account for 31.09%, 63.58%, and 5.33% by du-
ration, respectively. Figure S7 presents the sources, repre-
sentative observations, and original and retained durations.
Robot data combine public datasets and in-house demon-
strations spanning different arms, end effectors, camera
configurations, and manipulation tasks. They provide vi-
sual, state, and action supervision grounded in robot con-
trol. Egocentric videos and UMI recordings contribute ma-
nipulation sequences from additional scenes, broadening
the coverage of visual representation learning.
The three pretraining stages use different portions of this
pool.
Encoder distillation and CS-VAE training use all
filtered data to learn scene representations and their spa-
tiotemporal compression, respectively. World–action pre-
training uses only robot demonstrations to jointly predict
future visual latents and action chunks.
F.2. Robot Data Processing Pipeline
Our robot data curation follows practices in Qwen-
RobotManip [69] and related work, emphasizing consis-
tency among visual observations, robot states, actions, and
task instructions. Sources differ not only in file format but
also in timestamps, coordinate conventions, and the mean-
ings of control fields. We therefore first standardize the for-
mat and check temporal correspondence, then inspect vi-
sual and motion quality, trim redundant episode boundaries,
and align control semantics. Instruction augmentation, sam-
ple construction, and manual review complete the process
(Fig. S8).
Format conversion and temporal synchronization.
We
convert videos, states, actions, and task information to LeR-
obot V3, organize them by complete trajectory, and spec-
ify joint order, position units, rotation representations, and
gripper semantics. Measured states and action commands
are treated separately: the former describe actual robot mo-
tion, whereas the latter specify requested control and may
differ because of contact, control latency, or motion con-
straints. We associate video presentation timestamps with
state acquisition times and check matching errors. Videos
are fully decoded after conversion to verify frame counts,
state rows, and task boundaries. For sources requiring one-
to-one frame correspondence, we verify a complete one-to-
one mapping between video frames and state records.
Visual quality checks.
After decoding and synchroniza-
tion checks, we assess whether videos retain recognizable
manipulation details. We sample frames uniformly, con-
vert them to grayscale, and resize them to a common scale.
Sharpness is measured by Laplacian-response energy nor-
malized by gradient energy, reducing sensitivity to image
contrast. The default configuration samples eight frames
per clip and resizes the shorter side to 256 pixels. A clip
is flagged as a blur candidate when its median normalized
sharpness is below 0.18 and fewer than 50% of sampled
frames reach this threshold; candidates are reviewed against
the video.
Motion checks and static boundary trimming.
We sam-
ple short-interval frame pairs at different positions in a
video, estimate pixel displacement using optical flow, and
normalize it by image size and the frame interval. Clip-
level statistics capture both typical motion and short bursts
to identify prolonged inactivity or abrupt changes. Opti-
cal flow and sharpness provide complementary checks: the

and future actions. Each window contains 33 consecutive
state timestamps to construct a 32-step action chunk, with
nine RGB frames sampled per view. The complete window,
including the endpoint state needed for the final action, must
lie within one continuous task interval of a single trajectory.
Repeated occurrences of the same task are not joined across
intervening tasks. Intervals that are too short are not padded
with repeated states or frames to create training windows.
Manual semantic verification.
Manual review uses syn-
