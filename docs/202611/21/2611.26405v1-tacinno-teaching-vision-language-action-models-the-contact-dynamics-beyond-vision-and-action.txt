Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently been
equipped with tactile sensing to perceive visually subtle
contact events in contact-rich manipulation.
However,
adding tactile input yields inconsistent gains, and some
visuo-tactile VLA models rely little on tactile input, because
much of the tactile change, such as the gel deformation dur-
ing gripper closure, is predictable from visual observations
and executed actions. To address this problem, we introduce

Specifically, the TacInno tokenizer, conditioned on visual

1. Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a
promising approach to general-purpose robotic manipula-
tion [4, 5, 19, 27]. Building on pretrained Vision-Language
Models (VLMs), VLA models learn to predict robot ac-
tions from visual observations, language instructions, and
proprioceptive states through imitation learning [3]. Nev-
ertheless, contact-rich manipulation remains challenging,
as critical contact events, such as incipient slip and jam-
ming during insertion, are often difficult to infer from ex-
ternal visual observations. To capture contact events, re-
cent efforts augment VLA models with tactile observa-
tions [2, 11, 16, 22, 26, 38, 40, 42, 44], typically acquired
by vision-based tactile sensors such as GelSight [41] and
DIGIT [20], which capture the deformation of a soft gel.
Existing visuo-tactile policies incorporate tactile obser-
vations as additional inputs [16, 32, 40, 43, 49], predict fu-
ture tactile signals [9, 13, 33, 38, 46], or emphasize tactile
changes during training [10, 25, 36, 37, 45] (Fig. 1c). How-
ever, incorporating tactile inputs yields inconsistent gains
and can even reduce the success rate relative to the same
VLA without tactile inputs [40, 44]. Moreover, we apply
a tactile-off test, which removes tactile input at test time,
and find that the success rates of some existing policies
change little, indicating limited reliance on tactile infor-
mation (Sec. 4.4). We attribute the inconsistent gains and
the limited reliance to the nature of the tactile signal, since
a tactile sensor measures how the contact responds to the
robot’s own actions. Consequently, given the initial tactile
observation, a substantial portion of the subsequent tactile
change, such as the gel deformation during gripper closure,
is predictable from visual observations and executed actions
(Sec. 4.5, Fig. 1a). As an input, the predictable portion is
largely redundant, and adding an input modality can even
degrade performance [34]. When future tactile signals are
the prediction target, the predictable portion makes up most
of the target, so the prediction loss is dominated by changes
that vision and executed actions already explain. Weight-
ing the loss by the magnitude of tactile change does not
help, since the largest changes are often the predictable ones
(Fig. 4). A central question arises: How can the contact dy-
namics that vision and executed actions cannot explain be
isolated and used to guide VLA policy learning?
To answer this question, we propose TacInno, which
isolates the contact dynamics that vision and executed ac-
tions cannot explain as the supervision signal for VLA
training. Inspired by the notion of innovation in estima-
tion theory [17, 18], we define tactile innovation as the
residual tactile change after accounting for the initial tac-
tile observation, visual observations, and executed actions.
Three properties of tactile innovation determine the design
of TacInno. First, tactile innovation is conditional on visual
observations and executed actions. We therefore introduce
the TacInno tokenizer, which learns a compact discrete code
as a proxy for tactile innovation and conditions both its en-
coder and decoder on the corresponding visual observations
and executed actions, so that the code retains the contact
dynamics that the two conditions cannot explain (Fig. 1b).
Second, tactile innovation is dynamic, arising from transient
contact events, such as incipient slip, that span several con-
trol steps. The tokenizer therefore encodes a short sequence
of tactile observations with causal temporal attention rather
than a single pair of frames. Third, tactile innovation lies
in the future and is unavailable when the policy acts. We
therefore train TacInno-VLA to predict the code from cur-
rent observations as an auxiliary objective, supervising the
tactile representations used for action prediction. The code
serves only as a training target, and the tokenizer and auxil-
iary prediction heads are removed at deployment.
We evaluate TacInno on six contact-rich tasks from the
UniVTAC benchmark [8] and four real-world tasks using
a Franka FR3 robot equipped with a vision-based tactile
sensor.
TacInno-VLA achieves average success rates of
87.5% in simulation and 70.5% in the real world, the highest
among the evaluated visuo-tactile policies. Ablation studies
verify the contribution of each design, and the tactile-off
test confirms that TacInno-VLA relies on tactile input.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• With the tactile-off test, we show that some existing
visuo-tactile policies rely little on tactile input, attribute
the limited reliance to the redundancy of the tactile sig-
nal, and propose to separate the tactile change that vision
and executed actions cannot explain, termed tactile inno-
vation.
• We propose TacInno, which encodes tactile innovation
into a discrete code with a conditional multi-frame tok-
enizer and uses the code solely as an auxiliary training
target, adding no module for the code at deployment.
• TacInno-VLA outperforms the strongest visuo-tactile
baseline by 10.7 and 9.0 percentage points in average suc-
cess rate in simulation and the real world, respectively.
The success rate of TacInno-VLA drops from 88% to 0%
on Lift Can under the tactile-off test and by 21 percent-
age points on the lifting tasks when the code encodes the
entire tactile change.
2. Related Work
Visuo-tactile policy learning.
Earlier visuo-tactile poli-
cies fuse tactile images [15, 21, 23] or pretrained tactile
2


representations [14, 47] with visual features in imitation-
learning policies such as ACT [48] and Diffusion Pol-
icy [12]. Recent methods extend tactile sensing to VLA
models in three progressive directions (Fig. 1c). The first
incorporates tactile observations as additional inputs and re-
lies on the action loss alone to determine how the tactile in-
puts are used [2, 11, 16, 22, 26, 32, 40, 42, 43, 49]. Because
the action loss rewards only what improves the imitated ac-
tions, the policy is not required to use the tactile inputs,
and the gains are modest and occasionally negative [40, 44].
FACT [29] further replaces the tactile input with noise to ex-
amine how much of the reported gain originates from tactile
information. The second direction therefore assigns the tac-
tile signal an objective of its own and predicts future tactile
signals, either within the policy [9, 13, 24, 38] or through
tactile world models [33, 46]. Since predicting every fu-
ture tactile signal spends much of the supervision on peri-
ods in which the contact hardly changes, the third direction
emphasizes tactile changes during training by weighting or
gating the tactile signal according to the magnitude of its
change [10, 25, 36, 37, 45]. However, the second and third
directions still supervise the tactile representation with the
entire future tactile change, including the component that
vision and executed actions already explain. Our method
extends this progression by supervising only the compo-
nent that vision and executed actions cannot explain. The
closest work, ResTacVLA [44], uses the quantized residual
between visually predicted and observed tactile representa-
tions as a policy input, whereas our code encodes the future
tactile change that vision and executed actions cannot ex-
plain and serves only as a training target.
Discrete latent dynamics learning.
Vector-quantized
variational autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) [31] learn compact
discrete representations by mapping continuous features to
entries in a learned codebook and reconstructing observa-
tions from the quantized codes.
Vector quantization has
been extended to temporal modeling, where discrete la-
tent variables capture transitions between observations. Ge-
nie [6] learns latent actions from unlabeled videos to enable
controllable video generation. LAPA [39] employs a VQ-
VAE-based action tokenizer to extract discrete latent actions
from video transitions, which serve as supervision for VLA
pretraining. UniVLA [7] further incorporates language con-
ditioning into inverse- and forward-dynamics learning to
obtain task-centric latent actions. Building on discrete la-
tent dynamics learning, our method applies vector quanti-
zation to tactile observations. The encoder compresses a se-
quence of tactile observations into discrete codes, while the
decoder reconstructs future tactile changes conditioned on
the current tactile state, visual observations, and executed
actions.
Unlike latent action models, which infer unob-
served actions, our tokenizer is trained on demonstrations
with known actions and therefore provides the actions to
both the encoder and the decoder. With conditional recon-
struction, the codes retain only the contact dynamics that
the conditioning information cannot explain.
3. Method
In this section, we present TacInno in three parts (Figs. 2
and 3). First, Sec. 3.1 formulates the problem and defines
tactile innovation. Then, Sec. 3.2 presents the TacInno to-
kenizer, which isolates tactile innovation from demonstra-
tions and labels each demonstration frame with a discrete
code. Finally, Sec. 3.3 describes TacInno-VLA, which is
trained to predict the code as an auxiliary objective.
3.1. Problem Formulation
At control step t, the policy receives visual observations
ot, a language instruction ℓ, the proprioceptive state qt, and
the tactile observation Xt, which comprises the indentation
depth map and the marker displacement field of the vision-
based tactile sensors. The policy predicts an action chunk
at+1:t+H over the next H steps, where at+i denotes the
offset of the joint target at step t+i from qt. The policy is
trained by imitation learning on expert demonstrations.

Visual and action conditioning.
The conditions deter-
mine the content of the code. Information that the decoder
receives directly need not be encoded in the code. Given
Xt alone, the code would have to encode the entire tactile
change, whereas a decoder that also receives the visual ob-
servations and executed actions has access to all variables
on which the first term of Eq. (2) depends. Since the de-
coder receives no tactile observation after t, information
about ν reaches the decoder only through ct, and the re-
construction objective thus encourages the 16-bit code to
encode ν [1]. The encoder receives the same conditions,
which allows the encoder to identify the tactile changes that
the conditions already explain.

3.3. TacInno-VLA Policy
The code ct of each demonstration frame is provided to the
policy as a training target rather than an input, since ct sum-
marizes future observations that are unavailable when the
policy acts. The policy must therefore learn to predict ct
from the inputs available at step t, which is feasible because
early signs of tactile innovation, such as the shear that in-
creases before slip, often appear in the preceding tactile ob-
servations. To this end, we equip the VLA with a tactile
branch that encodes three tactile observations and is super-
vised by the code prediction loss (Fig. 3).
Policy architecture.
TacInno-VLA is built upon the pre-
trained VLA π0.5 [3], which combines a PaliGemma VLM
backbone with a Gemma 300M action expert trained with
flow matching. The backbone encodes the visual observa-
tions and the language instruction into prefix tokens, and
the action expert attends to the prefix tokens to generate ac-
tions. We insert three groups of condition tokens before the
noisy action tokens of the action expert. The three groups
are proprioceptive tokens encoded from qt, 60 tactile tokens
encoded by a lightweight Transformer tactile encoder from
the three tactile observations (t−8, t−4, and t), and four
learnable code queries u1, . . . , u4, which are used solely to
predict the tactile innovation code.
We adopt a block-wise attention mask under which the
code queries attend to the prefix, proprioceptive, and tac-
tile tokens, whereas no other token, including the action to-
kens, attends to the code queries, so the outputs of the code
queries never influence action prediction. During training,
the gradient of the code prediction loss in Eq. (4) reaches the
rest of the network only through the tokens attended by the
code queries. Since the action tokens attend to the same to-
kens, the code prediction loss shapes the tactile representa-
tions used for action prediction. Allowing the action tokens
to attend to the code queries would let the code prediction
loss bypass the shared tactile representations (Sec. 4.3).

4.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmark and data.
We adopt six tasks from the Uni-
VTAC benchmark [8], namely Insert Hole, Insert Tube, Lift
Bottle, Lift Can, Pull-out Key, and Put Bottle in Shelf (Put
Bottle), simulated in Isaac Sim with two vision-based tac-
tile sensors [41] rendered by Taxim [30]. Each task pro-
vides 100 expert demonstrations, of which demonstrations
0 to 49 are used to train the policies, 0 to 89 to train the
tokenizer, and 90 to 99 are held out for analysis. We further
set up four real-world tasks, namely wiping a board, insert-
ing a plug into a power strip, lifting a lying can upright, and
unscrewing a bottle cap, with a Xense vision-based tactile
sensor on the fingertip of a Franka FR3 gripper (Sec. 4.6).
For each real-world task, we collect 50 demonstrations by
teleoperation with GELLO [35] at 30 Hz, of which the first
45 train the policies and the tokenizer. Each policy is eval-
uated for 100 episodes per task with identical random seeds
in simulation (Tabs. 1 and 2) and for 50 trials per task in the
real world.
Baselines.
We compare TacInno-VLA against five visuo-
tactile baselines, namely UniVTAC-ACT, the visuo-tactile
ACT provided by the benchmark [8, 48], FTP-π0.5 and
FTP-1 (Foundation Tactile Policy) [40], N0-VTLA [26],
and SimpleTouch [38]. We also report π0.5 [3], the VLA on
which TacInno-VLA is built, fine-tuned as a multi-task pol-
icy without tactile input. For fair comparison, all baselines
are retrained on the same demonstrations with the official
configurations, including the action execution horizon, and
evaluated under the same protocol.
4.2. Simulation Experiment
As shown in Tab. 1, TacInno-VLA achieves an average suc-
cess rate of 87.5% across the six tasks, 10.7 and 12.2 per-
centage points above the two strongest baselines, Simple-
Touch and N0-VTLA. Moreover, TacInno-VLA is the only
policy that reaches at least 75% on every task, whereas each
baseline drops to 54% or below on at least one task. With
the execution horizon of TacInno-VLA, the baselines av-
erage 82.0% (FTP-1), 79.3% (SimpleTouch), 78.0% (N0-
VTLA), and 68.0% (FTP-π0.5), all below the 91.3% of
TacInno-VLA on the same seeds. Note that TacInno-VLA
requires no tactile pretraining, whereas FTP-1 and N0-

Predictability of the tactile change.

tactile history, and measure in nats, on the held-out demon-

encodes the entire future tactile change, the tactile history

for 97% of the information extracted with the tactile his-
tory. For the tactile innovation code, the tactile history adds
0.073 nats, 3.6× as much. The entire future tactile change,
which tactile world models use as the prediction target, is
therefore largely predictable without tactile input.

5. Conclusion
In this work, we address the redundancy of the tactile sig-
nal in visuo-tactile VLA models, where a substantial por-
8


tion of the tactile change is predictable from vision and exe-
cuted actions. To this end, we introduce TacInno, which iso-
lates tactile innovation and uses tactile innovation solely as
a training target. Central to TacInno is a conditional multi-
frame tokenizer that encodes tactile innovation into a 16-
bit discrete code, which TacInno-VLA predicts as an aux-
iliary objective. Experiments on six simulation tasks and
four real-world tasks show that TacInno-VLA achieves the
highest average success rates among the evaluated visuo-
tactile policies and relies on tactile input, and that removing
the conditioning from the tokenizer lowers the success rate
by 21 percentage points. The notion of innovation is not
specific to tactile sensing, and extending TacInno to other
modalities is left for future work.
