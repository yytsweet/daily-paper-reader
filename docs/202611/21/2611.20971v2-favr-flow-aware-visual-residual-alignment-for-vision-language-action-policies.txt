Abstract
Pretrained video representations provide rich temporal infor-
mation for robotic manipulation, yet effectively integrating
them into existing vision-language-action (VLA) policies re-
mains challenging. We present Flow-Aware Visual Residual
Alignment (FAVR), a lightweight framework for flow-aware
visual conditioning of frozen VLA policies. FAVR maintains
a compact temporal visual state through linear Test-Time
Training (TTT) and retrieves action-conditioned visual fea-
tures throughout flow-based action generation. By exploiting
the affine structure of the frozen action head, we explicitly
characterize how injected visual features influence predicted
action velocities and formulate flow-aware channel selection
as a box-constrained optimization problem. We develop a
FISTA-based solver for joint gate optimization and derive an
efficient closed-form approximation through diagonalization
and rescaling. With only 1.74–2.19M trainable parameters
and 5,000 adaptation steps, FAVR improves pretrained VLA
policies across benchmarks. On RoboTwin 2.0, FAVR im-
proves LingBot-VLA from 87.12% to 90.04% under clean
conditions and from 86.88% to 89.38% under randomized
conditions. A controlled ablation on five RoboTwin tasks fur-
ther shows that flow-aware gating increases average success
from 72.0% with a gate shared across flow steps to 82.0%,
demonstrating the benefit of adapting visual conditioning
throughout action generation.

explicitly account for the induced corrections in the action-

We introduce Flow-Aware Visual Residual Alignment
(FAVR), a lightweight framework that integrates pretrained
video representations into frozen VLA policies. FAVR em-
ploys linear Test-Time Training (TTT) [33] to aggregate fea-
tures from a frozen video encoder into a compact temporal vi-
sual state, queried by the current action features at each flow
step. Learned projections adapt state construction and re-
trieval to the control task. A lightweight predictor estimates
the target velocity residual, while the frozen affine action
head provides an exact mapping from gated visual features
to velocity corrections. We formulate flow-aware channel
selection as a box-constrained velocity-residual alignment
problem and employ a FISTA-based solver to jointly opti-
mize channel weights while accounting for their interactions.
The gated visual features are injected through a residual
connection immediately before the action head. Both pre-
trained backbones remain frozen, with only the auxiliary
visual interface and residual predictor trained.
Joint gate optimization, however, requires iterative com-
putation at each flow step. To reduce this overhead, we derive
an approximate closed-form gate through diagonalization
and analytic rescaling, simplifying channel interactions and
calibrating the resulting correction. A coupling-dependent
error bound characterizes the effect of the omitted interac-
tions. The approximation reduces computational cost at
the expense of a modest portion of the joint solver’s perfor-
mance gains. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the flow-aware gating
employed by FAVR adapts channel weights across flow steps,
unlike a shared gate. On five RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, this design
improves average success from 72.0% to 82.0%.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce FAVR, a lightweight framework that inte-
grates temporal video representations into frozen VLA
policies through action-conditioned retrieval and flow-
aware visual conditioning.
• We formulate visual channel selection as a box-constrained

• We validate FAVR across three manipulation benchmarks

demonstrating the benefits of flow-aware gating and
velocity-residual alignment.
2. Related Work
Vision-Language-Action Models. Vision-language-action
(VLA) models transfer pretrained visual and language repre-
sentations to robotic manipulation, as exemplified by Open-
VLA [17].
OpenVLA-OFT improves action generation
through parallel decoding, action chunking, and continu-
ous regression [18]. Flow-based policies such as π0 [6],
π0.5 [14], and LingBot-VLA [35] combine pretrained vision-
language backbones with action experts trained using flow
matching [21], generating continuous action chunks through
successive velocity evaluations.
Video Representations for Robot Control. Video pre-
training captures scene structure and temporal dynamics, as
demonstrated by the V-JEPA series [2, 3, 26]. WorldVLA
and DreamVLA incorporate visual prediction into action
generation through autoregressive modeling and diffusion
conditioning, respectively [7, 36]. JEPA-VLA integrates
frozen V-JEPA 2 features through input fusion or gated cross-
attention [25], while PVI injects auxiliary visual features
through a trainable action expert conditioned on the current
noisy action [37]. Both studies show that video-pretrained
representations can outperform image-pretrained features in
robotic manipulation [25, 37].
Test-Time Training for Robot Policies. Test-time train-
ing (TTT) maintains recurrent states through self-supervised
fast-weight updates [33]. Recent studies have extended TTT
to robotic policies, enabling the aggregation of historical
observations and action-generation features to support tem-
porally informed control [12, 16, 20, 24, 34, 38]. Unlike
methods that incorporate fast-weight updates within pre-
trained policy components, FAVR employs linear TTT as an
external temporal visual interface, aggregating features from
a frozen video encoder and retrieving them using current
action features at each flow step.

Standard manipulation. We evaluate color-conditioned

4.4. Ablation Studies
We investigate the key design choices of FAVR on five
RoboTwin 2.0 tasks:
hanging mug, move can pot,
click bell,
place bread skillet,
and
place can basket.
All variants are trained for
1,000 steps with a batch configuration of 2 × 4 and a
learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and evaluated over 50 trials per
task. The frozen LingBot-VLA baseline achieves 68.8%
average success (Table 5).
Flow-aware gating. As illustrated in Fig. 1, FAVR adapts
visual channel weights to the current flow state. Since the
predicted velocity evolves throughout flow integration, chan-
nel weights selected at the initial step may no longer provide
suitable corrections at later steps. To examine this, we com-
pare FAVR with a variant that reuses the initial gate across all
flow steps while retaining the action-conditioned visual read-
out. Recomputing the gate increases average success from
72.0% to 82.0%. On move can pot, success rises from
66% to 86%, illustrating how a gate selected at one flow state
can become less suitable as the velocity field evolves. This
improvement highlights the importance of adapting visual
corrections to the evolving action-generation state.
Injection position and action-head geometry. A key prop-
erty of FAVR is that the visual correction passes directly
through the frozen affine action head, yielding the exact map-
ping ∆vτ = W diag(xτ)gτ. This enables gate optimization
directly in velocity space. At earlier injection positions,
however, subsequent nonlinear layers invalidate this map-
ping. Applying the same head-derived optimization at layers
12, 24, and 36 yields success rates of 67.2%, 66.8%, and
65.6%, respectively, all below the frozen baseline. We there-
fore also evaluate learned gates that implicitly model the
downstream nonlinear response. These improve success to
75.2%, 79.6%, and 74.4%, but remain below FAVR’s 82.0%
(see Appendix D.3). Under the limited adaptation budget,
final-head injection is preferable because the known channel-
to-velocity mapping permits direct gate optimization instead
of learning the downstream nonlinear response.
Correction mechanisms. We test whether the gains arise
from velocity-residual alignment rather than simply adding
a correction branch. We compare three alternatives: Learned
Gate, which directly predicts channel weights; Direct Output,
which predicts bounded velocity corrections without gated
feature injection; and Hidden-Only, which removes the tem-
poral video-state interface and relies solely on VLA hidden
features. Their average success rates are 69.6%, 78.8%, and
79.6%, respectively, compared with 82.0% for FAVR.
Temporal visual state. We test whether recurrent visual

Video representation and multi-view conditioning. We
first examine the contribution of video-pretrained representa-
tions. On LIBERO, replacing V-JEPA with I-JEPA reduces
average success from 98.6% to 97.8%, with the largest differ-

that video-pretrained features are particularly useful for ma-
nipulation tasks involving extended temporal interactions.
We further investigate whether additional camera views
improve the visual information available to V-JEPA. On the
full 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, conditioning V-JEPA
on the head camera alone yields 88.48% average success,
whereas incorporating the head and two wrist cameras in-
creases success to 90.04%. The VLA policy retains its three-
view observations in both settings.
The open microwave task illustrates why multi-view
conditioning can be valuable (Fig. 5). During manipulation,
the gripper may slip from the handle, requiring the robot to
recover its grasp before continuing. Such contact changes
are difficult to distinguish from the head camera but clearly
visible from the wrist camera. With head-only video con-
ditioning, the auxiliary visual representation may miss this
local interaction change and continue to favor nominal task
progression. Incorporating wrist observations provides addi-
tional evidence of the actual contact state, allowing visual
corrections to better reflect the need for recovery. On this
task, three-view conditioning improves success from 45% to
64% (+19 pp). This example helps explain why multi-view
video representations can substantially benefit manipulation
tasks requiring precise contact monitoring and recovery.
Inference cost. V-JEPA processes ten-frame windows dur-
ing execution, with the final update overlapping VLA in-
ference at replanning. Averaged over 100 RoboTwin 2.0
calls (50-action chunks, 10 flow steps), inference latency is
1117.7 ms for the frozen baseline, 1134.3 ms for FAVR-CF,
and 1487.3 ms for FAVR. FAVR-CF thus reduces latency by
23.7% relative to FAVR with only 1.5% overhead over the
frozen baseline, while trailing FAVR by 0.35 and 0.50 per-
centage points on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, respectively.
5. Conclusion
We presented FAVR, a lightweight framework for integrating
pretrained video representations into frozen vision-language-
action policies. FAVR constructs a compact temporal visual
state through TTT and performs flow-aware visual residual
alignment by exploiting the affine structure of the frozen ac-
tion head. This formulation enables direct gate optimization
in velocity space, for which we develop a FISTA-based joint
solver and an approximate closed-form alternative, FAVR-
CF. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin
2.0, and real-world manipulation demonstrate consistent im-
provements with few trainable parameters, while FAVR-CF
offers a favorable trade-off between performance and infer-
ence efficiency. Future work may extend velocity-residual
alignment to more general action mappings and explore joint
adaptation of visual representations and action policies.

A.3. Causal state scheduling at inference
For each environment, the inference sequence is: (i) encode
newly observed frames when a complete ten-frame window
is available; (ii) commit the TTT updates; (iii) generate a
new action chunk using the fixed committed state and a
flow-dependent action query; and (iv) incorporate subse-
quently observed frames after execution. The π0.5 protocols
predict 50 actions and execute the first ten before replan-
ning, whereas RoboTwin executes all 50; thus a 50-action
RoboTwin interval can incorporate five consecutive observed
ten-frame windows before the next plan. Replanning and
visual-state updates operate on different clocks.

D.2. Flow-dependent gate refitting
The Shared Gate and flow-aware FAVR variants were in-
dependently retrained from the same initialization, produc-
ing distinct final checkpoints. Both use the same five-task
dataset (50 clean demonstrations per task, 250 total), a com-
mon training seed of 7, and 1,000 optimizer updates, each
sampling two trajectories with four supervised checkpoints.
Their frozen three-view V-JEPA encoder, 10-frame history
windows, and TTT state-update procedure are identical. The
evaluation pairs the initial scenes and policy noise across the
variants: demo clean, 50 rollouts per task (250 overall),
predicted/executed horizon 50/50, and ten flow evaluations.
At every flow evaluation, both variants query the current
TTT state using the current action hidden feature, so the
video readout is flow-conditioned in both. FAVR refits the
gate at each step; Shared Gate computes the gate at the first
step of a chunk and reuses those weights over the subsequent
nine steps, recomputing at the next action chunk. Shared
Gate uses one additional reference forward pass during train-
ing. Mean success increases from 72.0% with Shared Gate to
82.0% with flow-aware refitting; move can pot increases
from 66% to 86%.
D.3. Injection depth and alternative correction
heads
At the final affine head the map from gate to correction is
exactly Qg at each fixed flow state (Eq. (S4)). At earlier
action-expert layers, the same final-head-derived quadratic
is no longer the exact response after the nonlinear suffix.
We compare applying this head-derived optimization at in-
termediate layers with learning the gate at each location.
Table S5 reports the full five-task results. Learned gates
outperform head-derived optimization at every intermedi-
ate layer, whereas FAVR at the final affine head obtains the
highest mean success.
We additionally contrast three correction mechanisms.
Learned Gate directly predicts channel weights without solv-
ing the residual-fitting objective. Direct Output predicts
a bounded correction in velocity space, bypassing gated
feature injection. Hidden-Only removes the temporal video-
state branch and predicts corrections from VLA hidden fea-
tures. Their five-task means are 69.6%, 78.8%, and 79.6%,
respectively, versus 82.0% for FAVR.
D.4. State persistence versus static aggregation
Window-wise resetting reinitializes the TTT state to learned
S0 before each new video window. It retains the same pro-
jections and within-window update mechanism but does not
accumulate state across windows. Static pooling replaces
recurrent writes with aggregate visual features. On the five-
task experiment, these variants achieve 77.6% and 68.8%,
compared with 82.0% for full FAVR. This supports main-
taining the recurrent TTT state across observation windows.
D.5. Image-pretrained versus video-pretrained fea-
tures
On the four LIBERO suites, replacing V-JEPA with I-JEPA
changes the mean from 98.6% to 97.8%. Table S6 provides
the individual suite results. The largest difference occurs
on Long (+2.4 percentage points for V-JEPA); on Spatial,
I-JEPA is higher by 0.4 points.
D.6. Single-view versus three-view V-JEPA condi-
tioning
We study whether auxiliary video conditioning benefits from
views that reveal local contact states. The frozen VLA policy
itself receives three views in both configurations. Feeding
only the head-camera history to V-JEPA gives 88.48% mean
success on the full 50-task RoboTwin benchmark, while
head and both wrist-camera histories give 90.04%. Both the
head-only and three-view RoboTwin variants use a residual
auxiliary loss weight of 0.5.
The open microwave rollout illustrates one way the
extra wrist evidence can matter. The wrist view reveals
when the gripper slips from the handle, whereas a head view
alone makes the contact change difficult to distinguish. A
head-only visual correction may continue to favor nominal
progression when regrasping is needed. The three-view
configuration reaches 64% success on this task versus 45%
for head-only, a 19-point difference. Synchronized head-
and wrist-camera frames are shown in the main-paper figure.

E. Real-World Experimental Details
E.1. Platform, data, and comparison protocol
Real-world experiments use Cobot Magic, a dual-arm manip-
ulation platform based on the Mobile ALOHA teleoperation
framework [1]. We collect 35 teleoperated demonstrations
for each of four tasks. Two are standard manipulation tasks;
two require information observed before the robot begins act-
ing. The real-world visual-conditioning interface operates on
the observed camera history while the underlying bimanual
π0.5 policy is frozen during FAVR adaptation. The platform
records three RGB views: one fixed frontal external camera,
a left wrist camera, and a right wrist camera, each at an orig-
inal resolution of 640 × 480. The action executor publishes
commands at 30 Hz. The policy predicts 50 action targets
and executes the first ten before replanning. Each target is a
14-dimensional absolute-position command: six arm-joint
targets (in radians) plus one continuous gripper-opening tar-
get for each arm. Demonstrations synchronously record all
three camera streams, the actual follower-arm states, and
the leader-arm teleoperation commands; supervised action
labels are based on the leader commands, not obtained by
copying follower states.
For standard manipulation, both comparison branches
start from the same task-fine-tuned π0.5 checkpoint. One
branch continues fine-tuning the base policy; the other
freezes the base policy and V-JEPA and trains only the
FAVR modules using the same number of additional op-
timizer steps. The reported success fractions are over 20
trials for each branch and task, as summarized in Table S7.
E.2. Standard task definitions
Color-conditioned block pick-and-place. The instruction
identifies a target block by color. A successful episode re-
quires selecting that block and placing it into the designated
container. This task tests instruction-conditioned target se-
lection, reaching, grasping, and placement in a coordinated
sequence.
Left/right paper-cup grasping. The instruction speci-
fies which of the two paper cups the robot should pick up.
Compared with block pick-and-place, grasp success is sen-
sitive to the end-effector approach and contact pose. The
initial, continued-fine-tuned, and FAVR results in Table S7
follow the common 20-trial evaluation count for the respec-
tive tasks.
In the unadapted block policy, some failures involve
choosing the incorrect color despite the pretrained vision–
language backbone. The initial checkpoint records three
such errors among its ten failures.
E.3.
History-dependent
tasks
and
observa-
tion/action separation
The history-dependent experiments are motivated by earlier
uses of TTT as a recurrent memory mechanism, including
its application to robotic interaction histories [16, 33, 34].
We evaluate the history-conditioned selection enabled by the
TTT temporal visual interface.
In covered-block retrieval, one of two colored blocks is
initially covered by a paper cup. After the observation phase,
the cover is removed; the robot must identify and place
the previously covered block into a container. In history-
conditioned cup selection, the positions of two differently
colored blocks are visible before separate cups cover them.
After covering, the robot must choose the cup concealing the
block named by the instruction. Both tasks are constructed
so that the relevant identity or mapping is established during
observation, not by the final visible arrangement alone.
The trial has two phases. During the observation phase, a
human manipulates the cups while the robot receives visual
inputs and updates its TTT state but issues no movement
commands. The action phase begins only after the human
withdraws from the workspace. Observation-stage motion is
suppressed so that the robot cannot encode the target side in
its proprioception through anticipatory arm movement.
Each history-dependent task uses 35 demonstrations and
a 5,000-update FAVR adaptation. We report correct target
selection. Table S8 gives the completed counts. Both tasks
achieve 7/10 correct choices, above the 50% binary chance
level.

F. Inference Cost and Complexity
F.1. Inference latency on RoboTwin 2.0
Table S9 reports the average inference latencies for the
frozen policy, FAVR-CF, and FAVR. The two FAVR ver-
sions differ in the gate solver: FAVR employs joint FISTA
iterations, while FAVR-CF uses a diagonal approximation
followed by analytic scalar rescaling.
The 16.6 ms in-
crease of FAVR-CF over the frozen policy corresponds to
16.6/1117.7 = 1.5% (rounded); the 353.0 ms reduction
relative to FAVR corresponds to 353.0/1487.3 = 23.7%
(rounded).
During action execution, the V-JEPA branch processes
newly observed frames in ten-frame windows, with incre-
mental TTT state updates. At replanning, processing of the
remaining ten-frame window overlaps with VLA inference.
The reported latencies are measured under this pipelined
schedule.
F.2. State storage and arithmetic
Each TTT state contains 1282 FP32 scalars, or 1282 × 4 =
65,536 bytes (64 KiB). If committed and in-progress copies
are kept simultaneously, the two matrices occupy 128 KiB,
excluding key/value activations, video frames, pretrained
weights, and framework allocator overhead.
At fixed flow time, a joint gradient needs two products
with the frozen m×d action-head matrix, which costs O(md)
arithmetic. With Niter FISTA steps, the iterative gate there-
fore costs O(Nitermd) after computing its step-size infor-
mation. The closed-form gate computes s = x ⊙(W ⊤µ)
and cD = W(x ⊙gD) with cached column norms, cost-
ing O(md) arithmetic and O(d + m) extra working storage.
These are gate-subroutine costs; they do not include V-JEPA
encoding, TTT writes, backbone action inference, host syn-
chronization, or physical robot execution.
